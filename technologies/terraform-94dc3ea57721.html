    Hardening Kubernetes for GovCloud: A 3-Year Journey to FedRAMP Moderate Authorization

    Artur Beznosyuk, Hanhong Gao

    Artur Beznosyuk and Hanhong Gao describe Verkada’s path to FedRAMP Moderate authorization while running Kubernetes in AWS GovCloud. They explain what changed in node images, service-to-service encryption, vulnerability handling, and the deployment workflow.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Security, identity and policy

    Watch this if: You operate Kubernetes in a regulated environment and want an example of balancing audit requirements, patching, and developer workflows.

    1. 11:46 The speakers explain why Verkada switched from stock Amazon EKS node images to custom hardened images that enable FIPS mode.
    2. 14:33 The speakers describe moving from partial Istio use to mesh-wide encryption in GovCloud and sourcing FIPS builds of the Istio images.
    3. 17:42 The speakers use Argo CD and an Apache Airflow upgrade to illustrate how the team requested vulnerability exceptions when no patched image was available or an upgrade needed time.
    4. 22:33 The speakers describe using AWS CodeCommit as the source for Argo CD inside GovCloud while retaining GitHub for developer interaction and mirroring deployment changes between them.
    5. 25:54 The speakers describe bringing GovCloud deployments into the standard deployment system so the teams responsible for the services could review and approve the generated pull requests.

    Is Platform Engineering and the Platform Maturity Model Still Relevant in the Agentic AI Era?

    Chris Plank, Corey McGalliard

    Chris and Corey introduce the CNCF Platform Engineering Maturity Model as a map rather than a pass-or-fail assessment and describe community updates such as plainer language, translations, a self-assessment tool, and worked examples. They argue that agentic AI makes platform maturity more relevant because proofs of concept still need production ownership, guardrails, customer feedback, and redesign for machine-speed scale.

    25 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automationPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a practitioner walkthrough of applying the CNCF Platform Engineering Maturity Model to AI-era platforms.

    1. 5:15 The speakers describe the maturity model's five aspects of investment, adoption, interfaces, operations, and measurement across four levels, framing it as a map rather than a pass-or-fail assessment.
    2. 7:51 Chris uses the investment aspect to argue that many AI rollouts remain provisional projects focused on training and adoption numbers without considering day-two operation or product ownership.
    3. 11:05 The speakers say the updated model includes a community-contributed self-assessment tool whose questions can reveal concrete gaps in customer feedback and platform practice.
    4. 13:20 The speakers argue that AI proofs of concept encounter the maturity question when teams ask how they will run in production, who will own them, and how day-two maintenance will work.
    5. 20:29 The speakers argue that AI multiplies existing good and bad platform behavior, while systems designed for human-scale interaction must be redesigned for agents operating continuously and at much higher concurrency.

    Making Environments Repeatable: From Tools to Platform Capabilities

    Saiyam Pathak

    Saiyam explains the hidden setup, configuration, and readiness work behind requests for repeatable environments, and presents vCluster Stacks as a way to encode that work once. He argues that readiness should mean a validated user outcome, illustrated by GPU scheduling and a CUDA smoke test, rather than merely running components.

    5 min slot · Any Level · Keynote

    Delivery, GitOps and release safetyPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you are turning repeated environment provisioning steps into reusable platform capabilities.

    1. 0:13 Saiyam says the simple request for an environment hides installation, service trust, configuration handoff, and readiness work that platform teams must complete first.
    2. 0:53 Saiyam argues that repeatedly glue-fixing environments team by team eventually loses track of tools, versions, and ownership.
    3. 1:48 Saiyam says reusable environment automation has to encode ordered setup steps, pass one step's output into the next step, and prove readiness from the developer's perspective.
    4. 3:09 Saiyam uses a CUDA GPU stack to argue that readiness requires scheduler visibility of the GPU and a successful smoke-test job, not only healthy operator components.
    5. 4:21 Saiyam closes by asking platform teams what belongs in a reusable stack, what proves an environment ready, and which repeated requests the platform should handle for users.

    Open Policy Agent. (OPA) Intro & Deep Dive

    Sebastian Spaink, Johan Fylling

    Sebastian Spaink and Johan Fylling introduce Open Policy Agent as a general-purpose policy engine and Rego as its declarative policy language. They then cover this year's Rego changes, new Java and Swift evaluators built on OPA's intermediate representation, runtime improvements, and updates from Gatekeeper, opa-java-wasm and Conftest.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Security, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you write Rego policies and want to know about the new and, or and not semantics and the coming Rego v2.

    1. 0:53 Spaink describes the problem OPA targets: policy scattered across enforcement points owned by different teams, so nobody can easily answer what the policy is.
    2. 8:42 Fylling shows the new or keyword for inline disjunction, while still recommending helper rules to keep large logical expressions maintainable.
    3. 12:33 Fylling explains the pitfall where a not expression over an undefined input value stopped the whole rule, and how the compiler now wraps negations in closures.
    4. 17:14 Fylling says a Rego v2 import enables the new syntax per module, ahead of an OPA 2.0 release the team plans for next year.
    5. 20:06 Fylling presents Java and Swift evaluators that run OPA's intermediate representation inside applications as libraries, with bundle management and decision logging.

    Platform as a Product in a 300-Year-Old Bank

    Abby Bangser, Joel King

    Abby frames platform engineering as a shift toward self-service with guardrails and connects it to the CNCF white papers, maturity model, and an emerging marketplace model. Joel describes how NatWest used product thinking, Kubernetes CRDs, GitOps reconciliation, and clear ownership boundaries to cut EKS cluster provisioning from eight to twelve weeks to 35 minutes.

    25 min slot · Intermediate · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a regulated-enterprise case study connecting platform-as-product theory to Kubernetes and GitOps implementation plus measured outcomes.

    1. 3:43 Joel says reuse failed when each application team copied and modified patterns, producing more than 135 variants, high cognitive load, configuration drift, and an eight-to-twelve-week path to a usable EKS cluster.
    2. 8:34 Joel says NatWest adopted platform as a product by exposing curated products through an abstracted API, using Git as the source of truth, reconciliation loops, and fleet management rather than team-by-team manual change.
    3. 11:13 Joel explains that Kratix provides the product API through Kubernetes CRDs, after which pipelines write validated desired state to Git for downstream reconciliation.
    4. 19:55 Joel says the automated EKS product reduced provisioning from eight to twelve weeks to 35 minutes while including the AWS account, networking connectivity, guardrails, and self-service request path.
    5. 23:30 Abby closes by arguing that platform teams must optimize for outcomes over outputs, composing contributions into products that give users self-service access rather than merely automating infrastructure.

    Platform engineering and developer experience

    2. 9:03 Atulpriya defines delivery capital as the learning a services organization accumulates across engagements, and says it is the asset the organization truly owns.

    The Flywheel Effect: Why Community Is the Ultimate End-User Advantage

    Abby Bangser, Phill Morton

    Abby Bangser and Phill Morton tell how The Access Group built its own Kubernetes platform and Evo Builder, an AI app builder that calls trusted capability APIs instead of letting AI touch infrastructure directly. They describe how other teams now add capabilities with simple bash scripts, and how Phill fed those lessons into the upcoming CNCF platform engineering white paper.

    10 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    Community and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you run an internal platform and want other teams to add their own capabilities to it instead of filing tickets with you.

    1. 1:01 Phill Morton says coding agents brought sheer speed and scale to The Access Group, and that buying a platform meant buying someone else's opinions, so they built their own on Kubernetes, which Abby Bangser calls the platform for building platforms.
    2. 3:05 Demo of Evo Builder, their AI builder: describe a project, the platform generates and builds it, and one click deploys it, so power users get the platform without knowing what runs underneath.
    3. 4:17 Phill says letting AI roam free across infrastructure repos did not scale, so they put APIs in front of safe, trusted capabilities that Evo Builder calls; Abby names this the marketplace architecture for internal platforms.
    4. 7:11 Other teams now add capabilities themselves by writing two bash scripts, one to create and one to delete, while the platform runs them and reports back; Phill frames his role as an enabler, not a gatekeeper.
    5. 9:09 Phill says he is a co-author of the upcoming CNCF platform engineering white paper, applied each draft to his platform, and found gaps such as staged rollouts and what security and compliance teams need to see.

    We Have GitOps at Home: How Meta Went from Terraform on Cron to GitOps for AI Infra

    Phong Nguyen, Shaun Hopper

    Phong Nguyen and Shaun Hopper explain how Meta's AI infrastructure team overloaded Terraform layers to deploy Kubernetes apps, so a one-line Helm change could take hours or get stuck. They describe moving app delivery to Kargo and Argo CD with a config sync tool, a pipeline.yaml order form and a managed Argo CD service, and say deploy times dropped from hours to minutes.

    5 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    Accelerators and workload schedulingDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: your Terraform pipeline also deploys your Kubernetes apps and you want a worked path to Argo CD and Kargo.

    1. 0:04 Phong Nguyen says this is Meta's first time on the KubeCon keynote stage and that Meta's Kubernetes footprint grew fast over the past year.
    2. 0:51 Shaun Hopper says each research and inference cluster is split into about 12 Terraform layers that also deploy most of the cluster's apps, which he strongly suggests never doing, since a stuck DaemonSet or cloud SDK error can block a one-line Helm change.
    3. 2:40 Phong describes the new split: Terraform builds cloud infra, Kargo promotes apps and Argo CD deploys them, plus Ketch, which syncs Meta's internal configs into Git and merges layered Helm values.
    4. 3:31 He shows pipeline.yaml, an app order form where developers answer what to deploy, where to run it and how it rolls out, with no Kargo or Argo CD expertise needed.
    5. 3:57 He credits a managed, multi-tenant Argo CD service built inside Meta, and says early results cut deployment time from more than four hours to minutes.
