    Automated Resource Allocation Driven by Service Level Objectives

    Pedro Célestin, Julia Furst Morgado

    Pedro Célestin and Julia Furst Morgado present a case study in letting teams specify service objectives and using measured performance to test resource changes automatically. They describe using error budgets and production canaries to test proposed changes instead of letting an AI agent change allocation directly.

    25 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Observability and reliabilityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: You want to turn service objectives into allocation decisions without handing an AI agent direct control of production.

    1. 2:55 The speakers translate an example latency goal into an error budget, then explain how budget burn could inform whether to add or reclaim resources.
    2. 7:20 They propose canarying a resource change, not just a new build. They explain that if more resources do not improve the service-level indicator, the cause may instead be a bad release.
    3. 9:45 They describe the division of work in their case study: developers set the service objective, while the platform tests its own allocation proposals and can keep or reverse a change based on measured results.
    4. 11:28 They say letting AI change production allocation directly did not work well for them, so they limited it to proposing changes that a production canary could verify.
    5. 13:25 They walk through their stack: Prometheus collects metrics, Argo CD deploys changes, Argo Rollouts runs canaries, and Knative is used in some cases rather than being required.

    10 min slot · Intermediate · Lightning Talk

    From Scarcity to Self-Service: Rethinking GPU Access for Platform Teams

    Mirdul Swarup

    Mirdul describes the contradiction between scarce GPU access and idle reserved capacity, framing hoarding as an organizational design problem rather than individual misconduct. He argues for fast self-service access, fair queueing and reclamation, cost visibility, and product-like metrics that expose wait time, idle time, and cost.

    5 min slot · Any Level · Keynote

    Accelerators and workload schedulingPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: your platform team is designing self-service access, fair sharing, and cost visibility for scarce GPU capacity.

    1. 0:41 Mirdul says organizations simultaneously experience delayed AI projects because GPUs appear scarce and large amounts of reserved GPU capacity sitting idle.
    2. 1:52 Mirdul describes a scarcity and hoarding loop in which hard-to-get GPUs are retained even when idle, preventing other teams from using them.
    3. 5:07 Mirdul says fair sharing uses Kueue to prioritize GPU jobs and reclaim capacity from lower-priority or idle work so it returns to the pool.
    4. 5:38 Mirdul argues that making team-level GPU cost visible changes behavior because engineers can see when idle reservations are consuming meaningful budget.
    5. 6:31 Mirdul recommends keeping wait time, idle time, and cost visible to everyone as the operating metrics for treating GPU access as a product.

    Xing Yang, Dave Smith-Uchida

Smith-Uchida then presents two white papers in progress, on data protection best practices and on recovering from ransomware attacks.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Data, storage and stateKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you back up stateful workloads on Kubernetes or are planning how to recover from a ransomware attack.

    1. 0:53 Yang says GitOps can recreate manifests, but secrets and data in persistent volumes are not in Git, which is why the group builds Kubernetes-native data protection blocks.
    2. 5:47 Yang highlights COSI v1alpha2 support for one bucket access covering multiple buckets with read-write, write-only and read-only modes.
    3. 8:40 Yang explains changed block tracking, which lets backup vendors copy only the blocks that changed between two snapshots through a new CSI snapshot metadata service.
    4. 14:08 Smith-Uchida describes ransomware as an adversarial problem where the control plane and backups may be compromised and must be verified before restoring.
    5. 15:52 Smith-Uchida recommends immutable backups, such as S3 compliance mode, and moving backup tools from static API keys to short-lived tokens obtained via OIDC.

    “Allowed but Unsupported”: When Platform Support Boundaries Meet Enterprise Reality

    Niranjan Shankar

    Niranjan Shankar describes how the Azure Kubernetes Service team opened limited escape hatches for enterprise customers and what happened when support ownership was unclear. He uses packet loss at the boundary between a self-managed network plugin and a managed service mesh to show why teams need to name who owns the risk.

    25 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experienceSecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: You need to give advanced platform users more control without leaving production support ownership ambiguous.

    1. 4:38 Shankar introduces the 'Allowed but Unsupported' tier and uses Istio to show how a team can allow a few EnvoyFilter uses, support routine MeshConfig fields, and block higher-risk settings.
    2. 11:39 He uses packet loss with a self-managed CNI and a provider-managed service mesh to explain why the boundary between supported and unsupported parts is hard to diagnose during an incident.
    3. 13:12 He recalls customers having trouble migrating from self-managed Prometheus to the managed offering because it did not support a rule resource they used.
    4. 20:35 He recommends giving unsupported features a signpost: a way to get best-effort help, an internal owner for production risk, and a path back to a supported option.
    5. 23:08 He closes with criteria for deciding what to expose, including whether teams can isolate the security risk, find an owner, or offer a narrower subset instead of the entire API.
