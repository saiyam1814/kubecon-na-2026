    Battle-tested Autoscaling Paradigms with KEDA

    Jan Wozniak, Christian Melendez

    Jan Wozniak and Christian Melendez run a live audience quiz backed by a small CPU-hosted model and use it to show KEDA scaling the pods while Karpenter scales the nodes. They walk through their recommendations on metric freshness, fallbacks between metric sources, spare node capacity through capacity buffers, and conservative scale-down.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you autoscale workloads on Kubernetes and want practical guidance on metric sources, fallbacks, node headroom and scale-down settings with KEDA and Karpenter.

    1. 3:45 Wozniak groups scaling metrics into reactive ones such as CPU and memory, proactive ones such as load balancer and queue metrics, and predictive ones, and says reactive scaling tends to run behind the load.
    2. 10:13 He says Prometheus can keep working for smaller environments, and suggests KEDA's OpenTelemetry scaler, which has metrics pushed to it, for teams that want to remove the scrape delay or that run a very large number of scaled objects.
    3. 12:58 He describes KEDA scaling modifiers and their dynamic fallback operator, then has the OpenTelemetry source killed live to show scaling continue on the Prometheus copy of the metric.
    4. 16:56 Melendez explains Karpenter's capacity buffers, where a pod template and a buffer object act as virtual pods that keep headroom ready, and compares this with the older pattern of low-priority balloon pods.
    5. 26:41 For scale-down, Melendez advises a stabilization window longer than the time an application needs to start on a fresh node, plus a delay on Karpenter consolidation, with GPU nodes removed only when empty.

    Cortex: Surviving High Cardinality Metrics

    Friedrich Gonzalez

    Friedrich Gonzalez, a Cortex maintainer, explains how unbounded labels multiply series counts and what Cortex offers to detect, reduce and survive high cardinality, including per-tenant limits, active series trackers, remote write v2 with native histograms, resource-based query rejection and eviction, and the Parquet block format. He also reports on the independent security audit done for the project's CNCF graduation.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Observability and reliabilityData, storage and state

    Watch this if: you run Cortex or another Prometheus-compatible backend and need ways to find and contain high-cardinality metrics.

    1. 3:05 Friedrich Gonzalez separates bounded labels such as status codes and templated routes from unbounded ones such as user, request and trace IDs, and recommends putting unbounded data in logs, traces and exemplars.
    2. 8:16 He says Cortex limits protect a cluster but do not show which label is the problem, and introduces active series trackers in Cortex 1.22, which count active series matching a chosen label.
    3. 13:30 He explains that a classic histogram costs one series per bucket, 13 in his example, and recommends remote write v2 with native histograms or converted custom-bucket histograms to send each as a single series.
    4. 19:12 He describes resource-based protection in which a component checks its own CPU and heap every 100 milliseconds, rejects new work past a threshold and evicts the longest-running query, a feature he says now covers the querier.
    5. 24:58 He reports that the security audit required for CNCF graduation took place in April and found only medium and low issues, all of which he says are fixed, and asks Cortex users to upgrade.

    Exploring Telemetry Quality

    Jacob Aronoff

    Jacob Aronoff runs an interactive session on why telemetry quality matters, using audience polls and an incident from his own experience where bad telemetry overwhelmed the collectors. He then covers OpenTelemetry semantic conventions and Weaver, and introduces the policies work he and other OpenTelemetry maintainers are specifying to enforce quality rules inside the pipeline.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Birds of a Feather

    Observability and reliability

    Watch this if: you own an observability pipeline and struggle with inconsistent names, PII in logs or runaway cardinality, and want to know what OpenTelemetry is building to enforce quality rules.

    1. 3:23 Aronoff recounts an incident in which retries and stack traces overwhelmed OpenTelemetry collectors that had no persistent queuing, telemetry was dropped, and three emitters using three different names for pods made joins across the data nearly impossible.
    2. 11:25 He describes Weaver as a tool that lets an organization define its own semantic conventions with a schema, check them with custom Rego, generate types and docs, and live-check telemetry locally.
    3. 18:11 He introduces OpenTelemetry policies as matching rules plus actions that he says are meant to be portable across collectors and other pipelines, pushed dynamically without restarts, fail-open and testable locally.
    4. 24:58 He shows benchmarks in which, by his account, the Collector with OTTL and Vector with VRL stall after about one to two thousand rules, and says the policy implementation compiles rules into a Hyperscan regex database to avoid that.
    5. 26:17 On adoption, he says the OTEP has been merged, the protobufs and specification are in progress, sample libraries exist in Go, Rust and Zig, and Collector support is the nearer-term goal with SDKs hoped for next year.

    Not All Memory Is Equal: How To Properly Observe Memory Usage

    Braydon Kains, Roger Coll

    The speakers explain why host, node, pod and process memory metrics differ, tracing each to the Linux file it is read from and to how the kernel treats anonymous and file-backed pages. Using the OpenTelemetry Collector contrib binary in live demos, they show that much of its resident memory is file-backed data that can be reclaimed, and they recommend collecting system usage, container usage and working set together.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Observability and reliabilityKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: You size memory limits or alert on memory in Kubernetes and want to know what RSS, usage and working set each count.

    1. 4:40 Roger Coll says the metrics differ because they are read from different files: system memory usage comes from /proc/meminfo, while the kubelet's node usage, RSS and working set come from the cgroup memory stats.
    2. 11:17 Braydon Kains says an OOM kill happens when there are no clean file-backed pages left to reclaim, not simply when a process is over its limit.
    3. 15:34 Roger Coll says the Collector contrib binary has 12,000 Go init functions, about 17 megabytes loaded into RAM, which run once at startup.
    4. 17:41 In a live demo, forcing a reclaim removes a large share of the contrib Collector's memory and much of it never pages back in; Braydon Kains adds that working set drops only after a reclaim classifies file pages as inactive.
    5. 23:14 Roger Coll says working set is the value Kubernetes treats as memory usage and the one the kubelet uses to pick pods to evict under node memory pressure.

    Tracing at Scale: Running a 16 Billion Spans/Hour Pipeline at Nubank

    Felipe Araujo, Kaynan Coelho

    The speakers describe how Nubank built its distributed tracing pipeline, from instrumenting a handful of shared libraries to an ingestion platform built around the OpenTelemetry Collector, micro-batches on S3 and queues, KEDA scaling and tail sampling with Refinery before data goes to Honeycomb. They share the sampling rules they settled on, cases where traces helped them cut latency and storage, and advice for teams building something similar.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Observability and reliability

    Watch this if: You are scaling distributed tracing past what head sampling and a single vendor pipeline handle and want one bank's architecture and sampling choices.

    1. 1:34 Kaynan Coelho says the pipeline now receives more than 23 billion spans per hour, over 700 terabytes of data a day, and that five engineers work on it full time.
    2. 4:01 Coelho explains that because most Nubank services share the same Clojure template and libraries, they instrumented six core libraries instead of more than 4,000 services.
    3. 8:21 Felipe Araujo says streaming was too expensive and large batches too slow, so the collector writes roughly 10-second micro-batches to S3 and a queue notification tells the next application to pick them up, a pattern he identifies as claim check.
    4. 12:06 Araujo says percentage-based tail sampling hid rare critical paths, so they moved to dynamic rules that keep slow requests and all 500 errors and reduce fast, common successes.
    5. 14:23 Coelho describes using traces to find that some app requests waited on more than 30 content providers, and says the fixes brought latency in those transactions down to about 200 milliseconds.

    Two-Stage OTel Aggregation: Taming Istio Cardinality at 10k-Pod Scale

    Evelyn (Chungyu) Chen

    The speaker, from Yahoo, describes cutting the Istio metric series of a large production cluster by 86% by aggregating away pod-level identity in OpenTelemetry Collectors, first on each node and then at a gateway. She walks through the processor order this needs and the problems her team says it hit along the way, including counters that went down after pod restarts and data lost at one backend.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Observability and reliabilityNetworking and connectivity

    Watch this if: You run Istio at scale, pay per metric series, and want to see how one team aggregated pod-level labels away inside the OpenTelemetry Collector.

    1. 0:26 The speaker says one production cluster with over 800 nodes and 24,000 pods went from more than 2 million active series to an 86% smaller set, which she puts at about $140,000 a year saved on that cluster.
    2. 8:05 She explains the per-node steps: the transform processor deletes pod and container identifiers at the resource level, a batch step lets data points meet, groupbyattrs promotes the app label to the resource, and metricstransform sums the series.
    3. 12:47 She explains why the pipeline runs twice: each node agent collapses its own pods' metrics, then the gateway removes node identity and aggregates across nodes to one series per app.
    4. 15:05 She says summing cumulative counters across pods made totals drop when a pod restarted, producing rate spikes, and that the fix was converting to delta with cumulativetodelta first and rewriting queries from rate to sum_over_time.
    5. 21:16 She says a second vendor's backend stored only one point per second per series, so values pushed by three gateway pods in the same second collided and only the last was kept, a problem she says is still open.
