    AI on Kubernetes Is Just a Pod With a GPU (and That's the Problem)

    Graziano Casto

    Graziano Casto compares deploying a web application with deploying a model on Kubernetes, starting from a plain Deployment that runs a vLLM container. He names three differences, which are whole-number GPU allocation, slow startup while model weights load, and requests that cost very different amounts of GPU work, and for each he points to projects he says address it.

    12 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    AI inference and servingAccelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: You run web applications on Kubernetes and want a short orientation on what changes with model serving before choosing AI talks to watch.

    1. 0:36 The speaker shows what he calls the easiest way to deploy a model on Kubernetes: an ordinary Deployment running a vLLM container that serves a Llama 3.1 model.
    2. 3:01 He says a GPU is requested as a whole number, so a model that does not use a full GPU still leaves it overprovisioned.
    3. 3:43 He lists dynamic resource allocation in Kubernetes, HAMi for software-level enforcement and NVIDIA MIG for hardware partitioning as ways to share a GPU.
    4. 5:03 For slow startup he suggests relaxing liveness and readiness probes, caching downloaded weights on a persistent volume, and packaging weights as OCI artifacts mounted as image volumes.
    5. 6:57 He says round-robin load balancing does not fit chat workloads, and describes llm-d's KV-cache-aware routing, built on the Gateway API Inference Extension, as sending a conversation back to where its context lives.

    AI-DDoS: How AI-Generated Contributions Are Breaching OSS Communities, and What We Can Do about It

    Zixuan Feng

    Zixuan Feng presents a mixed-method study of what he calls AI-DDoS, where a flood of plausible AI-generated contributions overwhelms a community's capacity to review them. He reports repository-mining and survey results on how contribution patterns and maintainer responses changed, and argues that review and repository-management mechanisms need to change rather than only defend.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Community and open source adoptionAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you maintain an open source project and want data on how AI-generated pull requests are changing contribution patterns and maintainer responses.

    1. 3:45 Zixuan defines AI-DDoS by analogy with the security term, as a denial-of-service effect where plausible AI-generated contributions overwhelm a community's review capacity.
    2. 5:15 Zixuan describes the method: mining 294 repositories with more than 5,000 stars each, interviewing 11 maintainers, and validating the findings with over 200 surveyed maintainers.
    3. 9:01 Zixuan reports that 2025 pull request volume was significantly higher than his causal impact model predicted while issue volume was lower, and that one-time contributors' merge rate was about 18% below prediction.
    4. 12:46 Zixuan says more than 70% of surveyed maintainers chose preservative strategies such as tighter quality gates, about half chose adaptive ones, and transformative ideas such as reviewing intent or specs got low agreement.
    5. 17:15 Zixuan says a preliminary model flagged spam contributors from their history with about 85% accuracy, but he stopped the project over the risk of wrongly accusing the rest.

    Beyond Model Serving: Running Production LLMs on Kubernetes with KServe

    Johnu George, Jooho Lee

    The two KServe maintainers explain how the project added an LLMInferenceService API that builds on llm-d, vLLM and the Gateway API Inference Extension rather than a new inference engine. They then go through production patterns they say KServe addresses: cache-aware routing, streaming timeouts, split prefill and decode, multi-node serving, autoscaling signals, cold starts, model updates and LoRA adapters.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    AI inference and servingAccelerators and workload schedulingNetworking and connectivity

    Watch this if: you serve LLMs on Kubernetes and want the KServe maintainers' checklist of routing, scaling and cold-start issues to plan for.

    1. 4:07 Jooho Lee describes the LLMInferenceService API, in which he says a workload controller manages single-node, multi-node and separate prefill deployments while a route controller manages the scheduler and Gateway API resources.
    2. 8:44 Jooho follows a request from the gateway to the endpoint picker, which he says can weigh prefix cache match, queue depth and KV cache usage instead of treating every healthy replica as equal.
    3. 12:47 Johnu George warns that with streaming, timeouts at each hop must exceed generation time, because he says client retries otherwise pile more load on the GPUs.
    4. 16:15 Johnu argues that GPU utilization is a misleading autoscaling signal for LLMs and says the number of requests waiting in the inference runtime's queue is a better one.
    5. 17:30 Johnu describes cold-start work with a local model cache and container snapshot and restore, and says they are seeing startup drop from minutes to a few seconds.

    Composing Inference on Kubernetes: Gateway, Schedule, Serve, Observe

    Karena Angell, Alex Chircop

    Karena Angell and Alex Chircop argue that production inference on Kubernetes is too often built as a closed vertical stack, and present gateway, scheduling, serving and observability as separate layers joined by explicit interfaces. They go through six kinds of composability, show recorded lab runs of several traffic shapes and component swaps, and close with questions for teams to ask of their own clusters.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and servingNetworking and connectivityObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you are building a shared inference platform on Kubernetes and want a way to decide which layer owns routing, admission, serving and tracing so that parts can be swapped later.

    1. 3:15 Angell says the biggest trap they see in production is building separate silos for chat, RAG and agents, which duplicates routing, scheduling and telemetry for what she describes as one nested workload.
    2. 14:16 Chircop separates admission from routing: he says a scheduler such as Kueue decides once whether pods can exist under quota and fairness rules, while the endpoint picker chooses which replica serves each prompt, using signals such as queue depth and a warm KV cache.
    3. 17:02 Chircop says the OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions give components a shared vocabulary for spans and metrics, and W3C trace context propagation joins them into a single trace across the hops.
    4. 18:45 Angell cites Hugging Face moving Text Generation Inference into maintenance mode, and says teams that kept serving behind a gateway API contract could swap engines quickly while others had to rework their client apps.
    5. 27:13 Chircop narrates recorded lab runs in which gateways are swapped while routing live traffic, llm-d's endpoint picker is replaced by the reference picker for the Gateway API Inference Extension, and the scheduler is changed between Kueue, Volcano and KAI Scheduler.

    Dropping LLM Inference Cost by Two-Thirds with Open Weights and Kubernetes

    Omisha Gupta

    The speaker describes replacing a general-purpose LLM API with a fine-tuned, quantized Qwen model served by vLLM on Kubernetes for a multi-label classification workload. She reports her own accuracy, throughput, and cost numbers, and explains how a traffic surge test failed until she shortened GPU node startup.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and servingAccelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: you run a narrow, high-volume LLM task on a paid API and want one practitioner's account of moving it to a small fine-tuned model with autoscaled GPUs.

    1. 1:45 The speaker says newer model versions from both API providers scored lower on her task, and one broke her JSON output format 18% of the time.
    2. 6:08 The speaker reports exact-match results on her classification task: 56% with the tuned API prompt, 12% with the base 8B model, and 91% after fine-tuning it on her labeled data.
    3. 14:49 The speaker says AWQ 4-bit quantization shrank the model from 16 GB to 5.7 GB, and that the payoff was GPU memory left for the KV cache.
    4. 20:52 The speaker describes her surge test: KEDA asked for ten replicas but only one arrived in time, and she says only 18% of requests were served.
    5. 21:49 The speaker says compressing the container image and baking it into the node image brought a new GPU node to about four minutes from being useful.

    How Far Are Open-Source LLMs from Running Real Kubernetes Operations? A 10-Scenario Benchmark

    Hoon Jo

    Hoon Jo presents his own benchmark of 10 Kubernetes incident scenarios, run many times against open-weight and commercial models through the goose, OpenCode and Codex harnesses on a single MacBook with Ollama. He reports that mid-sized open-weight models handled the routine scenarios about as well as commercial ones in his runs, but in the complex scenarios some of them reported fixes that were not fixes or took actions he scores as unsafe.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automationObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you are thinking about letting a self-hosted open-weight model handle Kubernetes incidents and want to see where one benchmark found it safe and where it was not.

    1. 8:30 Jo describes his method: 10 scenarios, each run 20 times or more per model, with accuracy judged from API server audit logs and safety scored separately, including whether the agent undoes or leaves behind resources.
    2. 13:50 He shows the overall scores: commercial models come out on top, while the best open-weight models score close to the lowest commercial ones, and the gap he sees is in the complex scenarios, not the routine ones.
    3. 18:23 In the ephemeral storage scenario, where an app's growing logs keep restarting the pod, he shows agents expanding the storage and reporting the problem solved, which he counts as a temporary fix.
    4. 20:51 In the chaos scenario another team's chaos job is causing the failures; Jo says the right action is to analyze, stop and hand over, and shows a Qwen model patching the job to pause it instead.
    5. 26:24 For the unsafe-action scenario the database sits outside the cluster and cannot be reached; he says some open-weight models created a database themselves and reported the issue fixed, which the commercial models did not do in his runs.

    How Kubernetes Handles AI Generated Security Reports

    Vyom Yadav, Nathan Herz

    Nathan Herz and Vyom Yadav of the Kubernetes Security Response Committee describe how a more than tenfold rise in vulnerability reports over a year, which they attribute to AI tools, has strained a committee of ten volunteers, and walk through the intake and triage process and where it is bottlenecked. They then cover the threat model behind past Kubernetes CVEs, examples of AI-generated reports that fell outside it, and automation they want to build for duplicate detection, threat model checks and reproduction.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Security, identity and policyCommunity and open source adoptionAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you triage security reports for an open source project, or you submit AI-assisted reports and want to know what maintainers need from them.

    1. 1:00 Nathan Herz says the Kubernetes HackerOne program received 15 reports in August 2025 and 219 a year later, and that only around 1% of reports result in a CVE while about 5% need the committee's triage.
    2. 8:14 He says the committee is ten volunteers with roughly five hours a week each, that a single reporter has sent hundreds of automated reports to the mailing list, and that email intake will be forwarded to HackerOne.
    3. 12:21 He reports that duplicates closed in HackerOne went from 20 in 2025 to 700 in 2026, and sketches a duplicate filter that retrieves similar past reports from a vector database and asks an LLM for a confidence score.
    4. 19:09 Vyom Yadav gives an example of an AI-generated report about name-only matching in the kubectl credential plugin allowlist, behavior he says an official Kubernetes blog post already documented.
    5. 23:13 He suggests projects define their threat model somewhere tools can find it, mentioning the idea of a per-repository threat model Markdown file, and says writing one for every Kubernetes component is no longer realistic.

    Scaling AI Model Distribution with Dragonfly: From HuggingFace to Production GPU Clusters

    Pavan Madduri

    Pavan Madduri, a Dragonfly contributor presenting alone because co-speaker Wenbo Qi could not travel, argues that pulling model weights of hundreds of gigabytes onto every GPU node at once is an infrastructure problem and explains how Dragonfly's peer-to-peer distribution and preheating address it. He then walks through the two pieces he says he contributed, the Hugging Face and ModelScope backends and the dragonfly-injector webhook, plus a cross-cloud pattern and the project roadmap.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Data, storage and stateAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: large model downloads are slowing your GPU nodes or raising your egress bill and you want to see how Dragonfly handles it.

    1. 4:24 Pavan Madduri describes the thundering herd he sees with model weights: a scale-out sends 100 GPU nodes to the same registry at once, the origin saturates and downloads time out and retry.
    2. 10:04 He cites a test of a 130 GB model on 200 nodes where only the seed peer pulled from the origin, which he puts at about 99% less egress than 26 TB, and adds that the number depends on the setup.
    3. 13:01 He shows the Hugging Face and ModelScope backends: dfget with an hf scheme makes the seed peer fetch the model from the hub once and other nodes get the pieces from peers.
    4. 16:31 He explains the dragonfly-injector: a mutating admission webhook adds the Dragonfly client and daemon socket to pods carrying an inject annotation, so teams change Helm values instead of application code.
    5. 23:06 He lists the roadmap he took from version 2.6: a Python SDK after the Rust and Go ones, RDMA-based distribution between peers, and bandwidth negotiation across parent peers.
