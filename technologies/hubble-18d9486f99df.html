    Debugging the Invisible: Standardizing Kubernetes Networking Expertise

    Faseela Kundattil, Marino Wijay, James Spurin, Chad M. Crowell, Christophe Sauthier

    Five panelists who created or teach around the Certified Kubernetes Network Engineer (CKNE) exam discuss why CNCF added a hands-on networking certification and what foundational networking knowledge they think Kubernetes engineers need. They argue that AI tools can speed up troubleshooting but that an engineer still has to judge the suggested fix, and they describe what the exam covers and how to prepare.

    30 min slot · Beginner · Panel

    Networking and connectivityCommunity and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you are deciding whether to study for the CKNE exam, or you come from traditional networking and want to know how that knowledge carries over to Kubernetes.

    1. 4:10 Sauthier says CKNE is a separate hands-on exam, not an extension of CKA, because practice builds instincts for a 3 a.m. incident and he wants common ground between the older NetOps world and the cloud native one.
    2. 10:20 Spurin says network policy lets you ignore iptables, nftables and eBPF until something breaks, and argues that without that low-level knowledge a person can only approve whatever an AI tool concludes.
    3. 15:34 Kundattil gives the example of an AI tool suggesting to disable mTLS to restore pod-to-pod connectivity in a service mesh, which she calls not the right fix.
    4. 20:11 Sauthier says some exam questions name the project to use while others leave the choice open, for example Cilium or Istio, and Wijay adds that candidates need subnetting, routing and BGP fundamentals and must read a tcpdump.
    5. 28:36 Asked whether CKNE will count toward Kubestronaut, Sauthier says it becomes a requirement for both Kubestronaut and Golden Kubestronaut from February 15.

    From ListenerSets to eBPF Plugins: Cilium's Evolution Across the Stack

    Christine Kim, Katie Meinders, Hadrien Patte

    Katie Meinders walks through what the speakers say shipped in Cilium 1.20 and Tetragon 1.8, including datapath plugins, netkit auto mode and ztunnel-based mTLS in beta. Hadrien Patte of Datadog then explains why IPv6 for AWS ENI IPAM took four years to land, and Christine Kim covers the Gateway API additions of external auth, TCPRoute and UDPRoute, and ListenerSets.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Networking and connectivity

    Watch this if: you run Cilium and want a tour of 1.20, or want to see how one long-requested feature, IPv6 on AWS ENI, finally got built.

    1. 2:23 Katie Meinders describes datapath plugins, which she says came from Google and let third-party code instrument Cilium's datapath as a plugin without patching or forking Cilium.
    2. 3:35 She says ztunnel mTLS is now in beta, built on Istio's ztunnel, and replaces the older Cilium mutual authentication feature, which she says is deprecated.
    3. 8:38 Hadrien Patte shows the feature request for IPv6 in AWS ENI mode, opened in January 2022 the day after AWS announced IPv6 for EKS, and says it shipped in 1.20 in July 2026.
    4. 13:26 He explains the blocker: the operator listed every available IP in the CiliumNode resource, which cannot hold a /80 IPv6 prefix, so ENI mode was changed to advertise CIDRs as the multi-pool IPAM mode does.
    5. 18:20 Christine Kim explains ListenerSets, which she says lift the limit of 64 listeners per Gateway and let application teams manage their own TLS certificates, with the older listener winning conflicts.

    From Packets to Paychecks: eBPF-Based Network Cost Attribution at Scale

    Brian Ugie, Aanchal Khandelwal

    Brian Ugie and Aanchal Khandelwal describe how Adobe's platform team attributes AWS network costs to tenants on multi-tenant Kubernetes clusters by collecting per-pod byte counts with Retina, rolling them up in sharded Prometheus, and classifying each flow into billing categories. They say the pipeline runs in production but still needs hand tuning of Prometheus shards per cluster, and they call it not a victory lap.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Networking and connectivityObservability and reliabilityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you run multi-tenant clusters and need to split cloud network charges by team.

    1. 3:42 Brian says Hubble flow counts were not enough for chargeback, using an example where one namespace has 90% of the flows but moves far less data than another.
    2. 5:53 Aanchal walks through the pipeline: Retina on every node, Prometheus collection, a classification step, results in S3, and a billing team that turns bytes into cost.
    3. 10:24 Aanchal says raw flow queries timed out and shards ran out of memory until a recording rule on each shard pre-aggregated bytes with node region and zone every three minutes.
    4. 12:30 Aanchal explains the classifier picks the most specific matching CIDR for each destination IP, as the VPC does, to label traffic as internet, peering, transit gateway, VPC endpoint or local.
    5. 19:35 Brian gives fleet numbers of about 85 clusters, about 630 Prometheus shards and about 30 terabytes of requested memory, and says they tune every cluster by hand.

  • EnvoyCon

    Keeping Envoy Boring in an eBPF Datapath (Cilium L7)

    Kateryna Nezdolii

    Kateryna Nezdolii of Isovalent, an Envoy maintainer, follows one HTTP request and one policy update through Cilium to show where the eBPF datapath enforces L3/L4 checks and where a node-local Envoy enforces HTTP rules. She then describes how Cilium ships and tests its own Envoy build, keeps the last accepted configuration on rejected updates and agent restarts, and is migrating its xDS control plane.

    10 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Networking and connectivityKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: You run Cilium with L7 policies and want to know what Envoy does in that path and how updates are kept safe.

    1. 2:22 She says Cilium turns one CiliumNetworkPolicy into a datapath redirect plus an Envoy HTTP rule: the datapath checks identity, protocol and port, and Envoy checks method and path.
    2. 5:03 She explains that a request denied at L3/L4 is dropped in the datapath with no HTTP response, while one that fails the HTTP rule gets a 403 from Envoy, and that Hubble records the L7 verdict.
    3. 6:42 She says Cilium ships its own Envoy build called cilium-proxy with a minimal set of extensions, its own policy filters, a version compatibility matrix and a set of patches.
    4. 7:28 She says that when Envoy rejects a configuration update the last accepted config keeps running, and that an agent restart compares desired and accepted config so a working proxy is left alone.
    5. 8:37 She describes ongoing work: migrating from a custom split-xDS control plane to an ADS implementation, adding delta ADS, and a Network Policy Resource Discovery Service that splits the policy into smaller resources.

    Managing Network Security in Multi-Tenant Environments with Istio and Cilium

    Michael Bolot, Zaira Shaikh

    Michael Bolot and Zaira Shaikh of GEICO describe replacing a mix of cloud firewalls, on-premises firewalls and in-cluster policies with one API that configures Istio and Cilium across clusters on a flat, routable pod IP space. They explain how SPIRE identities and Istio authorization policies decide which applications may talk, while a small set of Cilium policies blocks what bypasses the sidecar, and they close with tips for writing Cilium policies safely.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Security, identity and policyNetworking and connectivity

    Watch this if: you run Cilium and Istio together and want to hear how one platform team splits network policy duties between them.

    1. 1:44 Bolot lists the problems with the legacy setup: no unified definition of the policy affecting a device, manual configuration, inconsistent enforcement and a complex network topology.
    2. 7:03 Shaikh describes the new system: pods are restricted by Istio resources and network policies written by a data plane agent on every workload cluster, configured from a central API server, with Cilium's BGP features advertising all pod IPs.
    3. 15:03 Bolot says SPIRE handles authentication by issuing workloads a certificate after attestation, and Istio authorization policies state what a given SPIFFE ID may reach, with service entries and workload entries connecting clusters.
    4. 19:55 Bolot gives three reasons they do not use Cilium for everything: they want authorization based on SPIFFE IDs, user-chosen labels would exhaust Cilium's identity map, and Cilium's layer 7 Envoy would duplicate the one Istio already runs.
    5. 25:29 Shaikh shares tips for Cilium policies, including using audit mode and Hubble, minding the default enforcement mode, and never cutting the operator or etcd off from the API server.

    The Missing Layer of Platform Maturity: Kubernetes Networking

    Faseela Kundattil, Christophe Sauthier, Chad M. Crowell, Marino Wijay, James Spurin

    The panel explains why Kubernetes networking remains a maturity gap despite higher-level abstractions, using incidents and observability workflows to show why engineers still need mental models of the underlying stack. The panelists describe the hands-on CKNE exam as a vendor-flexible way to validate networking skills while arguing that AI should assist rather than replace engineering judgment.

    35 min slot · Any Level · Panel

    Networking and connectivityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: your platform team owns Kubernetes networking troubleshooting or is evaluating the CKNE curriculum.

    1. 1:41 The panel argues that Kubernetes networking is a missing layer of platform maturity because usable abstractions still require underlying troubleshooting knowledge when failures occur.
    2. 10:08 Chad uses a 504 ingress incident to argue for peeling back the network path locally, from pod logs and events through service endpoints to ingress routing and host-header behavior.
    3. 15:21 James argues that engineers should learn enough iptables, nftables, conntrack, and eBPF to understand failures and judge AI-suggested fixes rather than treating AI as the authority.
    4. 17:35 Chad argues that knowing where in the networking stack a problem lives matters more than loyalty to a toolset, with observability used to narrow environment and namespace before deep packet inspection.
    5. 23:31 Christophe says SMEs from varied projects designed the hands-on CKNE exam so candidates can answer with different tools rather than being forced into one stack.

    Zero-Trust Telemetry: Stopping AI-Powered Poisoning Attacks on Observability Pipelines

    Chad M. Crowell, Diana Todea

    Chad M. Crowell and Diana Todea describe why telemetry should be treated as untrusted input when it feeds automated decisions. They propose independent controls for transport, query access, and network policy, then discuss how to verify those controls in Kubernetes.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Observability and reliabilitySecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: telemetry drives automated responses and you want to limit what each source and consumer can do.

    1. 13:37 The speakers describe using mutual TLS between OpenTelemetry Collectors and backends, and explain that verifying the sender does not verify that the data is true.
    2. 16:43 They propose checking the identity and scope of human and agent queries with fine-grained role-based access, then logging those decisions for audit.
    3. 20:02 They recommend default-deny network policies for the telemetry namespace, with explicit allowances for the application-to-collector and collector-to-backend flows.
    4. 22:42 They describe using Falco with Kubernetes audit events to detect suspicious activity, complementing the communication limits from network policy.
    5. 23:54 They recommend validating which sources feed Prometheus federation and restricting who can reach federation endpoints and exporters.
