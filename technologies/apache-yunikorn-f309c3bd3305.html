    KubeRay 101

    Kai-Hsun Chen, Richard Liaw, Mengjin Yan, Jui-An Huang

    The KubeRay maintainers give a short introduction to Ray and to KubeRay, the Kubernetes operator that runs Ray clusters, and say the session falls on the first day KubeRay is announced as donated to the CNCF. Kai-Hsun Chen then describes how his startup runs its audio model work on Ray, and the second half is audience questions on cluster sharing, reinforcement learning, MultiKueue and Grove.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Birds of a Feather

    Accelerators and workload schedulingCommunity and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you run Kubernetes and are new to Ray, and want to know what KubeRay's custom resources do and how one team pairs it with Kueue for training and serving.

    1. 5:42 Huang says Ray distributes actors across the resources it has but does not allocate those resources itself, and that on Kubernetes this is the job of KubeRay, an operator that manages the lifecycle of a Ray cluster.
    2. 6:12 He walks through the custom resources: RayCluster for a long-lived cluster suited to prototyping, RayJob and a cron variant that create a short-lived cluster and clean it up afterwards, and RayService for online serving.
    3. 9:32 Chen describes his company's audio model pipeline: Ray Data with CPU and GPU steps for data preparation, a Ray cluster with Kueue and MultiKueue for training and evaluation, and RayService for serving.
    4. 13:00 Huang lists recent contributions he attributes to other companies, including a history server for logs and events, a RocksDB backend for Ray's metadata store, Kubernetes RBAC for Ray token authentication, and integration with the Kubernetes workload API.
    5. 19:55 Asked about MultiKueue, Chen says he uses it with autoscaling and describes issues he ran into, such as one cluster holding hundreds of workload objects and a periodic reconnect that requeued everything, which he says were fixed upstream within days.

    Many Clusters, One GPU Pool: Locality-Aware Scheduling and Data Delivery With Kueue and Dragonfly

    Yiqing Wang, Adam Buran

    Yiqing Wang and Adam Buran describe how Roblox shares GPU capacity across clusters using Kueue while leaving each cluster to check its own capacity. They explain how a locality-aware dispatcher chooses clusters and how Dragonfly caches training data and checkpoints near the jobs that use them.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingData, storage and state

    Watch this if: You operate GPU clusters in more than one location and want to share capacity without repeatedly pulling the same datasets across regions.

    1. 6:55 The speakers describe two levels of MultiKueue admission: the hub checks team quota, then the candidate clusters check node capacity and the first to admit the job runs it with KubeRay.
    2. 8:15 The speakers explain how the dispatcher first filters for requirements such as GPU type and full-job fit, then prefers the data region for a limited time before considering other regions.
    3. 9:19 The speakers explain how Roblox separates pool-wide guaranteed quota from cluster-level borrowing and uses preemption to reclaim borrowed GPUs when a guaranteed job arrives.
    4. 14:51 The speakers describe Dragonfly looking for file pieces in the local cache, other peers, and seed peers before falling back to Amazon S3.
    5. 15:55 The speakers report that half of the data served came from node-local cache and another 43 percent from peers in the same cluster, leaving 7 percent served from Amazon S3.

    One Workload to Rule Them All: Workload Aware Scheduling for Distributed AI Training on Kubernetes

    Andrey Velichkevich

    Andrey Velichkevich explains why scheduling distributed training one pod at a time can deadlock jobs and how the Kubernetes Workload and PodGroup APIs model them as a unit. He shows how Kubeflow Trainer exposes those capabilities through training runtimes and compares three scheduling policies on a local cluster.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: You run distributed training on Kubernetes and want to understand when pod groups and topology constraints matter.

    1. 0:48 Velichkevich illustrates how two training jobs can deadlock when the scheduler gives each job some GPUs but neither gets enough to start all its ranks.
    2. 4:44 Velichkevich distinguishes the Workload template from runtime PodGroups and shows how a PodGroup can express a minimum pod count and topology constraints.
    3. 7:10 Velichkevich introduces composite PodGroups for workloads with a hierarchy, where a parent can require a minimum number of child groups before scheduling proceeds.
    4. 10:12 Velichkevich explains the Kubeflow Trainer split: platform admins define runtime blueprints, and scientists use a Python SDK to submit jobs without managing Kubernetes deployment details.
    5. 15:17 In a local kind demo, Velichkevich sets up a comparison of default pod-by-pod placement, gang scheduling, and rack-constrained placement for a PyTorch job.

    Spark on Kubernetes: The Gap Between “It Works” and “It Works Well”

    Damon Cortesi

    Damon Cortesi says Airbnb finished moving its Spark fleet from Hadoop to Kubernetes two weeks before the talk and goes layer by layer through what failed on the way: the control plane, scheduling, nodes, disks, networking, Spark itself and observability. He says most of the 34 changes were small config edits or backported patches that were hard to find, and sums up the lesson as check your config, stress test, repeat.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Data, storage and stateKubernetes internals and runtimesObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you run or plan to run Apache Spark on Kubernetes at scale and want a list of concrete failure points and the fixes one team applied.

    1. 1:23 Cortesi says Airbnb hit 100% two weeks earlier, shutting down its last legacy Hadoop cluster, with a fleet of about 588,000 daily Spark jobs.
    2. 4:55 On the control plane, he says dropping the events TTL from one hour to 10 minutes cut etcd P99 latency from half a second to under 25 milliseconds and the object count from 2 million to 400,000.
    3. 9:06 He describes two Apache YuniKorn problems, one asking for far more capacity than needed because of a Go bug and one asking for too little under FIFO sorting, and says KWOK let him test YuniKorn at scale without provisioning real capacity.
    4. 12:35 He says a Node Problem Detector log rotation bug marked healthy nodes as dead, so that about 74 nodes were removed in 24 hours, one of four node-health bugs they found.
    5. 16:28 He says unique pod labels pushed Cilium past its 65,000 identity limit at their pod creation rates, and the fix was basing identities on a short allow list of labels.

    Volcano for the Agentic AI Era: Unified Scheduling Across Training, Inference, and Agents

    Ricardo Aravena, Rainie Li

    Rainie Li explains how Volcano aims to schedule training, inference and agent workloads on one Kubernetes cluster, using a batch scheduler and an agent scheduler that share nodes through a sharding controller, plus multi-level gang scheduling, network topology hypernodes and gang-aware preemption. Ricardo Aravena then covers how workload controllers such as Kthena, KubeRay and Grove map onto Volcano primitives, the project's benchmarking work and the roadmap.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingAI agents and automationAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: you schedule AI training, inference or agent workloads on shared GPU clusters and want to know what Volcano offers for gang and topology-aware placement.

    1. 3:02 Rainie Li says Volcano uses two schedulers, a batch scheduler for coordinated workloads and an agent scheduler for latency-sensitive pods, with a sharding controller assigning candidate nodes to each and moving them as demand changes.
    2. 6:49 She introduces multi-level gang scheduling, in which a pod group contains subgroups with their own completeness requirements, such as a tensor-parallel group or a prefill or decode replica.
    3. 9:56 She explains gang-aware preemption, which treats victims as groups and simulates placement first, so no pod is evicted if the whole incoming gang cannot fit.
    4. 15:12 Ricardo Aravena says KubeRay support is merged upstream and listed for Volcano 1.16, and walks through how its head and worker groups translate into Volcano subgroups.
    5. 18:35 He describes the scheduling benchmark framework shipped with Volcano 1.15, which reports scheduling latency and binding throughput on kind, KWOK or an existing cluster, and notes it says nothing about GPU job speed.
