    Cloud Native Infrastructure in the AI Era: A TAG Infrastructure Panel

    Xing Yang, Dylan Page, Mesut Oezdil, Alexa Griffith, Kashif Khan, Thomas Schuetz

    The co-chairs and tech leads of the CNCF TAG Infrastructure describe the group's current work, including white papers on infrastructure lifecycle, AI for science, AI inference and data storage for AI, and a new telco AI-native initiative. They then each name what they see as the biggest challenge AI workloads pose for cloud native infrastructure and take audience questions on agent sandbox projects and moving HPC sites toward cloud native tools.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Panel

    Community and open source adoptionAccelerators and workload schedulingAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: you run or plan AI infrastructure and want to know what the CNCF TAG Infrastructure is writing about it and where you can contribute.

    1. 2:04 Dylan Page describes the infrastructure lifecycle white paper, which he says is meant to advise on managing mixed public cloud, private cloud, edge and bare metal environments, and Thomas Schuetz adds that it covers when operator-based deployment fits better than Terraform or OpenTofu.
    2. 5:13 Kashif Khan introduces the telco AI-native initiative, saying the challenge for AI-native network functions is the infrastructure beneath the model, and that the group is collecting shared definitions and gaps from operators and vendors.
    3. 10:03 Xing Yang summarizes the TAG's white paper on data storage for cloud native AI, saying training, inference and agents each have different storage patterns, so no single AI storage fits all.
    4. 11:40 Asked for the biggest challenge, Dylan Page answers that it is people, describing a collision of cloud native, telecom and HPC cultures, while other panelists name GPU utilization, placement and the speed at which infrastructure is needed.
    5. 19:00 Mesut Oezdil argues that GPUs must be sliced to be economical and that MIG and time slicing fall short in mixed-vendor environments, and points to HAMi, which he maintains, as a vendor-agnostic option.

    Kyverno: The Supercharged Future of Cloud Native Policy as Code

    Jim Bugwadia

    Jim Bugwadia presents Kyverno as a framework for governance in Kubernetes, with policies written as declarative YAML and enforced in pipelines, at admission control and at runtime. He describes its policy types, exception management and reporting, and says it now also applies to other JSON and YAML such as Terraform, OpenTofu and Dockerfiles.

    5 min slot · Level not listed · Lightning Talk

    Security, identity and policyDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: you want a five-minute overview of what Kyverno covers beyond Kubernetes admission control.

    1. 0:48 Jim Bugwadia describes Kyverno as a framework that enables governance in Kubernetes by making policies as simple to write as any Kubernetes manifest.
    2. 1:40 He says Kubernetes native policies only apply at admission control, while Kyverno also covers existing resources and adds image verification, complex checks, exception management and reporting.
    3. 2:08 He lists the policy types: validate, mutate, generate, and delete or clean up resources, plus image verification for signing and attestations.
    4. 2:29 He says later releases apply Kyverno policies to any JSON or YAML, such as Terraform, OpenTofu, Dockerfiles and cloud configurations, and mentions AI use cases like intercepting MCP and LLM calls with Envoy.
    5. 3:41 He says every enforcement point needs to produce reports for compliance, and that Kyverno supports OpenReports, a format he says other tools such as Trivy also produce.

    Platform Engineering after the AI Hype: Why IDPs Must Become Intent-Driven

    Daniel Bryant

    The speaker argues that AI agents calling internal platforms at machine speed expose three failure modes: too much API surface, confidently wrong requests, and nobody owning day two. He recommends intent-driven platform capabilities with guardrails, helpful refusal messages, and lifecycle ownership by the teams that produce them.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experienceAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: You build an internal developer platform and want to know what to change before AI agents become its main consumers.

    1. 3:39 The speaker describes an experiment in which several LLMs with kubectl access always produced a working Postgres, but he says each model and each run produced something subtly different.
    2. 10:40 The speaker names three failure modes he says he sees in the industry: too much surface, confidently wrong, and nobody owns day two.
    3. 15:42 The speaker contrasts resource-driven Terraform or OpenTofu with an intent-driven API where the consumer asks for a PCI DSS compliant database and the platform holds the guardrails.
    4. 18:53 The speaker borrows the idea of lane assist from Gregor Hohpe and says platform APIs should tell agents why a request is refused and what the options are, otherwise agents often work around the guardrail.
    5. 23:54 The speaker gives an example of a CVE fix rolled out once by the capability producer across a fleet, using Kratix promises, Crossplane composition revisions or KubeVela versioned definitions.

    Securing Rural Colorado with Open Source: Running a Student-Led SOC on Kubernetes

    Maciej Wal

    Maciej Wal describes how the Cybersecurity Center at Metropolitan State University of Denver replaced a vendor's security monitoring service, first with four virtual machines and then with a Kubernetes platform that he says he runs as the one full-time staff member. He covers the outages and adoption problems along the way, and how students learn log analysis and GitOps on the platform that monitors public sector partners.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Security, identity and policyObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you are a very small team weighing Kubernetes for a security monitoring service, or want ideas for teaching students with real infrastructure.

    1. 3:19 The speaker says that after the vendor ended the service he built a replacement in about 66 days on four virtual machines, with WireGuard, single-node OpenSearch and a new ticketing tool.
    2. 6:45 The speaker explains picking the Kubernetes stack from the CNCF landscape, including Keycloak, Argo CD, OpenTofu and Fluent Bit, and says it cost around $1,000 a month.
    3. 8:33 The speaker recounts a flood of Suricata logs taking down the OpenSearch cluster, which he says had too little memory and no autoscaling, and learning of breakage days late.
    4. 12:01 The speaker says one of the hardest problems he found was the gap between offering self-service and people using it, after students did not adopt browser certificate access.
    5. 13:48 The speaker describes a student workshop on Argo CD, Envoy Gateway, cert-manager and GitHub Actions that ended with deploying a Minecraft server through GitOps.

    So You Want to Build a Platform? You Probably Already Have One

    Juho Syrjänen

    Juho Syrjänen argues that teams with infrastructure-as-code modules, Helm charts and CI/CD pipelines already have a platform, and that the next step is to wrap that code behind a platform API instead of rebuilding it. He describes how module version drift and agent-generated pull requests strain the module-per-repository model, and shows an approach using Kratix where a short custom resource triggers the existing code.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you maintain Terraform or OpenTofu modules and Helm charts and want a way to turn them into a self-service platform without a rewrite.

    1. 3:11 The speaker describes the tempting plan of rebuilding everything with Crossplane, kro, Backstage and Kratix over a couple of years, and the pushback he says that proposal gets.
    2. 5:29 The speaker says the engineering behind existing infrastructure as code is solid and asks why it should not be reused.
    3. 11:54 The speaker proposes packaging the module and its wiring into one platform capability, which he describes as a single deterministic unit, with user configuration cut to about a dozen lines of YAML.
    4. 17:56 The speaker walks through delivery: the platform writes rendered output to a Git repository, Flux syncs it into destination clusters, and in-cluster operators apply it to the cloud provider.
    5. 19:19 The speaker says users and agents can then act on the platform without extra pull request approvals because a human already approved the platform code, then names Kratix and Crossplane as tools that help.

    The Flywheel Effect: Why Community Is the Ultimate End-User Advantage

    Abby Bangser, Phill Morton

    Abby Bangser and Phill Morton tell how The Access Group built its own Kubernetes platform and Evo Builder, an AI app builder that calls trusted capability APIs instead of letting AI touch infrastructure directly. They describe how other teams now add capabilities with simple bash scripts, and how Phill fed those lessons into the upcoming CNCF platform engineering white paper.

    10 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    Community and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you run an internal platform and want other teams to add their own capabilities to it instead of filing tickets with you.

    1. 1:01 Phill Morton says coding agents brought sheer speed and scale to The Access Group, and that buying a platform meant buying someone else's opinions, so they built their own on Kubernetes, which Abby Bangser calls the platform for building platforms.
    2. 3:05 Demo of Evo Builder, their AI builder: describe a project, the platform generates and builds it, and one click deploys it, so power users get the platform without knowing what runs underneath.
    3. 4:17 Phill says letting AI roam free across infrastructure repos did not scale, so they put APIs in front of safe, trusted capabilities that Evo Builder calls; Abby names this the marketplace architecture for internal platforms.
    4. 7:11 Other teams now add capabilities themselves by writing two bash scripts, one to create and one to delete, while the platform runs them and reports back; Phill frames his role as an enabler, not a gatekeeper.
    5. 9:09 Phill says he is a co-author of the upcoming CNCF platform engineering white paper, applied each draft to his platform, and found gaps such as staged rollouts and what security and compliance teams need to see.

    The Next Generation of Kyverno — Unified Policy as Code at Scale

    Jim Bugwadia, Justin Munoz

    Jim Bugwadia explains why Kyverno moved from its own policy language to CEL-based policy types, how to migrate before the legacy types are removed in 1.20, and what the project plans next. Justin Munoz describes how his platform team at Kinexys by J.P. Morgan adopted Kyverno for namespace baselines, certificate rotation, permission tightening and change-window checks on Argo CD syncs, and shares the lessons the team took from it.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Security, identity and policyAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you run Kyverno and need to plan the move to CEL-based policies, or want to see how a regulated platform team uses it beyond admission control.

    1. 3:36 Jim Bugwadia says Kyverno chose CEL because it is the native policy language in Kubernetes, compiled and fast, and that Kyverno adds reporting, exceptions and policies for existing resources on top of the built-in admission policy types.
    2. 9:52 Justin Munoz describes using Kyverno to watch for newly issued certificates and trigger rolling restarts of deployments that cannot reload them on their own.
    3. 11:57 He describes a policy that intercepts Argo CD sync events and allows them only when a custom resource shows an approved change management window is open, with a precise denial reason otherwise.
    4. 13:34 He shares lessons learned: start in audit mode, treat the denial message as the user interface, make the paved path compliant by default and ship policy through the normal software lifecycle.
    5. 16:09 Bugwadia says release 1.20 removes the older policy types but leaves their CRDs in place so old policies show as not ready, and that 1.21 will remove the CRDs as well.
