    Anthropic’s Kubernetes Scheduler for Frontier AI: What Worked, What Broke

    Decheng Dai

    Decheng Dai describes why Anthropic wrote its own Kubernetes scheduler for accelerator workloads, covering gang scheduling, the topologies of Google TPUs and AWS Trainium, and a tree model of data center networks. He walks through the design choices he says keep the scheduling loop fast on very large clusters, then discusses soft slicing, defragmentation and the lack of transactional binding in the API server.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingKubernetes internals and runtimesNetworking and connectivity

    Watch this if: you schedule large training or inference jobs on Kubernetes and want to hear how one team handles gang and topology constraints at very large cluster sizes.

    1. 1:27 Dai frames the problem as gang scheduling plus topology-aware scheduling and describes two gang modes: a full gang where every pod must run, and replicas that are each a gang but fail independently.
    2. 6:47 He says the team models every cluster as a tree of network domains whose leaves are TPU slices, Trainium servers or GPU nodes, and built a drop-in replacement scheduler that jobs select by scheduler name.
    3. 8:15 He describes the control loop: take a snapshot of the cluster, plan all pending jobs from highest to lowest priority, and schedule jobs rather than pods, with the loop kept within 10 seconds on their largest cluster.
    4. 16:48 He says scoring nodes only once for jobs that are identical from a scheduling perspective is by far the largest scaling factor in their system.
    5. 23:55 As something that does not work well, Dai points to gang binding without transactions in the API server: some of the bind calls can fail, forcing eviction of the pods already bound.

    Optimize Your Heterogeneous AI Cluster

    Mengxuan Li

    HAMi maintainer Mengxuan Li of Dynamia.ai explains the two problems the project targets, GPUs that can only be allocated whole and multi-GPU placements that ignore how cards are connected, and how HAMi's device plugin and HAMi-core enforce per-container memory limits to share a GPU. He then covers newer features (dynamic MIG, remote GPU access from nodes without GPUs, AMD support), a HAMi variant built on Dynamic Resource Allocation that works with other schedulers, and the roadmap.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: you operate GPU clusters on Kubernetes and want to share GPUs among workloads or see how HAMi fits with Dynamic Resource Allocation.

    1. 3:05 Li describes HAMi's two basic functions: GPU slicing, so a workload can be given part of a GPU, and scheduling that accounts for how GPUs on a node are connected when several are requested.
    2. 8:48 He explains dynamic MIG: the user states only the device memory needed, and HAMi finds a matching MIG template and a GPU with enough consecutive free slots, creates the instance, and recycles it when the workload ends.
    3. 11:22 He introduces remote GPU in version 2.11, where a pod on a CPU-only node reaches a GPU on another node over TCP and sees it when running nvidia-smi.
    4. 15:31 He says HAMi DRA, a webhook plus driver on top of Dynamic Resource Allocation, converts device-plugin style resource requests into resource claims automatically and adds allocation metrics, at the cost of the topology-aware scheduling of the original HAMi.
    5. 21:16 Roadmap: scheduling by rack and super pod network topology, kernel-level isolation through cgroups and MPS alongside HAMi-core, and multi-client remote GPU with per-client limits.
