    Anatomy of an Outage: When Bursty ML Workloads Break the Kubernetes Scheduler

    Cellan Hall, Jenny Vega, Karol Szuster

    Two engineers from Isomorphic Labs and a Kueue maintainer from Google recount a 10-day outage in which autoscaled inference deployments flooded a cluster with pending Kueue workloads. They walk through its three phases and causes, the upstream fixes in Kueue and Kubernetes, and the cluster, alerting and autoscaling changes they say they made afterward.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingObservability and reliabilityKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: You run autoscaled batch or inference workloads through Kueue and want to see how pending objects can take down scheduling.

    1. 6:35 Jenny Vega says a flood of pending pods and workloads in February 2026 pushed a production cluster into a 10-day outage in three phases, ending with 20 hours of no scheduling.
    2. 8:14 Karol Szuster says the Kueue controller restarts came from one shared client-side rate-limit bucket that also covered leader election, so a storm of reconciles stopped the lease from being renewed.
    3. 9:52 Jenny Vega shows how unschedulable GPU replicas at the head of a shared queue kept admissible CPU work from being reached, and says the team split the queue while Kueue added scheduling equivalence hashing upstream.
    4. 14:38 The speakers explain that after a restart the API server had to call Kueue's conversion webhook for every workload to rebuild its watch cache, which could not finish within the five-minute compaction interval.
    5. 21:06 Cellan Hall says the team now feeds the admitted pod count back into the KEDA scaling trigger, and reports 80 percent fewer pending Kueue workloads and 40 percent fewer workload objects in etcd.

    Pedro Célestin, Julia Furst Morgado

    Pedro Célestin and Julia Furst Morgado present a case study in letting teams specify service objectives and using measured performance to test resource changes automatically. They describe using error budgets and production canaries to test proposed changes instead of letting an AI agent change allocation directly.

    25 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Observability and reliabilityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: You want to turn service objectives into allocation decisions without handing an AI agent direct control of production.

    1. 2:55 The speakers translate an example latency goal into an error budget, then explain how budget burn could inform whether to add or reclaim resources.
    2. 7:20 They propose canarying a resource change, not just a new build. They explain that if more resources do not improve the service-level indicator, the cause may instead be a bad release.

    4. 11:28 They say letting AI change production allocation directly did not work well for them, so they limited it to proposing changes that a production canary could verify.
    5. 13:25 They walk through their stack: Prometheus collects metrics, Argo CD deploys changes, Argo Rollouts runs canaries, and Knative is used in some cases rather than being required.

    Autoscaling at Scale with KEDA

    Ali Aqel

    Ali Aqel, a Stripe engineer and KEDA contributor, describes how Stripe's Kubernetes team runs workload autoscaling with KEDA across its clusters, organized around four principles: safe, stable, scalable and simple. He covers rollout protection, break-glass locks, canaries for every scaler, tuning and per-cluster Prometheus workspaces, and walks through two hypothetical incidents.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingObservability and reliabilityDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: you run KEDA or HPA on large clusters and want one company's guardrails, locks and canaries.

    1. 3:09 Ali gives Stripe's numbers: more than 90 self-managed clusters, about 84,000 deployments and 180,000 application pods for over 8,600 services, run by about a dozen Kubernetes engineers.
    2. 10:03 He says Stripe built a pod deletion cost controller that annotates pods so scale-downs keep the spread across availability zones.

    5. 16:45 He says scaling on Prometheus broke Amazon Managed Prometheus rate limits, which led through caching and sync period changes to one autoscaling workspace per cluster.

    Battle-tested Autoscaling Paradigms with KEDA

    Jan Wozniak, Christian Melendez

    Jan Wozniak and Christian Melendez run a live audience quiz backed by a small CPU-hosted model and use it to show KEDA scaling the pods while Karpenter scales the nodes. They walk through their recommendations on metric freshness, fallbacks between metric sources, spare node capacity through capacity buffers, and conservative scale-down.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you autoscale workloads on Kubernetes and want practical guidance on metric sources, fallbacks, node headroom and scale-down settings with KEDA and Karpenter.

    1. 3:45 Wozniak groups scaling metrics into reactive ones such as CPU and memory, proactive ones such as load balancer and queue metrics, and predictive ones, and says reactive scaling tends to run behind the load.
    2. 10:13 He says Prometheus can keep working for smaller environments, and suggests KEDA's OpenTelemetry scaler, which has metrics pushed to it, for teams that want to remove the scrape delay or that run a very large number of scaled objects.
    3. 12:58 He describes KEDA scaling modifiers and their dynamic fallback operator, then has the OpenTelemetry source killed live to show scaling continue on the Prometheus copy of the metric.
    4. 16:56 Melendez explains Karpenter's capacity buffers, where a pod template and a buffer object act as virtual pods that keep headroom ready, and compares this with the older pattern of low-priority balloon pods.
    5. 26:41 For scale-down, Melendez advises a stabilization window longer than the time an application needs to start on a fresh node, plus a delay on Karpenter consolidation, with GPU nodes removed only when empty.

    Composing Inference on Kubernetes: Gateway, Schedule, Serve, Observe

    Karena Angell, Alex Chircop

    Karena Angell and Alex Chircop argue that production inference on Kubernetes is too often built as a closed vertical stack, and present gateway, scheduling, serving and observability as separate layers joined by explicit interfaces. They go through six kinds of composability, show recorded lab runs of several traffic shapes and component swaps, and close with questions for teams to ask of their own clusters.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and servingNetworking and connectivityObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you are building a shared inference platform on Kubernetes and want a way to decide which layer owns routing, admission, serving and tracing so that parts can be swapped later.

    1. 3:15 Angell says the biggest trap they see in production is building separate silos for chat, RAG and agents, which duplicates routing, scheduling and telemetry for what she describes as one nested workload.
    2. 14:16 Chircop separates admission from routing: he says a scheduler such as Kueue decides once whether pods can exist under quota and fairness rules, while the endpoint picker chooses which replica serves each prompt, using signals such as queue depth and a warm KV cache.
    3. 17:02 Chircop says the OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions give components a shared vocabulary for spans and metrics, and W3C trace context propagation joins them into a single trace across the hops.
    4. 18:45 Angell cites Hugging Face moving Text Generation Inference into maintenance mode, and says teams that kept serving behind a gateway API contract could swap engines quickly while others had to rework their client apps.
    5. 27:13 Chircop narrates recorded lab runs in which gateways are swapped while routing live traffic, llm-d's endpoint picker is replaced by the reference picker for the Gateway API Inference Extension, and the scheduler is changed between Kueue, Volcano and KAI Scheduler.

    Fine-Tuning a Vision Language Model Across Hundreds of GPUs on Kubernetes

    Phelipe Fabres

    Phelipe Fabres, an AWS solutions architect presenting without the customer co-speaker he says could not attend, tells how a Brazilian legal-tech startup fine-tuned a vision language model for messy legal documents on 32 nodes with 256 GPUs on Amazon EKS. He organizes the story as four patterns he says they learned, starting every worker together, moving data fast between nodes, keeping every GPU fed and recovering from failures, with two escalations as examples.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: you plan multi-node GPU training on Kubernetes and want to hear what stalled one team's jobs and the patterns they adopted in response.

    1. 2:04 Fabres opens with an escalation where instance checks on all 32 nodes passed, yet one overheating GPU stalled the whole distributed training job.
    2. 14:56 He describes gang admission, a pre-job that verifies every node is healthy before the job is submitted, because he says each hour of waiting costs GPU time across all 32 nodes.
    3. 17:46 He recounts a networking escalation in which performance dropped at batch transfers of 128 to 256 MB, traced to the NCCL, libfabric and Elastic Fabric Adapter layers inside the pod, and says tuning restored the same throughput at every size.
    4. 23:03 He explains the two data layers, object storage in Amazon S3 for prepared data and FSx for Lustre for checkpoints, copying only what each job needs to limit cost.
    5. 26:45 He outlines the recovery pattern: watch device health trends, block the node from new placements, wait for a compatible replacement and restart, and says the recovery job later grew into an agent.

They walk through the past year's changes, including structured z-pages, Resource State Metrics and native histograms, then list next year's plans and the subprojects where they say they need contributors.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Observability and reliabilityKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run Kubernetes monitoring and want the maintainers' view of what changed in metrics, logs and traces and where the SIG wants help.

    1. 4:10 Catherine Fang presents the statusz and flagz z-pages, which she says expose a component's binary version and effective flags on its serving port and are gaining structured output formats this year.
    2. 8:02 Catherine introduces Resource State Metrics, a new component that she says uses CEL-style expressions to read any field of a custom resource without writing Go code in kube-state-metrics.
    3. 9:00 Stephen Lang says kubernetes-mixin is new to SIG Instrumentation and describes work to cut alert fatigue and to tie mixin releases to Kubernetes versions with a scrape allow list and a version support matrix.

    Tracing at Scale: Running a 16 Billion Spans/Hour Pipeline at Nubank

    Felipe Araujo, Kaynan Coelho

    The speakers describe how Nubank built its distributed tracing pipeline, from instrumenting a handful of shared libraries to an ingestion platform built around the OpenTelemetry Collector, micro-batches on S3 and queues, KEDA scaling and tail sampling with Refinery before data goes to Honeycomb. They share the sampling rules they settled on, cases where traces helped them cut latency and storage, and advice for teams building something similar.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Observability and reliability

    Watch this if: You are scaling distributed tracing past what head sampling and a single vendor pipeline handle and want one bank's architecture and sampling choices.

    1. 1:34 Kaynan Coelho says the pipeline now receives more than 23 billion spans per hour, over 700 terabytes of data a day, and that five engineers work on it full time.
    2. 4:01 Coelho explains that because most Nubank services share the same Clojure template and libraries, they instrumented six core libraries instead of more than 4,000 services.
    3. 8:21 Felipe Araujo says streaming was too expensive and large batches too slow, so the collector writes roughly 10-second micro-batches to S3 and a queue notification tells the next application to pick them up, a pattern he identifies as claim check.
    4. 12:06 Araujo says percentage-based tail sampling hid rare critical paths, so they moved to dynamic rules that keep slow requests and all 500 errors and reduce fast, common successes.
    5. 14:23 Coelho describes using traces to find that some app requests waited on more than 30 content providers, and says the fixes brought latency in those transactions down to about 200 milliseconds.

    Untrusted Code on Secure Runtimes: Agent Sandboxes on Kubernetes

    Mofi Rahman

    Mofi Rahman leads a hands-on lab on GKE, with four Google colleagues helping attendees in the room, and explains that it uses the newly open-sourced Agent Substrate where the abstract promised Agent Sandbox. Attendees create workers, actor templates and actors, suspend and resume them with their state, allow a model call through an egress policy and run 20 actors on two workers, and he says internal benchmarks reached more than 100 million actors.

    80 min slot · Beginner · Tutorial

    AI agents and automationSecurity, identity and policyKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you want to follow a full lab on suspending and resuming agent workloads on Kubernetes and see how actors differ from one pod per sandbox.

    1. 21:39 Rahman explains why the lab switched projects: Agent Sandbox maps a sandbox to a pod with warm pools, while Agent Substrate adds actors, many per pod, and he suggests Sandbox for agents that run for days and Substrate for short, bursty sessions.
    2. 25:22 He lists Substrate's parts: an API server, Postgres in place of etcd, which he says could not scale to the 100 million actor goal, egress control, and a router that proxies requests so actors do not each need an IP address.
    3. 36:15 He suspends an actor, sends it a request and gets an answer anyway, explaining that the router holds the request while the actor resumes from its snapshot mid-state, unlike Cloud Run, Lambda or Knative starting from zero.
    4. 49:14 A model call from the actor fails because egress is denied by default, and it succeeds after he creates an egress gateway and a policy that allows the one model URL.
    5. 57:09 A third actor times out on two full workers, and he stresses that Substrate does not decide when to suspend: the platform team defines idle, here three seconds without a request, after which 20 actors share two workers.

    Your Peers Are Struggling With LLM Inference on Kubernetes Too. Here's What the Data Says.

    Danielle Cook, Andy Suderman

    Danielle Cook of Akamai and Andy Suderman of Fairwinds walk through an Akamai-commissioned survey of 200 practitioners running AI in production, along with CNCF, Forrester and Cast AI figures, to show where teams say inference on Kubernetes is hard. They group the problems into scheduling, scaling, routing, placement and ownership, and close with five steps they recommend, from starting on an AI-conformant cluster to setting a time-to-first-token SLO.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and servingObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you run LLM inference on Kubernetes and want survey numbers to compare your latency targets, GPU utilization and routing setup against.

    1. 4:33 Cook presents the survey's performance expectations: 82% of respondents need an end-to-end response within 500 milliseconds, 64% within 250, and nearly half expect to roll back within 15 minutes when inference degrades.
    2. 8:19 Suderman cites a Cast AI figure of 5% average GPU utilization across Kubernetes clusters and suggests Dynamic Resource Allocation, Kueue and right-sized requests as practical responses.
    3. 10:48 Suderman argues against autoscaling inference on CPU and recommends scaling on latency signals such as time to first token, using the metrics vLLM exposes with KEDA.
    4. 12:01 Cook explains why round-robin load balancing fits LLM replicas poorly, since each has its own KV cache, and relays an example where an inference-aware gateway doubled prefix cache hits from 35% to 70%.
    5. 22:15 The speakers give their five steps: start from an AI-conformant cluster, cut model startup time, autoscale on inference signals, put an inference-aware gateway in front, and set a p95 time-to-first-token SLO.
