    Data-Driven Platform Guardrails: What 16,500 Incidents Tell You to Automate First

    Henrique Santana, Henrique Dalssaso

    Henrique Santana and Henrique Dalssaso walk through the top root causes they found in more than 16,000 Kubernetes incidents, including IP exhaustion, auth failures and node provisioning problems. For each one they suggest platform guardrails to automate, and they explain how to run the same analysis on your own incident data.

    25 min slot · Intermediate · Session

    Observability and reliabilityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a list of common Kubernetes incident root causes and the guardrails these speakers suggest automating for each.

    1. 1:35 The speakers describe their dataset as more than 16,000 incidents across many Kubernetes clusters from January to mid-May, grouped with AI and then reviewed by hand. They say DNS did not make their top 10 root causes.
    2. 3:57 They say about a third of one top pattern was pods with no IP address available, from small or shared subnets, and suggest Cilium multi-pool IPAM and broader Karpenter node pools.
    3. 6:59 For auth failures they separate missing permissions, RBAC that was never bound to the pod, and policies on the target endpoint, and suggest Kyverno policies, projected service account tokens and cert-manager.
    4. 16:20 For node provisioning failures, which they put at almost 15% of incidents, they suggest setting kube-reserved and system-reserved, Cluster API machine health checks and Karpenter node auto repair.
    5. 20:59 To repeat the analysis on your own data, they suggest classifying by what actually failed rather than the symptom, ranking by frequency, and asking whether the platform, the app team or both can fix it.

    Sitesh Pattanaik

    Pattanaik demonstrates how replacing Ingress NGINX annotations with separate Gateway API and Envoy Gateway objects can leave route policies out of sync. He shows how his example uses a resource graph to manage those objects together and compares that approach with other ways to manage their lifecycle.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Networking and connectivityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you are migrating from Ingress NGINX and need a way to keep routes and their policies from drifting apart.

    1. 6:19 Pattanaik identifies the four resources in his example: an HTTPRoute plus security, backend traffic, and client traffic policies.
    2. 10:43 He explains that a policy's target reference does not tell the route about the policy or require it to share the route's lifetime.
    3. 20:47 He describes the platform team owning a ResourceGraphDefinition while an application team supplies inputs such as the hostname.
    4. 27:41 He shows that the graph manages the route and its authorization policy together when he switches to a replacement resource.
    5. 29:13 He compares Helm, kro, Crossplane, and a custom operator, asking which component will manage the full resource graph as a unit.

Joel describes how NatWest used product thinking, Kubernetes CRDs, GitOps reconciliation, and clear ownership boundaries to cut EKS cluster provisioning from eight to twelve weeks to 35 minutes.

    25 min slot · Intermediate · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a regulated-enterprise case study connecting platform-as-product theory to Kubernetes and GitOps implementation plus measured outcomes.

    1. 3:43 Joel says reuse failed when each application team copied and modified patterns, producing more than 135 variants, high cognitive load, configuration drift, and an eight-to-twelve-week path to a usable EKS cluster.
    2. 8:34 Joel says NatWest adopted platform as a product by exposing curated products through an abstracted API, using Git as the source of truth, reconciliation loops, and fleet management rather than team-by-team manual change.

    Atulpriya Sharma

    Atulpriya argues that platform engineering models built for product companies do not directly fit consultancies and services organizations. He presents delivery capital and a capability layer as ways to reuse organizational learning across client engagements while measuring ramp time, consistency, proposal strength, and delivery time.

    25 min slot · Intermediate · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you build or operate platforms in a consultancy, systems integrator, or client-facing delivery organization.

    1. 2:59 Atulpriya argues that platform engineering's familiar vocabulary was not built around the operating model of consulting firms and services organizations.
    2. 9:03 Atulpriya defines delivery capital as the learning a services organization accumulates across engagements, and says it is the asset the organization truly owns.
    3. 11:04 Atulpriya says the capability layer joins reusable organizational knowledge with an internal consultant IDP of sandboxes and demo clusters so teams can apply prior learning.
    4. 12:57 Atulpriya maps the approach across cloud, IaC, CI/CD, observability, and monitoring, with artifacts such as decision records, checklists, templates, baselines, and named alert owners.
    5. 23:29 Atulpriya argues that ownership, rotation, and billable-work identity block adoption in services firms, and says time-to-ramp, delivery consistency, proposal strength, and delivery time are the measures to watch.

    Carlos Santana argues that a Kubernetes platform should treat agent-generated code as untrusted and provision isolation, credentials, and network policy separately. He describes using kro to compose those controls behind a platform API and shows captures of an Agent Sandbox warm pool assigning a ready pod.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automationSecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you need to give teams a Kubernetes API for agents without giving those agents unrestricted access.

    1. 5:25 Santana describes SPIFFE and SPIRE as a way to identify an agent by URI and associate that identity with a credential across different environments.
    2. 12:14 Santana shows a kro ResourceGraphDefinition and explains how a declarative resource graph can give platform users a custom API without requiring the platform team to write a Go controller.
    3. 19:05 Santana compares gVisor, Kata Containers, and AWS Nitro Sandboxes as different isolation choices for running untrusted agent code.
    4. 21:27 Santana shows captures of a two-pod Agent Sandbox warm pool and says applying a claim assigned a ready pod instead of waiting for a fresh sandbox to start.
    5. 25:48 Santana describes a credential proxy that attaches a credential to an outbound request without handing the underlying secret to the agent, and gives an Agent Substrate example.
