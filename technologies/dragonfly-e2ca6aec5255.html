    Explore TAG Workloads Foundation: Core Runtime, Batch Scheduling, and Moar

    Stephen Rust, Yuan Tang, Marlow Warnicke, Rajas Kakodkar, Paco Xu

    Five leads of the CNCF TAG Workloads Foundation explain what a technical advisory group does, what this one covers, and how its batch subproject and white paper initiatives run. They then walk through graduated, incubating and sandbox projects they place in the TAG's scope and describe how newcomers can join meetings, reviews and initiatives.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Community and open source adoptionKubernetes internals and runtimesAccelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: you maintain or use a workload, runtime or scheduling project and want to know what the TAG does and how to take part.

    1. 2:10 Stephen Rust describes a TAG as a technical advisory group that supports CNCF projects and says it is a way to scale advice across many projects with few people.
    2. 5:13 Rust says initiatives are time-bound efforts with a deliverable such as a white paper, while subprojects carry longer-term work, and that the TAG helps projects with their maturity reviews.
    3. 10:23 Marlow Warnicke describes the batch subproject's scheduling capabilities white paper, which she says aims to compare schedulers under different workload mixes, and asks for contributors.
    4. 14:15 Paco Xu lists graduated projects he places in the TAG's scope and says Karmada and Kubeflow graduated this year and KubeVirt graduated recently.
    5. 19:07 Rajas Kakodkar says no single role or expertise is needed to get involved, and suggests joining meetings, sharing pain points, demoing a project or reviewing white papers.

    Follow the GPUs: How a Regulated Fortune 100 Serves Customers When No Single Cloud Has Enough

    Yuva Peavler, Michael Kinsley

    Yuva Peavler and Michael Kinsley of GEICO describe their hybrid cloud platform, a flat network across providers with a zero trust overlay built on SPIFFE/SPIRE, Istio and Cilium, and say it lets them move workloads to wherever GPUs are available. Michael then shares a tool they wrote to probe GPU availability across AWS and Azure regions, what four weeks of samples showed them, and their idea for a booking agent that reserves capacity ahead of demand.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingNetworking and connectivityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you need GPUs without long reservations and want measured data on whether multi-region and multi-cloud help.

    1. 1:45 Michael gives the takeaways first: current single-GPU SKUs need multi-region support, flagship eight-GPU nodes have to be booked ahead, and he says the autoscaler will not do the booking.
    2. 3:43 Yuva explains the platform from the bottom up: one flat L3 network across every provider, identically built Kubernetes clusters, then SPIFFE/SPIRE identity, Istio mutual TLS and Cilium deny-by-default policy.
    3. 8:54 Yuva says they made SPIRE's data store pluggable in their fork and run Cassandra behind it for an active-active multi-region control plane, and that they have proposed this upstream.
    4. 16:54 Michael introduces their Follow the GPUs tool, which asks for GPUs, doubles the request after each yes until it gets a no, then waits about an hour and starts again.
    5. 22:02 Michael reports that in their data multi-region did not matter for small single-GPU SKUs but raised the chance of getting an RTX Pro 6000 from about 45% to 70 to 75%.

    Many Clusters, One GPU Pool: Locality-Aware Scheduling and Data Delivery With Kueue and Dragonfly

    Yiqing Wang, Adam Buran

    Yiqing Wang and Adam Buran describe how Roblox shares GPU capacity across clusters using Kueue while leaving each cluster to check its own capacity. They explain how a locality-aware dispatcher chooses clusters and how Dragonfly caches training data and checkpoints near the jobs that use them.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingData, storage and state

    Watch this if: You operate GPU clusters in more than one location and want to share capacity without repeatedly pulling the same datasets across regions.

    1. 6:55 The speakers describe two levels of MultiKueue admission: the hub checks team quota, then the candidate clusters check node capacity and the first to admit the job runs it with KubeRay.
    2. 8:15 The speakers explain how the dispatcher first filters for requirements such as GPU type and full-job fit, then prefers the data region for a limited time before considering other regions.
    3. 9:19 The speakers explain how Roblox separates pool-wide guaranteed quota from cluster-level borrowing and uses preemption to reclaim borrowed GPUs when a guaranteed job arrives.
    4. 14:51 The speakers describe Dragonfly looking for file pieces in the local cache, other peers, and seed peers before falling back to Amazon S3.
    5. 15:55 The speakers report that half of the data served came from node-local cache and another 43 percent from peers in the same cluster, leaving 7 percent served from Amazon S3.

    Scaling AI Model Distribution with Dragonfly: From HuggingFace to Production GPU Clusters

    Pavan Madduri

    Pavan Madduri, a Dragonfly contributor presenting alone because co-speaker Wenbo Qi could not travel, argues that pulling model weights of hundreds of gigabytes onto every GPU node at once is an infrastructure problem and explains how Dragonfly's peer-to-peer distribution and preheating address it. He then walks through the two pieces he says he contributed, the Hugging Face and ModelScope backends and the dragonfly-injector webhook, plus a cross-cloud pattern and the project roadmap.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Data, storage and stateAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: large model downloads are slowing your GPU nodes or raising your egress bill and you want to see how Dragonfly handles it.

    1. 4:24 Pavan Madduri describes the thundering herd he sees with model weights: a scale-out sends 100 GPU nodes to the same registry at once, the origin saturates and downloads time out and retry.
    2. 10:04 He cites a test of a 130 GB model on 200 nodes where only the seed peer pulled from the origin, which he puts at about 99% less egress than 26 TB, and adds that the number depends on the setup.
    3. 13:01 He shows the Hugging Face and ModelScope backends: dfget with an hf scheme makes the seed peer fetch the model from the hub once and other nodes get the pieces from peers.
    4. 16:31 He explains the dragonfly-injector: a mutating admission webhook adds the Dragonfly client and daemon socket to pods carrying an inject annotation, so teams change Helm values instead of application code.
    5. 23:06 He lists the roadmap he took from version 2.6: a Python SDK after the Rust and Go ones, RDMA-based distribution between peers, and bandwidth negotiation across parent peers.

    Securing the Agentic Future: Distributing LLM Weights and Sandboxed AI Agents via Harbor

    Chenyu Zhang, Yan Wang

    Chenyu Zhang and Yan Wang present Harbor as a shared registry for model weights and sandboxed agent images, with different checks for the two supply chains. They demonstrate importing a model from Hugging Face and mounting it in Kubernetes, then discuss declaring an agent's requested permissions alongside its image.

    30 min slot · Beginner · Session

    AI agents and automationSecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you want to distribute model weights through Harbor and review the permissions a sandboxed agent requests.

    1. 6:45 The speaker explains that ModelPack packages a model as an OCI artifact so a compliant registry can store it without tying distribution to a serving stack.
    2. 12:32 The speaker distinguishes model scanning from ordinary CVE scanning and describes signing model artifacts with Cosign as a separate supply-chain check.
    3. 17:33 The speaker shows a Harbor sync policy for a Hugging Face model, previews the source file list, and inspects the model card and license after the sync.
    4. 19:43 The speaker shows a Kubernetes deployment pointing at Harbor and says Kubernetes mounts the synced model files into the pod.
    5. 22:14 The speaker describes the Docker Sandbox Kit Spec as a declaration attached to the agent image that lists the network destinations, credentials, and paths the agent asks to use.
