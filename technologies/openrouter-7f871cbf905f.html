    Building the Next Generation of AI with Open Source

    Warren Barkley, Ali Golshan

    The speakers describe NVIDIA's open-source contributions to GPU scheduling and argue that agent workloads are changing how they size AI infrastructure. They introduce NVIDIA Kubernetes Engine for building AI infrastructure and describe OpenShell as a runtime for governing agent behavior.

    7 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    AI inference and servingCommunity and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you want NVIDIA's overview of its open-source AI infrastructure work, from GPU scheduling to agent runtimes.

    1. 0:27 The speaker says NVIDIA donated its DRA driver, KAI Scheduler, and Run:ai to the open-source community.
    2. 1:26 The speaker uses OpenRouter as an example, saying agent-generated tokens passed human-generated tokens there in February.
    3. 2:08 The speaker says NVIDIA is specifying more CPUs per GPU for some AI factory workloads and attributes the shift to agents.
    4. 3:15 The speaker introduces NVIDIA Kubernetes Engine as a set of components and recipes for getting from bare-metal provisioning to serving model tokens.
    5. 3:40 The speaker describes OpenShell as a runtime for governing agent behavior and says NVIDIA is donating it to the cloud-native community.

    Chris Aniszczyk, Jonathan Bryce, Rita Zhang, Kevin Cochrane, Robert Nishihara, Justin Boitano, Bassam Tabbara

    The opening speakers say that agents are driving inference demand and describe how cloud providers are contributing fixes from production back to open source. The session includes announcements about KubeRay and OpenShell and a ModelPlane demo that moves an inference deployment between clouds.

    Length not listed · Level not listed · Type not listed

    AI inference and servingCommunity and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you want the opening announcements on AI infrastructure, with examples spanning Kubernetes inference, agent controls, and multi-cloud model serving.

    2. 16:27 Zhang says CoreWeave feeds lessons from production back upstream, citing fair GPU scheduling in Kueue and hardening work in vLLM, NVIDIA Dynamo, and Grove.

    How Kubernetes Handles AI Generated Security Reports

    Vyom Yadav, Nathan Herz

    Nathan Herz and Vyom Yadav of the Kubernetes Security Response Committee describe how a more than tenfold rise in vulnerability reports over a year, which they attribute to AI tools, has strained a committee of ten volunteers, and walk through the intake and triage process and where it is bottlenecked. They then cover the threat model behind past Kubernetes CVEs, examples of AI-generated reports that fell outside it, and automation they want to build for duplicate detection, threat model checks and reproduction.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Security, identity and policyCommunity and open source adoptionAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you triage security reports for an open source project, or you submit AI-assisted reports and want to know what maintainers need from them.

    1. 1:00 Nathan Herz says the Kubernetes HackerOne program received 15 reports in August 2025 and 219 a year later, and that only around 1% of reports result in a CVE while about 5% need the committee's triage.
    2. 8:14 He says the committee is ten volunteers with roughly five hours a week each, that a single reporter has sent hundreds of automated reports to the mailing list, and that email intake will be forwarded to HackerOne.
    3. 12:21 He reports that duplicates closed in HackerOne went from 20 in 2025 to 700 in 2026, and sketches a duplicate filter that retrieves similar past reports from a vector database and asks an LLM for a confidence score.
    4. 19:09 Vyom Yadav gives an example of an AI-generated report about name-only matching in the kubectl credential plugin allowlist, behavior he says an official Kubernetes blog post already documented.
    5. 23:13 He suggests projects define their threat model somewhere tools can find it, mentioning the idea of a per-repository threat model Markdown file, and says writing one for every Kubernetes component is no longer realistic.

    I Built an AI Running Coach: A Personal kagent Agent in Production

    Adam Gordon Bell

    Adam Gordon Bell of Pulumi tells how he built a personal AI running coach that messages him in Slack, first as a Lambda function and then as a single long-running kagent session on a small Kubernetes cluster, to train for a trail race against a friend. He describes the design choices he says worked for him, shows his own training graphs from the year, and encourages the audience to build small agents for their own needs.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you want a small, concrete example of a long-running personal agent on Kubernetes, told as a story with the design choices that kept it simple.

    1. 9:15 Bell describes his first version: an AWS Lambda called by a Strava webhook, a scraper for his Peloton treadmill data, a morning cron job, DynamoDB for storage and one LLM call that posted to Slack.
    2. 12:22 He shows the kagent version, an agent defined as one Kubernetes resource deployed with Pulumi, where the Strava hook, the Peloton sync and a morning cron all post messages into the running session over the agent-to-agent protocol.
    3. 13:54 His main lesson is to use deterministic code wherever possible: the MCP tool parses raw run data and hands the agent only the date, duration, incline and heart rate zones.
    4. 15:25 He keeps one session going indefinitely and handles memory with a custom compaction rule under fixed headings, plus tools that let the agent write to a plan file and an athlete file.
    5. 27:47 He reports finishing the 30K race in 4:30 against his friend's 4:41 from the previous year, after a year in which he says his weeks with three or more runs almost doubled.
