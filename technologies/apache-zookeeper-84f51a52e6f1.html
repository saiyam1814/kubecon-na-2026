    Fencing Tokens and the Three-Party Contract: What Most Distributed Locks Get Wrong

    Benjamin Wang

    Benjamin Wang explains how a paused client can keep writing after its lock lease expires, and how fencing tokens, an idea he credits to Martin Kleppmann, let the resource reject those writes. He frames this as a three-party contract between the lock service, the client and the target resource, and shows disco, his open source coordination framework built on etcd that implements it.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Data, storage and stateObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you use distributed locks or leader election and want to see why the protected resource has to check a token, not just the client.

    1. 2:36 Wang walks through Kleppmann's fencing token example: the lock service issues token 33 and then 34, the storage remembers the highest token it has seen, and the paused client's write with 33 is rejected.
    2. 4:12 He describes his three-party contract: the coordination service issues monotonically increasing tokens, the client attaches the token to every request, and the target resource checks it against its high watermark, the part he says most people forget.
    3. 6:18 He says disco offers lock, read-write lock, semaphore and leader election behind a pluggable provider model, with etcd fully supported today and ZooKeeper or Redis as possible later backends.
    4. 13:50 In the live demo client A takes the lock and hangs, client B acquires it after the five-second lease expires and writes with a 200, and client A's late write gets a 409 Conflict.
    5. 17:01 Asked about several replicas of the resource server each holding its own in-memory high watermark, Wang agrees the replicas would need to sync the watermark between them.
