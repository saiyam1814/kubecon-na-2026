    Falco Never Flies Alone: Runtime Security as an Ecosystem

    Leonardo Grasso, Alessandro Cannarella

    Leonardo Grasso and Alessandro Cannarella describe how Falco, now ten years old, spent the year consolidating its core and growing an ecosystem around it. They cover core and plugin updates, new experiments for AI coding agents, Falco Operator 0.5, and a from-scratch rewrite of Falcosidekick as version 3.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Kubernetes internals and runtimesSecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you run Falco for runtime security and want to see what changed in its core, the Falco Operator and Falcosidekick.

    1. 0:51 Grasso explains how Falco taps the Linux kernel with an eBPF probe or kernel module, enriches events with plugins, and matches them against YAML rules.
    2. 3:22 Grasso lists core cleanups such as removing the legacy BPF probe and gVisor support, new list operators in the rule language, and an experimental GPU hijacking rule.
    3. 6:38 Grasso introduces a new subproject that gives coding agents a version-pinned knowledge base and skills for contributing to the Falco code base.
    4. 16:35 Cannarella says Falco Operator 0.5 pulls artifacts from the registry once, serves them to every node, and checks rules against the Falco version before installing.
    5. 19:55 Cannarella announces Falcosidekick version 3, rewritten with per-output pipelines, live config reloads, API and UI configuration, and a single binary.

    From Citadel to Contract: Migrating a Monolith with 500+ Services to Kubernetes

    Holly Mooneyham

    Holly Mooneyham describes how Cisco Meraki started with Kubernetes outposts and then designed a platform contract for migrating parts of its monolith. She explains how the team moved workloads incrementally across two platforms and what she learned about ownership, early adopters, and retiring the old system.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Kubernetes internals and runtimesPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you are moving a mature monolith to Kubernetes and need to decide what the platform owns.

    1. 5:33 Mooneyham says Meraki initially left the Citadel monolith alone and put early services that did not need to sit close to the databases in separate Kubernetes outposts.
    2. 9:01 Mooneyham defines a platform contract as agreed promises, interfaces, and ownership boundaries, including who takes responsibility when a team leaves the preferred path.
    3. 12:58 Mooneyham shows the Elastic Shard deployment flow and explains why even a shared Helm chart did not settle where teams should draw the ownership boundary.
    4. 17:20 Mooneyham says the deployment system lets teams move workloads between the legacy and Kubernetes platforms by service, by shard, or by both, using capacity needs and service-owner intent.
    5. 22:35 Mooneyham says retiring the old platform is part of finishing the migration, not a task to leave indefinitely after the new platform goes live.

    Two Namespaces, One Mesh: Zero-Downtime Releases for an Always-On Aviation Platform

    Rahul Raj Valia Veetil

    The speaker describes how his team at Etihad Airways releases a three-tier airline operations platform without downtime by running the live and candidate versions in two namespaces behind one Istio mesh. He says a header routes only testers to the candidate, and after approval users get a two-minute notice before traffic switches and the old version is drained.

    Length not listed · Level not listed · Type not listed

    Unclassified

    Watch this if: You need blue-green releases across front end, back end and database for an application that cannot take downtime.

    1. 0:46 The speaker says the operations platform must always be on, and that a release has to keep a static front end, a Kubernetes back end and a database compatible.
    2. 1:57 He describes two namespaces, one holding the live version and one the candidate, with an Istio ingress deciding where traffic goes.
    3. 3:54 He says Istio marks the new version as a candidate reachable only by testers, and after promotion the team monitors for two to three minutes and can roll back.
    4. 5:06 He shows that users get a pop-up with a two-minute timer to save their work before being moved to the new version.
    5. 6:22 He says a header passed through the front end, back end and database channels is what sends testers to the preview version, and he then plays a recorded demo of the pipeline.
