    Agentic AI, Assembled in the Open

    Jorge Palma

    Jorge Palma asks how teams can reproduce an agent's work after its compute goes away and define what runs inside it. He introduces a durable session journal and a portable agent definition, then walks through a scenario where an agent pauses for human approval and resumes later.

    5 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    AI agents and automationCommunity and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you want to understand what an agent platform needs for reproducible work, human approvals, and auditing.

    1. 0:37 Palma frames two problems: preserving an agent's work after compute is released and defining what runs inside the agent.
    2. 1:47 Palma explains how recording completions in a hash-linked, append-only journal lets a session be replayed or forked without making the same model calls again.
    3. 3:03 Palma introduces a portable, versioned JSON definition for an agent's instructions, tool providers, build information, and evaluation cases.
    4. 4:14 Palma describes a remediation workflow that waits for human approval and releases idle compute while the session waits.
    5. 4:49 Palma walks through how a suspended session resumes for a change-ticket request and how an auditor could replay or verify the record later.

    Kubernetes Secrets Were a Bad Idea. Now What?

    Maxime Coquerel

    Maxime Coquerel argues, as his own opinion, that Kubernetes Secrets are risky because they are only base64 encoded in etcd, readable with broad RBAC permissions and have no lifecycle. He recommends encrypting etcd with a customer managed key, keeping secrets out of Git and mounting secrets from a real vault at runtime with the Secrets Store CSI Driver or the OpenBao agent.

    5 min slot · Beginner · Lightning Talk

    Security, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you are deciding whether Kubernetes Secrets are good enough for your platform or which alternative to use.

    1. 0:43 Maxime Coquerel says a Kubernetes Secret is stored in etcd only base64 encoded, not encrypted, and that etcd is a key value store, not a vault.
    2. 1:46 He lists the weaknesses he sees, including that anyone with get, list or watch permissions can read secret contents.
    3. 2:05 He adds that Kubernetes Secrets have no lifecycle: no expiration date and no automatic renewal.
    4. 2:47 His first recommendation is to encrypt etcd with a customer managed key, which he says managed services such as Azure Kubernetes Service support.
    5. 3:49 He compares Sealed Secrets, SOPS and External Secrets Operator, and recommends mounting secrets as files at runtime with the Secrets Store CSI Driver or the OpenBao agent.

    Minting Your Own Tokens: The Server Side of the Token Economy

    Suraj Deshmukh, Nilekh Chaudhari

    Suraj Deshmukh and Nilekh Chaudhari walk through vLLM deployments on Kubernetes, from a single GPU to a model split across two nodes. They compare throughput and first-token latency and explain how GPU memory and interconnects influence the deployment choices.

    80 min slot · Beginner · Tutorial

    AI inference and serving

    Watch this if: You want to deploy a model with vLLM and understand what changes when it no longer fits on one GPU.

    1. 15:18 The presenters inspect the running single-GPU deployment and show where the GPU request is specified in the vLLM configuration.
    2. 22:07 The presenters explain the difference between prefill, which processes the prompt and produces the first token, and decode, which generates the rest of the reply.
    3. 37:59 The presenters raise benchmark concurrency from one request to four and discuss why higher aggregate token throughput need not mean a shorter wait for each user.
    4. 53:21 The presenters animate how tensor-parallel workers exchange partial results at each layer and discuss why GPU interconnect speed matters.
    5. 1:07:01 The presenters introduce LeaderWorkerSet's leader and worker pattern before showing how they configure pipeline parallelism across two nodes.
