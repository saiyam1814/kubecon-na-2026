  • Platform Engineering Day

    Data-Driven Platform Guardrails: What 16,500 Incidents Tell You to Automate First

    Henrique Santana, Henrique Dalssaso

    Henrique Santana and Henrique Dalssaso walk through the top root causes they found in more than 16,000 Kubernetes incidents, including IP exhaustion, auth failures and node provisioning problems. For each one they suggest platform guardrails to automate, and they explain how to run the same analysis on your own incident data.

    25 min slot · Intermediate · Session

    Observability and reliabilityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a list of common Kubernetes incident root causes and the guardrails these speakers suggest automating for each.

    1. 1:35 The speakers describe their dataset as more than 16,000 incidents across many Kubernetes clusters from January to mid-May, grouped with AI and then reviewed by hand. They say DNS did not make their top 10 root causes.
    2. 3:57 They say about a third of one top pattern was pods with no IP address available, from small or shared subnets, and suggest Cilium multi-pool IPAM and broader Karpenter node pools.
    3. 6:59 For auth failures they separate missing permissions, RBAC that was never bound to the pod, and policies on the target endpoint, and suggest Kyverno policies, projected service account tokens and cert-manager.
    4. 16:20 For node provisioning failures, which they put at almost 15% of incidents, they suggest setting kube-reserved and system-reserved, Cluster API machine health checks and Karpenter node auto repair.
    5. 20:59 To repeat the analysis on your own data, they suggest classifying by what actually failed rather than the symptom, ranking by frequency, and asking whether the platform, the app team or both can fix it.

    Engineering Kubernetes for the Next Era of Workloads

    Mike Stefaniak

    Mike Stefaniak describes bottlenecks AWS found when running large Kubernetes clusters and says the team contributed fixes upstream. He walks through watch-cache and scheduler changes, accelerator-aware provisioning with Karpenter, and parallel image unpacking in containerd.

    5 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    Accelerators and workload schedulingKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run large GPU clusters and want concrete examples of upstream fixes from image unpacking to accelerator placement.

    1. 0:47 Stefaniak says AWS replaced a long-held Kubernetes API server watch-cache lock with a lazy snapshot, cutting writer wait from over half a second to 2 ms in its million-pod benchmark.
    2. 1:03 Stefaniak says a new Horizontal Pod Autoscaler selector store removed a read/write bottleneck, letting AWS run four times as many HPA workers in its largest clusters.
    3. 1:21 Stefaniak says an incremental scheduler cache avoided rebuilding volume reference counts and that AWS saw scheduling stay above 300 pods per second in its largest clusters.
    4. 2:28 Stefaniak explains that Dynamic Resource Allocation can describe GPU and network topology, and says Karpenter can now model devices for instances it has not launched yet.
    5. 3:48 Stefaniak says parallel layer unpacking in containerd brought a 10 GB image pull from about two minutes to under 45 seconds in AWS's testing.

    SIG Scheduling Update: Multi-Level and Topology-Aware Workload Scheduling

    Heba Elayoty, Dominik Marcinski

    The SIG Scheduling speakers explain how the Kubernetes scheduler handles pod groups, nested workloads, and topology constraints. They describe plans for sharing scheduling logic with autoscalers and letting Kueue focus on quotas and fair sharing.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: you want to understand how Kubernetes evaluates groups of pods and where autoscalers fit.

    1. 8:45 The speakers explain why gang scheduling needs to work alongside the lifecycle for individual pods in the same scheduler.
    2. 19:52 They walk through a pod-group scheduling attempt that uses one snapshot for all pods in the group.
    3. 23:15 They show an example where a failed second pod leaves the group below its minimum, so the scheduler reverts the first placement.
    4. 26:43 They illustrate trying the same pod group against multiple topology options to see which placements are feasible.
    5. 31:07 They describe the read-only scheduler library as a way for callers to test a hypothetical placement with the same logic as the live scheduler.

    SIG Storage Intro and Deep Dive

    Xing Yang, Hemant Kumar, Michelle Au

    Xing Yang, Hemant Kumar and Michelle Au explain what Kubernetes SIG Storage owns, from persistent volume APIs to CSI and COSI, and walk through recent storage features by category. They cover volume health, security changes, ephemeral volume updates, data protection, storage-aware scheduling and features still in design, then explain how to get involved.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Data, storage and stateKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run stateful workloads on Kubernetes and want a tour of the storage features landing in recent releases.

    1. 2:49 Kumar shows volume health reported on PVC and pod objects, plus a new alpha condition for the health of a whole storage subsystem on the CSINode object.
    2. 6:41 Au explains that the gitRepo volume plugin was removed because kubelet ran git clone on the host, and recommends an init container or sidecar instead.
    3. 10:02 Au introduces dynamic node-local ephemeral volumes, alpha in 1.38, which add and remove volumes from a running pod without recreating it.
    4. 14:55 Yang describes volume group snapshots, GA in 1.36, and changed block tracking, beta in 1.36, which lets backup vendors fetch only the blocks that changed.
    5. 19:58 Kumar explains an opt-in CSIDriver setting that stops pods from being scheduled to nodes without the driver, and how it interacts with Cluster Autoscaler.
