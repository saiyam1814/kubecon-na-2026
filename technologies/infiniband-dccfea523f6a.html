    From Zero to GPU Cloud: 12 months building Mistral Compute on top of Open Source

    Léonard Suslian

    Léonard Suslian of Mistral AI describes how a team of about ten built Mistral Compute, a bare-metal Kubernetes service he says now manages 5,000 hosts, on Cluster API, Metal3, Kamaji and Sveltos plus its own operators. He then goes through what he says broke along the way, from Ironic and board management controllers to SNAT exhaustion, etcd storage and a DHCP storm.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you are building or running bare-metal GPU clusters and want a candid list of what breaks between the racks arriving and tenants getting Kubernetes.

    1. 9:07 Suslian explains running each tenant's control plane as pods in the management cluster with Kamaji, which he says saves hardware and turns a control plane upgrade into a deployment rollout.
    2. 12:53 He describes using Sveltos from the management cluster to install add-ons chosen by the tenant, with drift detection that he says restores a deleted Cilium DaemonSet.
    3. 18:47 He says the team replaced Ironic with its own API-compatible rewrite in Go, scoped to their hardware, and went from first commit to production in 19 days.
    4. 21:03 He says hosts that cannot be fixed automatically get an AI agent in a pod with temporary credentials and scoped network access, and reports more than 20 such runs a day.
    5. 25:02 He recounts waking up to almost 900 nodes down because 10-minute DHCP leases caused a renewal storm the server could not keep up with, fixed by longer leases.

    Multicluster AMA: The Community Answers

    August Simonelli, Laura Lorenz, Liqian Luo, Trevor Swanson

    Moderator August Simonelli puts audience questions about running many Kubernetes clusters to Laura Lorenz of SIG Multicluster, Liqian Luo of Azure Kubernetes Service and Trevor Swanson of Evernorth. The panelists share their views on going from one cluster to many, GitOps and scheduling, staged upgrades, self-service for developers, and where AI fits.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Panel

    Platform engineering and developer experienceNetworking and connectivityCommunity and open source adoption

    Watch this if: your cluster count grew without a plan and you want to hear how an operator, a cloud provider and the upstream SIG think about managing a fleet.

    1. 6:05 Laura Lorenz says SIG Multicluster focuses on APIs for cross-cluster problems and names the Multi-Cluster Services API, the Work API and the newer Cluster Inventory API.
    2. 8:05 Trevor Swanson advises building for a fleet of one cluster: nothing bespoke to a cluster name, label everything and make it policy-based from day one.
    3. 8:48 Liqian Luo says GitOps may suit static placement, but in her view is not enough when workloads must be placed by GPU type, topology and available capacity.
    4. 18:17 Lorenz says the SIG has focused on small composable building blocks rather than one monolithic solution, and that it could do more to explain how the pieces fit together.
    5. 23:45 Swanson argues that declarative beats clever when agents act on a fleet, and Luo says AI helps with observing and diagnosing but that letting it change clusters arbitrarily is not safe.

    Quota Isn't Enough: Topology Aware Scheduling on Modern GPU Clusters

    Amy Chen, JJ Jordan

    Amy Chen and JJ Jordan explain why GPU quota alone does not tell you whether a training job can fit within the physical network layout. They walk through Kueue topology-aware admission and share operational examples involving node replacement, preemption, and fragmented capacity.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: You schedule distributed GPU training and need to reason about rack boundaries, queue delays, or stranded capacity.

    1. 3:58 The speakers compare two placements with the same GPU count and explain why an uneven split across racks makes every all-reduce round wait on slower cross-rack transfers.
    2. 8:07 The speakers show how an example 128-GPU job asks Kueue for two 64-GPU slices and how admission checks both quota and rack-level fit.
    3. 13:31 The speakers compare unconstrained, preferred, and required placement, framing the choice as a trade-off between queue time and training speed.
    4. 16:37 The speakers describe replacing one unhealthy node within the same topology so a run can resume from a checkpoint without readmitting the whole workload.
    5. 19:16 The speakers explain why Kueue can decline to preempt smaller jobs when evicting them still would not free a large enough contiguous placement.

    What the Docs Don't Tell You: A Telecom's Year Operating Cache-Aware LLM Inference on Kubernetes

    YoungJoon Jeong, Seol Heo

    The speakers describe how LG Uplus raised its KV cache hit rate from 71.6% to 95.4% on 16 vLLM replicas by reordering prompts, routing each request to the pod that holds its prefix with llm-d, and adding a CPU memory tier for evicted blocks. They say time to first token is queue time plus prefill time, so once prefill was cached they tuned concurrency, and they report that splitting prefill and decode cut inter-token latency while lowering throughput.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    AI inference and servingObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: You serve LLMs for multi-turn or agent traffic on Kubernetes and want measured numbers on what prefix-aware routing, cache tiers and prefill/decode separation each bought.

    1. 4:51 YoungJoon Jeong says vLLM hashes prompt blocks in a chain, so one small change near the start, such as a timestamp in the system prompt, turns every later block into a miss; his advice is to put the stable part first.
    2. 9:07 He frames KV cache hit rate as ceiling times placement times survival, which he calls their own framing and not an upstream metric, with a different owner for each factor.
    3. 13:05 Seol Heo says their initial hit rate was 71.6% because routing on pod load sent turns of one conversation to different pods, duplicating prefixes in GPU memory and causing evictions.
    4. 17:38 He says that after prefill was optimized the queue became the bottleneck, and setting vLLM's max number of sequences to 24 brought time to first token from 2.09 to 0.39 seconds in their benchmark.
    5. 20:11 He reports that separating prefill and decode pools, with KV transfer over InfiniBand RDMA through NIXL, cut inter-token latency from 69.8 to 19.5 milliseconds while generation throughput fell about 30%.
