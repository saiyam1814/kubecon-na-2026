    Following the Evidence with HolmesGPT: Troubleshooting Kubernetes Apps and Infrastructure

    Yash Sharma

    Yash Sharma, presenting alone after saying his co-speaker could not attend, describes his own experiment with HolmesGPT on a demo shop application running on a DigitalOcean Kubernetes cluster with three planted faults. He shows it investigating a full Postgres volume, a CPU-hungry CronJob and a cloud-side volume attachment limit, and then outlines how he would run it in production with read-only access and human approval for changes.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Observability and reliabilityAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you are considering an AI troubleshooting assistant for Kubernetes and want to see it work through storage, CPU and cloud-provider faults before deciding how much access to give it.

    1. 4:41 Sharma describes HolmesGPT's loop: starting from an alert thread, it calls toolsets such as kubectl, PromQL, APIs and SQL, reruns the investigation if the results are not enough, and returns a root cause with evidence and a suggested fix.
    2. 12:21 In the live demo, after he fills the Postgres volume and checkout errors rise, the holmes ask command returns evidence of no space left on the device along with possible fixes.
    3. 17:26 He says HolmesGPT runs read-only in his cluster, so a kubectl exec it attempted was denied as needing approval, and it still traced the slow checkout API to the batch job he had added.
    4. 18:38 For the third case he explains a setup where a 16th pod cannot get a volume because of a per-node attachment limit on DigitalOcean Kubernetes, and shows holmes investigate alertmanager attributing the pending pod to the cloud provider side.
    5. 24:44 His security advice is to keep the tool read-only, require human approval for any write, issue least-privilege tokens per task, and check the logs rather than fully trusting the output, since logs can carry injected content.

    Kubernetes under Fire: Igniting Performance and Burning Through Bottlenecks with Kube-Burner

    Andrew Collins

    Andrew Collins introduces kube-burner as a Go tool that creates, deletes and churns Kubernetes resources from YAML configs while collecting metrics, so teams can reproduce scale problems in a lab. He lists recent features and community testing work, and closes by urging people to burn down their lab rather than production.

    5 min slot · Level not listed · Lightning Talk

    Observability and reliabilityKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you want a four-minute overview of what kube-burner does before trying it for cluster scale testing.

    1. 0:34 Andrew frames the problem with a simple pod-count reproducer and the questions it raises about scale rate, latency measurement, namespaces, metrics and churn.
    2. 1:16 Andrew describes kube-burner as a Go tool that takes YAML configs to create, delete and churn Kubernetes resources by count or percentage.
    3. 1:29 Andrew says it can collect metrics from the cluster's Prometheus and write them to JSON files, OpenSearch, local storage or Prometheus TSDB chunks.
    4. 1:54 Andrew lists newer features including scaffolded configs, pprof collection, grouping resources into waves, and incremental load that ramps up gradually.
    5. 2:35 Andrew names community testing done with kube-burner, including OVN-Kubernetes, node remediation operators, schedulers, Cluster Autoscaler and KubeVirt VM scale tests.

    Securing Rural Colorado with Open Source: Running a Student-Led SOC on Kubernetes

    Maciej Wal

    Maciej Wal describes how the Cybersecurity Center at Metropolitan State University of Denver replaced a vendor's security monitoring service, first with four virtual machines and then with a Kubernetes platform that he says he runs as the one full-time staff member. He covers the outages and adoption problems along the way, and how students learn log analysis and GitOps on the platform that monitors public sector partners.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Security, identity and policyObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you are a very small team weighing Kubernetes for a security monitoring service, or want ideas for teaching students with real infrastructure.

    1. 3:19 The speaker says that after the vendor ended the service he built a replacement in about 66 days on four virtual machines, with WireGuard, single-node OpenSearch and a new ticketing tool.
    2. 6:45 The speaker explains picking the Kubernetes stack from the CNCF landscape, including Keycloak, Argo CD, OpenTofu and Fluent Bit, and says it cost around $1,000 a month.
    3. 8:33 The speaker recounts a flood of Suricata logs taking down the OpenSearch cluster, which he says had too little memory and no autoscaling, and learning of breakage days late.
    4. 12:01 The speaker says one of the hardest problems he found was the gap between offering self-service and people using it, after students did not adopt browser certificate access.
    5. 13:48 The speaker describes a student workshop on Argo CD, Envoy Gateway, cert-manager and GitHub Actions that ended with deploying a Minecraft server through GitOps.
