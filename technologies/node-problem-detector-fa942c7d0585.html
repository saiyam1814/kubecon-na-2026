    Containerd: Project Update and Deep Dive

    Wei Fu, Samuel Karp

    Maintainers Samuel Karp and Wei Fu describe containerd's move to a four-month release cadence aligned with Kubernetes, a yearly long-term support release, and the features they shipped in 2.4 and propose for 2.5. Karp then explains how a surge of AI-assisted security reports in 2026 changed the project's triage and patching process.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Kubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run containerd under Kubernetes and want to plan upgrades around the new release and LTS schedule, or you maintain a project facing a flood of AI-generated security reports.

    1. 1:16 Karp says containerd moved to a four-month release cadence timed with Kubernetes so that features depending on the container runtime arrive in both projects at about the same time.
    2. 2:33 Karp describes a long-term support release that comes out once a year and is supported for two years, giving a year of overlap between LTS releases.
    3. 14:37 Fu explains that direct unpack for overlayfs removed the implicit flush on unmount, so a crash could leave a committed snapshot without its data, and says a single EROFS layer file is easier to fsync.
    4. 18:43 Fu presents a proposed 2.5 feature, a sandboxed runc shim that handles the sandbox API, with the goal of removing the pause container that he calls a waste of resources for most pods.
    5. 21:51 Karp reports 156 incoming security reports in 2026 against 36 from 2020 to 2025, with 14 CVEs issued this year, and attributes the rise to LLM-assisted bug hunting.

    SIG-Node: Intro and Deep Dive

    Peter Hunt, Tim Allclair, Dawn Chen, Mrunal Patel, Sergey Kanzhelev

    Host Peter Hunt introduces what SIG Node owns and then runs a panel of SIG Node leads on how the kubelet should serve agentic workloads alongside traditional ones. The panelists discuss dynamic containers, agent frameworks that bypass Kubernetes features, and how the SIG is trying to move enhancements from alpha to GA faster.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Kubernetes internals and runtimesAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you run agent or other short-lived workloads on Kubernetes and want to hear where SIG Node is taking the pod and the kubelet.

    1. 3:54 Peter Hunt lists what he says went GA in Kubernetes 1.36 and 1.37, including user namespaces, PSI and OCI volumes, and what moved to beta.
    2. 9:36 Dawn Chen says agent frameworks find the API server too slow for actors that want to resume in milliseconds, and that she told the Agent Substrate team to move fast and learn from users.
    3. 14:42 Tim Allclair says agent frameworks that do everything inside one container become opaque to Kubernetes and miss its observability, auditing, policy and identity.
    4. 16:46 Tim Allclair explains dynamic containers: injecting a container into a running pod with pre-allocated pod-level resources, which he says takes a few hundred milliseconds.
    5. 31:27 Asked when dynamic containers will be ready, Tim Allclair says the plan is alpha in 1.38, beta in 1.39 and GA in 1.40, and that he would not be surprised by a delay.

    Spark on Kubernetes: The Gap Between “It Works” and “It Works Well”

    Damon Cortesi

    Damon Cortesi says Airbnb finished moving its Spark fleet from Hadoop to Kubernetes two weeks before the talk and goes layer by layer through what failed on the way: the control plane, scheduling, nodes, disks, networking, Spark itself and observability. He says most of the 34 changes were small config edits or backported patches that were hard to find, and sums up the lesson as check your config, stress test, repeat.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Data, storage and stateKubernetes internals and runtimesObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you run or plan to run Apache Spark on Kubernetes at scale and want a list of concrete failure points and the fixes one team applied.

    1. 1:23 Cortesi says Airbnb hit 100% two weeks earlier, shutting down its last legacy Hadoop cluster, with a fleet of about 588,000 daily Spark jobs.
    2. 4:55 On the control plane, he says dropping the events TTL from one hour to 10 minutes cut etcd P99 latency from half a second to under 25 milliseconds and the object count from 2 million to 400,000.
    3. 9:06 He describes two Apache YuniKorn problems, one asking for far more capacity than needed because of a Go bug and one asking for too little under FIFO sorting, and says KWOK let him test YuniKorn at scale without provisioning real capacity.
    4. 12:35 He says a Node Problem Detector log rotation bug marked healthy nodes as dead, so that about 74 nodes were removed in 24 hours, one of four node-health bugs they found.
    5. 16:28 He says unique pod labels pushed Cilium past its 65,000 identity limit at their pod creation rates, and the fix was basing identities on a short allow list of labels.

    The Future of Kubernetes Eviction and Node Lifecycle

    Lucy Sweet, Ryan Hallisey, Filip Křepinský

    Three members of the Node Lifecycle Working Group describe two efforts: Specialized Lifecycle Management, which adds shared lifecycle conditions to nodes, and a declarative EvictionRequest API meant to replace one-shot API-initiated eviction. Filip Křepinský explains the requester and responder model and runs a live demo, and Lucy Sweet says Anthropic already uses eviction requests in production.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Kubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you drain or upgrade nodes under stateful or long-running workloads and want to see how eviction is meant to become declarative and observable.

    1. 3:52 Ryan Hallisey introduces node lifecycle conditions such as drain in progress and maintenance in progress, which he says merged as alpha in Kubernetes 1.37 to replace ad hoc node labels.
    2. 5:08 He names the open problem: anyone with permission can write these conditions, so two drains with different options can conflict, and the group is considering a new API to declare intent and serialize writers.
    3. 8:37 Lucy Sweet calls API-initiated eviction a point-and-shoot gun with a one-bit answer that cannot be observed until it happens, and says users build admission webhooks on top of it.
    4. 14:47 Filip Křepinský explains the responder contract: each responder reports a heartbeat within a deadline now set at 20 minutes, and one that stalls is skipped, ending with a fallback that calls the eviction API and respects PDBs.
    5. 25:38 Lucy Sweet says Anthropic uses the eviction request API in production and has issued millions of eviction requests, and that other companies have told the working group they use or plan to use it.

    When Training Dies at 3 AM: Correlating Kubernetes Events with AI-Framework Failures

    Wenjia Zhang, Yuchen Zhou

    Wenjia Zhang and Yuchen Zhou of Google, standing in for the work's main contributor Richa Banker, explain why a hardware fault under a distributed training job is hard to trace back from Kubernetes events hours later. They describe the pipeline they say is merged upstream in Ray and KubeRay, which joins events to pods and parent jobs while the pod still exists, shows them in a Platform Events tab in the Ray dashboard and exports them with Ray's own events.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Observability and reliabilityAccelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: ML engineers on your platform ask whether their code or the infrastructure killed a training job and you cannot answer from Kubernetes events the next morning.

    1. 3:39 Zhang walks through a 512-GPU training run where a GPU fault at 3:14 a.m. becomes, seven seconds later, a Python exception reported by Ray as an application failure, and by 9 a.m. kubectl get events shows nothing.
    2. 8:52 She names three obstacles: events expire after a one-hour TTL, or five minutes in a large cluster with heavy churn, they carry no labels or owner references, and the pod needed for the join may already be garbage collected.
    3. 15:43 Zhou describes two watches in the Ray head: one keeps an in-memory list of the cluster's live pods by label, the other checks namespace pod events against that list, with events kept in a fixed buffer of 1,000 keyed by event UID.
    4. 17:19 He says platform events are wrapped in the standard Ray event format and exported asynchronously to a log file or HTTP endpoint, so they stay searchable in a logging backend after the cluster and the Kubernetes events are gone.
    5. 20:59 He explains that node events land in the default or kube-system namespace where a Ray head cannot see them, so the KubeRay operator watches node warnings and re-emits one event per affected RayCluster into the team's namespace.
