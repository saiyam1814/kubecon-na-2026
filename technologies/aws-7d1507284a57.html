    Agentic AI: Let’s Build a Reference Architecture, Live? Or at Least Try!

    Ricardo Rocha

    Ricardo Rocha invites practitioners to sketch a cloud-native reference architecture for agentic AI from their own deployments. The group maps layers for gateway and inference, then discusses caller identity, privacy, and why an agent's lifetime affects how it should be run.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Birds of a Feather

    AI agents and automationPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you are planning an agent platform and want to compare architecture choices with other practitioners.

    1. 6:36 Rocha shows the draft architecture and places the gateway at the entry point, LangChain around orchestration, and Langfuse in the observability layer.
    2. 10:11 An attendee says caller identity can be lost on the way to the model and suggests preserving it across the chain for attribution and tracing, mentioning SPIFFE and SPIRE.
    3. 13:06 An attendee describes identifying and tokenizing sensitive fields before sending requests to a third-party inference engine, even if the requests are already signed.
    4. 22:16 An attendee contrasts one-shot agents that are seldom reused with long-running jobs, and Rocha adds that distinction to the architecture discussion about checkpointing and resuming work.
    5. 28:34 Rocha asks teams to contribute reference architectures and says submissions should include what worked, what did not, and the custom integration work they needed.

    Free Tokens for Everyone

    William Morgan

    William Morgan says running open-weight models on Kubernetes shifts inference costs from buying tokens to operating GPU capacity. He presents Kinference as a layer for defining tenant policy, tracking usage, and routing requests to local or external backends.

    30 min slot · Beginner · Session

    AI inference and serving

    Watch this if: You run a Kubernetes platform and need to share inference capacity across teams without giving up visibility or control.

    1. 12:01 Morgan explains that choosing an open-weight model variant depends on available memory, context length, and expected concurrency, not just the model family.
    2. 14:39 Morgan frames shared GPU inference as a platform problem: teams want self-service and different levels of service while operators must keep finite capacity reliable.
    3. 18:12 Morgan describes putting a traffic enforcement gateway in front of model servers to handle multiple models and tenants with explicit policy.
    4. 21:01 Morgan says the gateway can export Prometheus-compatible metrics for existing dashboards and break out performance by model and tenant.
    5. 23:47 Morgan walks through policies that can choose a model, reject a request, or send it to an external provider using prompt, GPU, and tenant signals.

    Is Platform Engineering and the Platform Maturity Model Still Relevant in the Agentic AI Era?

    Chris Plank, Corey McGalliard

    Chris and Corey introduce the CNCF Platform Engineering Maturity Model as a map rather than a pass-or-fail assessment and describe community updates such as plainer language, translations, a self-assessment tool, and worked examples. They argue that agentic AI makes platform maturity more relevant because proofs of concept still need production ownership, guardrails, customer feedback, and redesign for machine-speed scale.

    25 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automationPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a practitioner walkthrough of applying the CNCF Platform Engineering Maturity Model to AI-era platforms.

    1. 5:15 The speakers describe the maturity model's five aspects of investment, adoption, interfaces, operations, and measurement across four levels, framing it as a map rather than a pass-or-fail assessment.
    2. 7:51 Chris uses the investment aspect to argue that many AI rollouts remain provisional projects focused on training and adoption numbers without considering day-two operation or product ownership.
    3. 11:05 The speakers say the updated model includes a community-contributed self-assessment tool whose questions can reveal concrete gaps in customer feedback and platform practice.
    4. 13:20 The speakers argue that AI proofs of concept encounter the maturity question when teams ask how they will run in production, who will own them, and how day-two maintenance will work.
    5. 20:29 The speakers argue that AI multiplies existing good and bad platform behavior, while systems designed for human-scale interaction must be redesigned for agents operating continuously and at much higher concurrency.

    Many Clusters, One GPU Pool: Locality-Aware Scheduling and Data Delivery With Kueue and Dragonfly

    Yiqing Wang, Adam Buran

    Yiqing Wang and Adam Buran describe how Roblox shares GPU capacity across clusters using Kueue while leaving each cluster to check its own capacity. They explain how a locality-aware dispatcher chooses clusters and how Dragonfly caches training data and checkpoints near the jobs that use them.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingData, storage and state

    Watch this if: You operate GPU clusters in more than one location and want to share capacity without repeatedly pulling the same datasets across regions.

    1. 6:55 The speakers describe two levels of MultiKueue admission: the hub checks team quota, then the candidate clusters check node capacity and the first to admit the job runs it with KubeRay.
    2. 8:15 The speakers explain how the dispatcher first filters for requirements such as GPU type and full-job fit, then prefers the data region for a limited time before considering other regions.
    3. 9:19 The speakers explain how Roblox separates pool-wide guaranteed quota from cluster-level borrowing and uses preemption to reclaim borrowed GPUs when a guaranteed job arrives.
    4. 14:51 The speakers describe Dragonfly looking for file pieces in the local cache, other peers, and seed peers before falling back to Amazon S3.
    5. 15:55 The speakers report that half of the data served came from node-local cache and another 43 percent from peers in the same cluster, leaving 7 percent served from Amazon S3.

    Platform as a Product in a 300-Year-Old Bank

    Abby Bangser, Joel King

    Abby frames platform engineering as a shift toward self-service with guardrails and connects it to the CNCF white papers, maturity model, and an emerging marketplace model. Joel describes how NatWest used product thinking, Kubernetes CRDs, GitOps reconciliation, and clear ownership boundaries to cut EKS cluster provisioning from eight to twelve weeks to 35 minutes.

    25 min slot · Intermediate · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a regulated-enterprise case study connecting platform-as-product theory to Kubernetes and GitOps implementation plus measured outcomes.

    1. 3:43 Joel says reuse failed when each application team copied and modified patterns, producing more than 135 variants, high cognitive load, configuration drift, and an eight-to-twelve-week path to a usable EKS cluster.
    2. 8:34 Joel says NatWest adopted platform as a product by exposing curated products through an abstracted API, using Git as the source of truth, reconciliation loops, and fleet management rather than team-by-team manual change.
    3. 11:13 Joel explains that Kratix provides the product API through Kubernetes CRDs, after which pipelines write validated desired state to Git for downstream reconciliation.
    4. 19:55 Joel says the automated EKS product reduced provisioning from eight to twelve weeks to 35 minutes while including the AWS account, networking connectivity, guardrails, and self-service request path.
    5. 23:30 Abby closes by arguing that platform teams must optimize for outcomes over outputs, composing contributions into products that give users self-service access rather than merely automating infrastructure.

    Platform-First AI Agent Security: Zero-Trust Patterns for Kubernetes

    Carlos Santana

    Carlos Santana argues that a Kubernetes platform should treat agent-generated code as untrusted and provision isolation, credentials, and network policy separately. He describes using kro to compose those controls behind a platform API and shows captures of an Agent Sandbox warm pool assigning a ready pod.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automationSecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you need to give teams a Kubernetes API for agents without giving those agents unrestricted access.

    1. 5:25 Santana describes SPIFFE and SPIRE as a way to identify an agent by URI and associate that identity with a credential across different environments.
    2. 12:14 Santana shows a kro ResourceGraphDefinition and explains how a declarative resource graph can give platform users a custom API without requiring the platform team to write a Go controller.
    3. 19:05 Santana compares gVisor, Kata Containers, and AWS Nitro Sandboxes as different isolation choices for running untrusted agent code.
    4. 21:27 Santana shows captures of a two-pod Agent Sandbox warm pool and says applying a claim assigned a ready pod instead of waiting for a fresh sandbox to start.
    5. 25:48 Santana describes a credential proxy that attaches a credential to an outbound request without handing the underlying secret to the agent, and gives an Agent Substrate example.

    The Easiest Path Wins: Earning Platform Adoption with DDD and Product Thinking

    Fabrizio Lazzaretti

    Fabrizio Lazzaretti of Wavestone argues that mandates make platforms serve everyone and no one, and lays out four steps for earning adoption instead. He combines a product canvas, domain-driven design techniques, Kotter's change steps and implementation advice such as offering a GUI and WebAssembly extensions.

    25 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you are planning a new internal platform and want product canvas, DDD and change-management steps to earn adoption rather than mandate it.

    1. 1:41 Lazzaretti says mandates push a platform to serve everybody and turn the goal into registering more users instead of checking whether they are satisfied.
    2. 3:23 For the why, he suggests a product canvas over a long business plan: the label pitch, goals, key metrics, a specific target user group (never everybody) and how the platform is funded.
    3. 7:37 He brings in domain-driven design: domain storytelling with a business expert to agree a shared language, then event storming to find bounded contexts and where the software can be split.
    4. 14:29 On the human side he follows Kotter's eight steps for change, including a guiding coalition with a management sponsor and short-term wins from a lighthouse project.
    5. 17:03 For implementation, he says not to underestimate a GUI for getting new users started, and suggests server-side WebAssembly extensions as a sandbox where others can add features in their own language.
