    Cloud Native: Built by Humans, Engineered to Evolve

    Jeffrey Sica

    Jeffrey Sica argues that cloud native can adapt to AI because the people who maintain its projects keep changing how they work without abandoning human review. He connects agent workloads to familiar distributed systems practices such as limiting blast radius, rolling back quickly, and sandboxing untrusted code.

    10 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    Community and open source adoptionKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you want to connect AI agent workloads to the reliability practices and contribution norms cloud native teams already use.

    1. 2:55 Sica asks the audience to see the people who keep showing up behind each logo in the cloud native landscape.
    2. 4:25 Sica says maintainers responded to AI-generated contributions by writing policies for what a good contribution looks like and what they expect the submitting human to review.
    3. 5:37 Sica uses the term agent speed for the number of machine actions between human decisions and argues that the change matters for software architecture.
    4. 6:30 Sica points to small blast radii, fast iteration and rollback, sandboxing, and APIs as reasons cloud native systems can adapt to agent workloads.
    5. 7:59 Sica argues that Kubernetes fits training and inference because they are distributed systems problems, not because Kubernetes was originally built for AI.

    From Vibe to Live: Building for the Real World

    David Biel, Trey Caliva

    David Biel, who says he has no engineering background, and cloud security engineer Trey Caliva tell how David vibe-coded his family dental cabinet company's website and back-office tools and got them to production on managed services. Trey argues that the advice he gave in person should live in the AI's context as skills, hooks and MCP server permissions, so that platform teams can guide the many non-engineers he says are already building this way.

    30 min slot · Beginner · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experienceAI agents and automationDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: non-engineers in your company are shipping AI-built apps and you want ideas for guiding them.

    1. 2:22 David says he got the work done with a $200 Claude subscription, compared with the $5,000 to $10,000 his family was quoted for a website and brand about ten years earlier.
    2. 5:28 Trey says David's app runs on Firebase Hosting, Cloud Functions, Firestore and Firebase Auth, chosen so there are no servers to patch or clusters to run.
    3. 7:49 Trey lists what first-time builders may miss, such as open database rules, unauthenticated endpoints and pasting an API key into the chat.
    4. 10:16 Trey proposes putting guardrails in the AI's context through skills, hooks and MCP server access, with skills moving from ad hoc to a shared repo to a governed catalog.
    5. 13:47 Trey says skills advise and hooks enforce, showing a simplified hook that runs a script on each prompt to catch pasted secrets, plus deny rules that block deploying from a laptop.

    Salesforce Runs on Heroku: How We Empower Every Builder at Cloud Native Scale

    Brandon Schoen, Shiva Nimmagadda

    Shiva Nimmagadda describes what Salesforce needs from Heroku so employees with different levels of technical experience can deploy internal apps. Brandon Schoen explains how Heroku uses Kubernetes, Cloud Native Buildpacks, and OpenTelemetry to handle orchestration, base image updates, and observability for those builders.

    3 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    Platform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a concrete example of how an internal app platform can hide deployment, patching, and observability work from builders.

    1. 0:29 Nimmagadda says Salesforce runs more than twenty thousand Heroku apps and gives examples of what sales, operations, and engineering teams build.
    2. 0:59 Nimmagadda lists requirements for taking AI-assisted code to production, including simple deployment, isolation, single sign-on, and visibility into failures.
    3. 1:42 Schoen names three CNCF projects behind Heroku and describes Kubernetes as its orchestration and isolation layer.
    4. 2:26 Schoen explains that standardizing image builds lets Heroku patch base operating systems across its fleet without rebuilding app code.
    5. 2:40 Schoen describes how Heroku collects logs, traces, and metrics and links the routing layer, platform APIs, and runtime to show a request's journey.

    TAG DevEx State of the Union: What We're Shipping and Why It Matters

    Daniel Oh, Julien Semaan, Kevin Dubois, Simon Forster, Erica Hughberg

    Daniel Oh and fellow panelists introduce the CNCF Technical Advisory Group for Developer Experience, explaining its scope, its focus areas and initiatives such as a study of AI-assisted development in CNCF projects and the runtime conditions work. In an open discussion with the audience they talk about how to take part without being a chair or tech lead, how developers can find their way through the CNCF landscape, and where to find the meeting calendar and Slack channel.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experienceCommunity and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you care about developer experience in cloud native projects and want to know what the CNCF's developer experience group works on and how to join in.

    1. 5:10 Daniel Oh says the group does not aim to create new CNCF projects but to work with existing ones on standards, guidelines and white papers, using the gap between platform engineers and developers around Backstage portals as an example.
    2. 9:02 Kevin Dubois describes the State of AI-Assisted Development initiative, which surveyed and interviewed CNCF project maintainers on how they use AI and produced a draft white paper in the TOC repository.
    3. 14:00 Dubois says the group also reviews projects moving to sandbox, incubating or graduated status for their developer experience angle, citing its review of Buildpacks for graduation.
    4. 15:10 Simon Forster describes joining as a platform architect through the runtime conditions initiative, which is about how applications state their intent to platforms, and says contributions of any size were welcomed.
    5. 23:18 Answering a Java developer overwhelmed by choices, Dubois says a use-case map of the landscape is hard to make without favoring projects, and the panel suggests asking maintainers to document how their projects fit together.
