    Compliance Drift on Kubernetes: Building Proof Infrastructure for AI Agents in Regulated Industries

    Saideep Navakoti

    Saideep Navakoti presents ACRA, a Kubernetes proof workflow intended to detect changes in AI-agent behavior and make the resulting investigations auditable. He shows how the demo displays investigation tickets and describes how auditors could query evidence drawn from traces.

    25 min slot · Intermediate · Session

    AI agents and automationSecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: You need to show auditors what an AI agent was approved to do and what it actually did.

    1. 7:55 Navakoti explains that Kyverno checks pod admission and OpenTelemetry records traces, but argues that neither alone tells an operator whether an agent's behavior has drifted.
    2. 9:32 He introduces ACRA's API server, controller, and gate, then explains how its contract evaluator checks agent behavior against a declared baseline.
    3. 12:21 He shows a demo UI that calls the API to display investigation tickets, after describing how a drift event produces evidence from collected traces.
    4. 20:04 He frames drift as a reason to re-approve an agent rather than silently fix it, and walks through how a custom Kubernetes controller reports the agent's state.
    5. 21:32 He describes evidence an auditor can query for a specific task or incident, including a telemetry graph rather than a manually maintained spreadsheet.

    Data-Driven Platform Guardrails: What 16,500 Incidents Tell You to Automate First

    Henrique Santana, Henrique Dalssaso

    Henrique Santana and Henrique Dalssaso walk through the top root causes they found in more than 16,000 Kubernetes incidents, including IP exhaustion, auth failures and node provisioning problems. For each one they suggest platform guardrails to automate, and they explain how to run the same analysis on your own incident data.

    25 min slot · Intermediate · Session

    Observability and reliabilityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a list of common Kubernetes incident root causes and the guardrails these speakers suggest automating for each.

    1. 1:35 The speakers describe their dataset as more than 16,000 incidents across many Kubernetes clusters from January to mid-May, grouped with AI and then reviewed by hand. They say DNS did not make their top 10 root causes.
    2. 3:57 They say about a third of one top pattern was pods with no IP address available, from small or shared subnets, and suggest Cilium multi-pool IPAM and broader Karpenter node pools.
    3. 6:59 For auth failures they separate missing permissions, RBAC that was never bound to the pod, and policies on the target endpoint, and suggest Kyverno policies, projected service account tokens and cert-manager.
    4. 16:20 For node provisioning failures, which they put at almost 15% of incidents, they suggest setting kube-reserved and system-reserved, Cluster API machine health checks and Karpenter node auto repair.
    5. 20:59 To repeat the analysis on your own data, they suggest classifying by what actually failed rather than the symptom, ranking by frequency, and asking whether the platform, the app team or both can fix it.

    From Black-Box to Auditable AI-Grid: A Deterministic Governance Layer with Hybrid Cloud

    Fatih E. Nar, Andrew Hendrickson

    The speakers outline a governance layer for AI recommendations in communication networks that makes the decision process auditable without requiring identical model wording. They describe how versioned evidence, policy gates, and energy measurements could feed into decisions about network operations.

    30 min slot · Beginner · Session

    AI agents and automationSecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you need to explain what evidence and policy allowed an AI-assisted decision in a regulated network.

    1. 4:58 Hendrickson says fixing the input, rule version, and evidence snapshot should make it possible to reconstruct the rules, evidence, and citations behind a decision.
    2. 10:33 Nar distinguishes using AI to improve network operations from using network infrastructure to host new AI workloads.
    3. 14:35 Nar walks through classifying intent, retrieving evidence, constraining reasoning with rules, synthesizing a result, and blocking an action if there is no supporting citation.
    4. 20:19 Nar cites Open Policy Agent and Kyverno for policy and describes using Git to keep track of models, rules, and standards.
    5. 24:19 Nar proposes using Kepler to measure power at host, platform, and workload levels so operators can weigh power use against service commitments.

    Platform as a Product in a 300-Year-Old Bank

    Abby Bangser, Joel King

    Abby frames platform engineering as a shift toward self-service with guardrails and connects it to the CNCF white papers, maturity model, and an emerging marketplace model. Joel describes how NatWest used product thinking, Kubernetes CRDs, GitOps reconciliation, and clear ownership boundaries to cut EKS cluster provisioning from eight to twelve weeks to 35 minutes.

    25 min slot · Intermediate · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a regulated-enterprise case study connecting platform-as-product theory to Kubernetes and GitOps implementation plus measured outcomes.

    1. 3:43 Joel says reuse failed when each application team copied and modified patterns, producing more than 135 variants, high cognitive load, configuration drift, and an eight-to-twelve-week path to a usable EKS cluster.
    2. 8:34 Joel says NatWest adopted platform as a product by exposing curated products through an abstracted API, using Git as the source of truth, reconciliation loops, and fleet management rather than team-by-team manual change.
    3. 11:13 Joel explains that Kratix provides the product API through Kubernetes CRDs, after which pipelines write validated desired state to Git for downstream reconciliation.
    4. 19:55 Joel says the automated EKS product reduced provisioning from eight to twelve weeks to 35 minutes while including the AWS account, networking connectivity, guardrails, and self-service request path.
    5. 23:30 Abby closes by arguing that platform teams must optimize for outcomes over outputs, composing contributions into products that give users self-service access rather than merely automating infrastructure.

    Arthur Vardevanyan and Jennifer Weir describe how Ford checks GitOps changes from engineers, dependency automation, and coding agents. They explain how a Go-based validator traces affected overlays, checks Kubernetes semantics, and reports actionable findings in a single pull request comment.

    Thomas Schuetz, Dr. Constanze Roedig

    The Identity Crisis: Who is the AI Agent in Your Cluster?

    Abdel Sghiouar

    Abdel Sghiouar argues that agents sharing a Kubernetes service account are hard to distinguish when something goes wrong. He proposes short-lived SPIFFE identities, gateways for authorization, and sandboxing, with humans setting policy instead of approving every action.

    10 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    AI agents and automationSecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you need to separate agent identity, authorization, and runtime isolation when designing a Kubernetes platform.

    1. 1:23 Sghiouar says it is hard to predict which tools or endpoints an agent will call, and argues that a shared Kubernetes service account makes it hard to trace an incident to a particular replica.
    2. 2:40 Sghiouar distinguishes agents acting for a human from agents triggered autonomously, and argues the latter need a unique, short-lived identity tied to their task.
    3. 3:29 Sghiouar proposes SPIFFE and SPIRE for giving agents short-lived identities at runtime, potentially scoped to a replica or session.
    4. 4:15 Sghiouar says identity does not answer authorization and discusses using a gateway to evaluate what an agent wants to do before it accesses an external resource.
    5. 8:09 Sghiouar urges platform engineers to move from human approval of each action to human-defined governance that sets boundaries for what agents can do.

    Your CI's Mistaken Identity

    Andrew McNamara, Maia Iyer

    Andrew McNamara and Maia Iyer say a namespace and service account can tell you where a CI task runs without telling you what it should be allowed to do. They describe pairing per-task workload identities with admission-time policy and using those identities to authorize access and sign attestations.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Delivery, GitOps and release safetySecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: You want to avoid giving every task in a Kubernetes CI pipeline the same privileges.

    1. 4:30 The speakers explain that SPIRE can use more than namespace and service account when assigning workload identities, so CI pods in the same namespace can have different roles.
    2. 9:19 The speakers explain that a Tekton bundle's digest changes when its task definition changes, and show where Sigstore signatures and attestations can be stored.
    3. 14:14 The speakers describe using Kyverno admission policy to require extra verification before a task can get a production identity.
    4. 16:57 In the live demo, the speakers show that an unsigned task from an untrusted catalog was blocked before a pod was scheduled.
    5. 22:21 The speakers explain how task-scoped identities let a verification policy check not just for an attestation, but for the specific CI task expected to produce it.
