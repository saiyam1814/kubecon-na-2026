    A Decade of AI on Kubernetes: Kubeflow’s Journey to CNCF Graduation

    Valentina Rodriguez Sosa, Alexander Perlman, Christian Heusel, Shivay Lamba, David Aronchick

    Kubeflow co-founder David Aronchick recounts how the project started, and contributors describe its components, governance and what they say CNCF graduation required. The speakers then outline planned work, including Kubeflow Pipelines 3.0 changes such as removing the ML Metadata service, a unified Python SDK, trainer and notebook features, an MCP server and experimental Helm charts for the community distribution.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Community and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you run or contribute to Kubeflow and want the origin story, what graduation involved, and the maintainers' stated plans for Pipelines 3.0, the SDK and agent support.

    1. 2:30 David Aronchick says the first Kubeflow release was deliberately small, with only a Jupyter notebook, a TensorFlow CRD and what later became KServe, and that pipelines came about six months later.
    2. 12:22 Christian Heusel says Kubeflow reached CNCF graduated status in July 2026, and that graduation required maintainers from at least two organizations per sub-project, independent adopters and a security and governance review.
    3. 20:56 Alexander Perlman says Kubeflow Pipelines 3.0 will consolidate the per-step driver pods into one pod per run, roughly halving the pod count, using Argo Workflows executor plugins.
    4. 22:13 He says 3.0 removes the ML Metadata microservice and database and moves execution tracking, artifacts, lineage and caching into the Pipelines API server.
    5. 29:24 Shivay Lamba describes an experimental Kubeflow MCP server that exposes trainer jobs to agents while applying the user's own Kubernetes permissions so the agent stays within bounds.

    Architecture Without Gatekeepers: Decoupling for Neutrality

    Kate Obiidykhata

    Kate Obiidykhata of Percona tells how Percona Everest, which had its database operators and monitoring baked into the core, drew almost no outside contributions until it was renamed OpenEverest and donated to the CNCF. She says a second change, shrinking the core and moving everything else into providers and plugins, was followed by more supported technologies and new contributors.

    5 min slot · Level not listed · Lightning Talk

    Community and open source adoptionData, storage and state

    Watch this if: you maintain an open source project started inside one company and want to hear what one team changed to attract outside contributors.

    1. 1:04 Obiidykhata says the project started under the Percona brand with MySQL, PostgreSQL and MongoDB support based on Percona's own operators, plus monitoring based on Percona Monitoring and Management.
    2. 1:49 She says all of those elements were baked into the core and the project had almost zero traction from the open source community because it was hard to contribute to and change.
    3. 2:31 She describes renaming Percona Everest to OpenEverest and donating it as a CNCF sandbox project, saying the governance protects the license and core, and that GitHub stars grew right after the move in early 2026.
    4. 3:32 She explains the architecture change: a very small core with providers for adding new technologies through an SDK and plugins for extending things like the UI, and says adding a database now takes days instead of weeks or months.
    5. 4:35 She reports that the community added technologies such as ClickHouse and KServe, that the project went from three technologies to 12, and that 89 new contributors had merged pull requests.

    Beyond Model Serving: Running Production LLMs on Kubernetes with KServe

    Johnu George, Jooho Lee

    The two KServe maintainers explain how the project added an LLMInferenceService API that builds on llm-d, vLLM and the Gateway API Inference Extension rather than a new inference engine. They then go through production patterns they say KServe addresses: cache-aware routing, streaming timeouts, split prefill and decode, multi-node serving, autoscaling signals, cold starts, model updates and LoRA adapters.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    AI inference and servingAccelerators and workload schedulingNetworking and connectivity

    Watch this if: you serve LLMs on Kubernetes and want the KServe maintainers' checklist of routing, scaling and cold-start issues to plan for.

    1. 4:07 Jooho Lee describes the LLMInferenceService API, in which he says a workload controller manages single-node, multi-node and separate prefill deployments while a route controller manages the scheduler and Gateway API resources.
    2. 8:44 Jooho follows a request from the gateway to the endpoint picker, which he says can weigh prefix cache match, queue depth and KV cache usage instead of treating every healthy replica as equal.
    3. 12:47 Johnu George warns that with streaming, timeouts at each hop must exceed generation time, because he says client retries otherwise pile more load on the GPUs.
    4. 16:15 Johnu argues that GPU utilization is a misleading autoscaling signal for LLMs and says the number of requests waiting in the inference runtime's queue is a better one.
    5. 17:30 Johnu describes cold-start work with a local model cache and container snapshot and restore, and says they are seeing startup drop from minutes to a few seconds.

    Composing Inference on Kubernetes: Gateway, Schedule, Serve, Observe

    Karena Angell, Alex Chircop

    Karena Angell and Alex Chircop argue that production inference on Kubernetes is too often built as a closed vertical stack, and present gateway, scheduling, serving and observability as separate layers joined by explicit interfaces. They go through six kinds of composability, show recorded lab runs of several traffic shapes and component swaps, and close with questions for teams to ask of their own clusters.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and servingNetworking and connectivityObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you are building a shared inference platform on Kubernetes and want a way to decide which layer owns routing, admission, serving and tracing so that parts can be swapped later.

    1. 3:15 Angell says the biggest trap they see in production is building separate silos for chat, RAG and agents, which duplicates routing, scheduling and telemetry for what she describes as one nested workload.
    2. 14:16 Chircop separates admission from routing: he says a scheduler such as Kueue decides once whether pods can exist under quota and fairness rules, while the endpoint picker chooses which replica serves each prompt, using signals such as queue depth and a warm KV cache.
    3. 17:02 Chircop says the OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions give components a shared vocabulary for spans and metrics, and W3C trace context propagation joins them into a single trace across the hops.
    4. 18:45 Angell cites Hugging Face moving Text Generation Inference into maintenance mode, and says teams that kept serving behind a gateway API contract could swap engines quickly while others had to rework their client apps.
    5. 27:13 Chircop narrates recorded lab runs in which gateways are swapped while routing live traffic, llm-d's endpoint picker is replaced by the reference picker for the Gateway API Inference Extension, and the scheduler is changed between Kueue, Volcano and KAI Scheduler.

    Reduce vLLM Pod Cold Starts with KServe Kernel Caching

    Jooho lee, Vishwa Hiren Gandhi

    The speakers explain how GPU kernel compilation can slow vLLM startup even when model weights are already cached. They describe KServe's approach for sharing signed kernel caches across pods and report what changed in their benchmark.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and serving

    Watch this if: Your vLLM replicas scale slowly even after you have cached the model weights.

    1. 4:33 The speakers break down a baseline vLLM startup of about 250 seconds and say the compilation step accounts for roughly 35 seconds, excluding image pulls and model downloads.
    2. 5:38 The speakers explain why a new replica can compile kernels again even after another pod has compiled them: the new pod does not inherit the previous pod's local cache.
    3. 10:31 The speakers propose packaging compiled kernels as a signed OCI artifact in a registry so another node can verify the signer before making the cache available.
    4. 13:52 The speakers distinguish a workload fingerprint that identifies where a cache came from from a compatibility fingerprint that checks whether compilation settings match.
    5. 18:24 In one benchmark, the speakers report that reusing the cache cut startup from 160 to 110 seconds and compilation from about 35 seconds to just over one second.

    Your Peers Are Struggling With LLM Inference on Kubernetes Too. Here's What the Data Says.

    Danielle Cook, Andy Suderman

    Danielle Cook of Akamai and Andy Suderman of Fairwinds walk through an Akamai-commissioned survey of 200 practitioners running AI in production, along with CNCF, Forrester and Cast AI figures, to show where teams say inference on Kubernetes is hard. They group the problems into scheduling, scaling, routing, placement and ownership, and close with five steps they recommend, from starting on an AI-conformant cluster to setting a time-to-first-token SLO.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and servingObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you run LLM inference on Kubernetes and want survey numbers to compare your latency targets, GPU utilization and routing setup against.

    1. 4:33 Cook presents the survey's performance expectations: 82% of respondents need an end-to-end response within 500 milliseconds, 64% within 250, and nearly half expect to roll back within 15 minutes when inference degrades.
    2. 8:19 Suderman cites a Cast AI figure of 5% average GPU utilization across Kubernetes clusters and suggests Dynamic Resource Allocation, Kueue and right-sized requests as practical responses.
    3. 10:48 Suderman argues against autoscaling inference on CPU and recommends scaling on latency signals such as time to first token, using the metrics vLLM exposes with KEDA.
    4. 12:01 Cook explains why round-robin load balancing fits LLM replicas poorly, since each has its own KV cache, and relays an example where an inference-aware gateway doubled prefix cache hits from 35% to 70%.
    5. 22:15 The speakers give their five steps: start from an AI-conformant cluster, cut model startup time, autoscale on inference signals, put an inference-aware gateway in front, and set a p95 time-to-first-token SLO.
