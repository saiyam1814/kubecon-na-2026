    Anthropic’s Kubernetes Scheduler for Frontier AI: What Worked, What Broke

    Decheng Dai

    Decheng Dai describes why Anthropic wrote its own Kubernetes scheduler for accelerator workloads, covering gang scheduling, the topologies of Google TPUs and AWS Trainium, and a tree model of data center networks. He walks through the design choices he says keep the scheduling loop fast on very large clusters, then discusses soft slicing, defragmentation and the lack of transactional binding in the API server.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingKubernetes internals and runtimesNetworking and connectivity

    Watch this if: you schedule large training or inference jobs on Kubernetes and want to hear how one team handles gang and topology constraints at very large cluster sizes.

    1. 1:27 Dai frames the problem as gang scheduling plus topology-aware scheduling and describes two gang modes: a full gang where every pod must run, and replicas that are each a gang but fail independently.
    2. 6:47 He says the team models every cluster as a tree of network domains whose leaves are TPU slices, Trainium servers or GPU nodes, and built a drop-in replacement scheduler that jobs select by scheduler name.
    3. 8:15 He describes the control loop: take a snapshot of the cluster, plan all pending jobs from highest to lowest priority, and schedule jobs rather than pods, with the loop kept within 10 seconds on their largest cluster.
    4. 16:48 He says scoring nodes only once for jobs that are identical from a scheduling perspective is by far the largest scaling factor in their system.
    5. 23:55 As something that does not work well, Dai points to gang binding without transactions in the API server: some of the bind calls can fail, forcing eviction of the pods already bound.

    Battle-tested Autoscaling Paradigms with KEDA

    Jan Wozniak, Christian Melendez

    Jan Wozniak and Christian Melendez run a live audience quiz backed by a small CPU-hosted model and use it to show KEDA scaling the pods while Karpenter scales the nodes. They walk through their recommendations on metric freshness, fallbacks between metric sources, spare node capacity through capacity buffers, and conservative scale-down.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you autoscale workloads on Kubernetes and want practical guidance on metric sources, fallbacks, node headroom and scale-down settings with KEDA and Karpenter.

    1. 3:45 Wozniak groups scaling metrics into reactive ones such as CPU and memory, proactive ones such as load balancer and queue metrics, and predictive ones, and says reactive scaling tends to run behind the load.
    2. 10:13 He says Prometheus can keep working for smaller environments, and suggests KEDA's OpenTelemetry scaler, which has metrics pushed to it, for teams that want to remove the scrape delay or that run a very large number of scaled objects.
    3. 12:58 He describes KEDA scaling modifiers and their dynamic fallback operator, then has the OpenTelemetry source killed live to show scaling continue on the Prometheus copy of the metric.
    4. 16:56 Melendez explains Karpenter's capacity buffers, where a pod template and a buffer object act as virtual pods that keep headroom ready, and compares this with the older pattern of low-priority balloon pods.
    5. 26:41 For scale-down, Melendez advises a stabilization window longer than the time an application needs to start on a fresh node, plus a delay on Karpenter consolidation, with GPU nodes removed only when empty.

    Binary Version Is Not Behavior: Skip-Level Kubernetes Control Plane Upgrades With KEP-4330

    Sameer Marulkar

    Sameer Marulkar of LinkedIn's compute infrastructure team describes how his team moved the control planes of more than 250 on-prem clusters from Kubernetes 1.31 to 1.34 in one binary rollout by pinning the emulated version from KEP-4330 to 1.31 and promoting it later. He says this cut the upgrade from about 40 business days to under seven, and he lists the traps they hit and the metrics they used to verify what behavior was live.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Delivery, GitOps and release safetyKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run your own Kubernetes control planes, are several minor versions behind, and want an upgrade path with a rollback step.

    1. 1:35 Marulkar states the result: instead of six fleet rollouts going one minor at a time, they shipped the 1.34 binary while it still behaved like 1.31, then did a single promotion, taking about seven business days instead of 40.
    2. 6:24 He explains the emulated version flag from KEP-4330: roll out the new binary with the flag pinned to the release you run today as the deploy step, then raise it later as the release step.
    3. 9:30 He lists the traps: the new code still needs a canary, a feature gate removed by 1.34 stops the binary from starting so gates must be pruned first, and kubelet skew is measured against the emulated version.
    4. 11:53 To verify emulation is on he says not to trust the build info, which only reports the binary, and to check the version info metric and the feature enabled metric instead.
    5. 15:51 At promotion he warns of two things that are easy to miss: removed APIs keep being served during the soak and break only when the emulated version is raised, and alpha features are not covered by emulation.

    Death by a Thousand Patches: How Routine Kubernetes Operations Can Turn Healthy Clusters Read-Only

    Sharik Pahwa, Hardeep Singh Tiwana

    Sharik Pahwa and Hardeep Singh Tiwana demonstrate a healthy cluster going read-only after a storm of status patches fills a deliberately small etcd quota, and then trace the triggers and the failure back through etcd's multi-version storage. They walk through recovery with compaction and defragmentation and suggest alerts, admission policies and scheduled defragmentation to prevent a repeat.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Observability and reliabilityKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run Kubernetes control planes or write controllers and want to understand how frequent status and annotation writes can fill etcd, and how to recover and guard against it.

    1. 3:22 In the demo, Pahwa says a script creates 80 pod replicas and sends a storm of status patches, each of which makes etcd store a full copy of the object, until the cluster with a 100 MB etcd quota turns read-only while the pods keep running.
    2. 4:43 Pahwa lists triggers that he says compound: scheduler retries on unschedulable pods, crash-looping containers, node lease renewals, controllers that write status without a change, annotations used as a message channel, and jobs without TTLs.
    3. 10:19 He argues that a read-only cluster is worse than an outage because pod status still shows Running and alarms stay green, and failures then arrive as a cascade of alarms that hides the etcd cause.
    4. 18:03 Tiwana explains that compaction only marks old revisions as deleted and leaves the file size unchanged, so space comes back only after defragmentation, which he advises running on one etcd member at a time because it pauses that member.
    5. 24:04 As a preventive guardrail, Tiwana shows a validating admission policy that limits how large an annotation or status update can be, and a test in which the oversized write is rejected.

    Saturated by Design - Maximizing Cluster Allocation in On-Premises Kubernetes

    Dakota Sullivan, Isaac Wilson

    The speakers describe how The Trade Desk raised allocation in its fixed-size on-premises Kubernetes clusters from about 20% with default scheduler settings to what they say is a consistent 97%. They walk through four problems, node scoring, deployment overscaling, fragmentation and right-sizing, and the scoring change, two custom scheduler plugins and a simulation-driven controller they built for them.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: You run fixed-capacity or on-premises Kubernetes clusters with large pods and topology constraints and want to see how one team pushed allocation toward full.

    1. 4:14 Isaac Wilson says switching node scoring from least allocated to most allocated was the easiest and most significant change, taking utilization from about 20% to 85%.
    2. 6:30 Dakota Sullivan describes Plimsoll, a custom scheduler plugin that holds back extra pods created during rollouts and deletions until terminating pods are gone, which he says also works with out-of-tree controllers such as Argo Rollouts.
    3. 11:24 Wilson describes ResourceShuffler, a second scheduler plugin that moves pods without topology spread constraints to make room for constrained pending pods while respecting PodDisruptionBudgets, bringing utilization to 97% by their account.
    4. 15:09 Sullivan explains modeling workloads elastically, which he compares to CSS flexbox: each has a basis scale plus a min and max, so all workloads shrink or grow fairly when capacity changes.
    5. 18:24 He introduces Bento, a controller that snapshots the cluster, runs simulations with the real Kubernetes scheduler to find ideal scales and moves workloads part way toward them on each loop.

    SIG Autoscaling Project Updates

    Jack Francis, Jason Deal, Heba Elayoty, Kuba Tużnik

    Jack Francis, Jason Deal, Heba Elayoty and Kuba Tużnik give updates on Kubernetes SIG Autoscaling projects, including Karpenter's DRA support, balanced consolidation and node repair, the capacity buffer and capacity quota APIs, and the split of Cluster Autoscaler into a core plus separate provider projects. Elayoty then explains what workload-aware scheduling needs from autoscalers, and Tużnik presents scheduler-driven autoscaling, a proposed redesign in which the scheduler requests nodes from the autoscaler.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run Karpenter or Cluster Autoscaler and want the latest feature updates plus the proposed redesign of how autoscalers work with the scheduler.

    1. 3:28 Jason Deal says Karpenter added DRA support in its scheduling simulation in v1.14, covering structured parameters, consumable capacity, partitionable devices and prioritized alternatives, with provider implementations in the AWS provider and EKS Auto Mode.
    2. 6:11 He introduces balanced consolidation, which weighs the share of pods disrupted against the share of cost saved and only acts when the score passes a threshold.
    3. 10:08 Jack Francis says Cluster Autoscaler is being split into a core repository and separate provider projects, and that the SIG will keep supporting releases in the older all-in-one model for a transition period.
    4. 19:50 Kuba Tużnik describes the split brain problem: the autoscaler has to predict what the scheduler will decide, and when its predictions are off it makes wrong scaling decisions.

    The Latest in GPU, TPU, NIC and Other Device Support - WG Device Management

    John Belamaric, Morten Jæger Torkildsen

    Morten Torkildsen and John Belamaric of Google give the Device Management working group update, recapping why Dynamic Resource Allocation replaced the device plugin's opaque counters and what its four parts are. They cover the features that shipped in Kubernetes 1.37 and those planned for 1.38, go deeper on allocating CPU and memory through DRA and on its integration with workload-aware scheduling, and describe ecosystem adoption and next steps such as preemption and attaching GPUs to running pods.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you manage GPUs or other accelerators in Kubernetes and want to know which DRA features landed in 1.37 and what is planned next.

    1. 3:06 Morten Torkildsen outlines DRA's four parts: the resource slice API for describing devices, the resource claim API for requesting them, the scheduler matching the two, and the kubelet API that works with the DRA driver on the node.
    2. 6:11 He explains the extended resource request feature in 1.37, which lets cluster admins adopt DRA while workloads keep using device-plugin style requests that are matched to DRA devices underneath.
    3. 7:42 He describes device taints, which let a single failing GPU or TPU be tainted instead of the whole node, so workloads on the other devices are not disrupted.
    4. 11:52 John Belamaric explains node allocatable resources with DRA, which lets CPU and memory be selected through DRA so they can be aligned with the NIC and GPU, and tells the scheduler how to account for CPU requested in both the container spec and a resource claim.
    5. 16:30 He describes the DRA integration with workload-aware scheduling, where a resource claim attached to a pod group allocates a shared resource for a new replica and cleans it up when the replica scales down.

    Volcano for the Agentic AI Era: Unified Scheduling Across Training, Inference, and Agents

    Ricardo Aravena, Rainie Li

    Rainie Li explains how Volcano aims to schedule training, inference and agent workloads on one Kubernetes cluster, using a batch scheduler and an agent scheduler that share nodes through a sharding controller, plus multi-level gang scheduling, network topology hypernodes and gang-aware preemption. Ricardo Aravena then covers how workload controllers such as Kthena, KubeRay and Grove map onto Volcano primitives, the project's benchmarking work and the roadmap.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingAI agents and automationAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: you schedule AI training, inference or agent workloads on shared GPU clusters and want to know what Volcano offers for gang and topology-aware placement.

    1. 3:02 Rainie Li says Volcano uses two schedulers, a batch scheduler for coordinated workloads and an agent scheduler for latency-sensitive pods, with a sharding controller assigning candidate nodes to each and moving them as demand changes.
    2. 6:49 She introduces multi-level gang scheduling, in which a pod group contains subgroups with their own completeness requirements, such as a tensor-parallel group or a prefill or decode replica.
    3. 9:56 She explains gang-aware preemption, which treats victims as groups and simulates placement first, so no pod is evicted if the whole incoming gang cannot fit.
    4. 15:12 Ricardo Aravena says KubeRay support is merged upstream and listed for Volcano 1.16, and walks through how its head and worker groups translate into Volcano subgroups.
    5. 18:35 He describes the scheduling benchmark framework shipped with Volcano 1.15, which reports scheduling latency and binding throughput on kind, KWOK or an existing cluster, and notes it says nothing about GPU job speed.
