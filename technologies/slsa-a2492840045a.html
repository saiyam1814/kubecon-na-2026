    Fighting Fire with Fire: Scaling KubeEdge Vulnerability Management in the New AI Era

    Yin Ding

    KubeEdge maintainer Yin Ding gives background on KubeEdge and its Sedna edge AI toolkit, then says AI-assisted scanning has pushed vulnerability reports beyond what the project's few volunteer security maintainers can triage. He walks through a proposed, partly implemented pipeline in which AI agents scout upstream fixes, rank and auto-close reports that do not apply, generate patches and score them before a human review.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Security, identity and policyAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you maintain an open source project with few security volunteers and want to see how one CNCF project plans to use AI agents for vulnerability triage and patching.

    1. 9:05 Yin Ding describes Sedna's joint inference, where a smaller model on the edge node answers first and the request goes to a larger cloud model only when the confidence score is too low.
    2. 12:07 He says security vulnerability reports are accelerating with AI help, and that the project went from seven security issues fixed across 2023 to early 2026 to four CVEs and five other issues fixed in the second half of 2026.
    3. 13:17 He says 27 fixes are waiting for review because the project lacks people to verify and review the patches.
    4. 16:59 He describes a scouting step that uses the cheapest model to check what upstream Kubernetes is already fixing, so KubeEdge can wait for the upstream patch and bump its base version.
    5. 20:48 He says the plan is for four agents to judge each generated patch and produce a confidence score, with low-confidence patches going back to human judgment, and that only the correctness check exists so far.

    I Packaged My Application in a Container Image, and Now What?

    Aurélie Vache

    The speaker takes a small Go API packaged as a container image and walks through supply chain security steps for it, with a live demo for each one. She covers generating an SBOM, scanning for vulnerabilities, publishing a VEX file, signing the image, generating SLSA provenance and automating all of it in a GitHub Action.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Security, identity and policyDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: You can build and push a container image and want to see the next supply chain security steps done live, one command at a time.

    1. 2:18 The speaker starts with the SBOM, an inventory of the components in an image, and says it is no longer nice to have but becoming mandatory.
    2. 10:32 In the demo, a Trivy scan of the image reports 26 vulnerabilities, and the speaker points out that the first one applies to a Go version her application does not use.
    3. 13:22 The speaker introduces VEX as a way to tell users whether a vulnerability is exploitable in the application, and says OpenVEX is her favorite format.
    4. 17:11 After generating a VEX file with govulncheck and fixing its product ID, the speaker scans again with Trivy and she says the filtered result shows zero exploitable ones among the high and critical findings.
    5. 20:32 The speaker generates a key pair in a KMS, signs the image with Cosign and verifies the signature.

    In-Toto: Experiences a Few Million Attestations Later

    Santiago Torres-Arias, James Carnegie, Sivana Hamer, Aditya Sirish A Yelgundhalli

    Santiago Torres-Arias moderates a panel on how in-toto attestations are used in practice, with James Carnegie describing Datadog's internal deployment, Aditya Sirish A Yelgundhalli speaking as a maintainer and a Bloomberg engineer, and Sivana Hamer sharing her research on attestations found in the wild. The panelists discuss making attestations easy to create, a fragmented storage picture, the coming policy framework, how consumers can trust public attestations, and what agent-written code changes.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Panel

    Security, identity and policyDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: you produce or consume software supply chain attestations and want to hear how two companies and a researcher see in-toto adoption, storage and policy.

    1. 8:08 Carnegie says Datadog made attestations easy to create by binding ephemeral keys to OIDC, and that it now collects source attestations for every commit and over 2 million attestations a month.
    2. 9:21 He says Datadog began evaluating policy at deployment time, first in monitoring mode and then enforcing, and now also checks policy before pull requests merge, in CI and at runtime.
    3. 14:46 Hamer says her research found 42 million attestations in the wild as of June across almost 150,000 components, often produced by tooling such as npm provenance or PyPI trusted publishing without users knowing.
    4. 17:00 Yelgundhalli explains that the in-toto policy framework sets requirements such as artifact rules without mandating one policy engine or language, and that verification itself emits an attestation.
    5. 21:33 Carnegie says consumers lack a standard way to learn which attestations a producer should publish and with which keys; Datadog distributes keys and policies internally with TUF, which he rarely sees in open source.

    Signed, Sealed, Denied: Attestation-Driven Admission with Kyverno + CEL at AT&T

    Chad Swenson, Sagar Kundral

    Chad Swenson of AT&T argues that a signature alone is not a deployment approval and describes a platform design where GitHub Actions emits Sigstore-backed attestations and Kyverno's ImageValidatingPolicy checks them against each environment's rules at admission. Sagar Kundral of Nirmata demos the policies on a live cluster and presents Kyverno 1.19 and 1.20 changes, benchmark numbers and advice on exceptions, failure behavior, evidence retention and rollout.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Security, identity and policyDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: you sign images already and want to see how attestations can drive per-environment admission decisions that you can later explain to an auditor.

    1. 3:38 Chad says a valid signature and provenance are useful but neither is a deployment approval, so the platform must still ask whether trusted claims meet the destination's requirements.
    2. 5:15 Chad gives an example where the same image digest is admitted in development on provenance alone but denied in production because it lacks a signed SBOM attestation.
    3. 13:51 Chad says verifying only against GitHub's private Sigstore instance broke down for upstream and vendor images with other trust roots, and credits a fix released in Kyverno 1.19 after he raised it with the lead maintainer.
    4. 17:28 Sagar runs a scripted demo on a live cluster showing verification with a signer workflow identity, an unattested image blocked, a production denial for missing code reviews, a policy exception in dev, and an org ID pin.
    5. 24:38 Sagar presents benchmark numbers from a kind cluster where he says p50 admission latency fell from 990 ms to 96 ms and registry calls from 14 to 3 in Kyverno 1.20.

    Validating Kubernetes Workloads: Checklists and Best Practices

    Nabarun Pal, Maciej Szulik, Liz Rice, Ricardo Aravena

    Organizers of the Kubernetes working group on workload conformance explain its charter: define how a well-behaved workload acts in a cluster, write objective tests for that, and eventually propose a CNCF conformance program. Most of the session is discussion with the audience about what should be in scope, including configuration variants, SBOMs, neutrality of the requirements and upgrade paths.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Birds of a Feather

    Kubernetes internals and runtimesCommunity and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you ship or operate applications on Kubernetes and want a say in what a conformance check for well-behaved workloads should test.

    1. 1:53 Pal sets out the working group's charter: define generic behavior of workloads on Kubernetes, write verifiable tests for it, and build a conformance suite to hand to steering and architecture for vetting, while saying the group is far from that point.
    2. 6:25 Szulik says one goal is for an application vendor to be able to say its workload is a good citizen, for example respecting disruptions and graceful node shutdown, in the way a provider shows a cluster is conformant, with tooling anyone can run on their own apps.
    3. 14:16 An audience member asks for SBOMs with every delivered application; Szulik says the group had planned only must conditions and that he now leans toward adding optional ones, and Pal notes this would mean choosing between formats such as CycloneDX and SPDX.
    4. 18:35 An audience member warns that an earlier operator conformance effort turned into subjective rules such as requiring startup probes, and Szulik answers that he has no solution yet and that finding common ground is why the working group was started.
    5. 27:38 On upgrades, Pal says the group does want to check upgrade readiness, such as flagging use of deprecated Kubernetes APIs, and points to the API server's deprecated API usage metrics as one way to test it.

    Welcome to the Vulnpocalypse!

    Liz Rice, Adrian Mouat, Hannah Foxwell, Andrew Martin

    The panelists say AI-assisted discovery is surfacing more vulnerabilities than security teams can triage using severity labels alone. They discuss using platform context, deployment inventories, and emergency patch paths to decide what to fix first.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Panel

    Security, identity and policy

    Watch this if: You need to prioritize patches when vulnerability reports outnumber the issues your team can fix immediately.

    1. 1:40 The panel says AI-assisted vulnerability discovery has increased the volume of reports while teams have less enriched CVE data to help triage them.
    2. 6:41 A panelist recommends offering security controls as platform capabilities and teaching engineering teams how to use them.
    3. 10:00 The panel discusses keeping a bill of materials and an inventory of what's installed where, so teams can locate affected dependencies when a vulnerability is disclosed.
    4. 18:08 A panelist says teams can keep their normal update process while reserving a faster route for emergencies, depending on their risk tolerance.
    5. 24:55 A panelist says a medium-severity issue in a critical environment can matter more than a higher-severity one with little exposure, and explains why application reachability matters.
