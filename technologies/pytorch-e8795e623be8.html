    From Pending Pod to Ready Node: Declarative Worker Images for Kubernetes Scale-Out

    Matt Boersma, Mark Rossetti

    The speakers break down what happens between a pending pod and a new worker doing useful work, and argue for moving repeatable setup into prepared, validated worker images. They describe a controller they built on Cluster API that declares worker images as custom resources, and share timings from a four-node GPU training job on Azure.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Kubernetes internals and runtimesAccelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: You scale GPU or other specialized node pools and want to shorten the time from a pending pod to a worker doing useful work.

    1. 3:55 The speakers separate reusable preparation from the work every new machine must still do, and say it matters for node replacement and rolling updates as well as scale-out.
    2. 6:38 The speakers follow a Ray training job waiting for GPU nodes, and say useful work starts long after the containers report running.

    5. 16:49 The speakers report a median of about 13 minutes 10 seconds from capacity request to first training step with stock images, and about 9 minutes 23 seconds with prepared images.

    Hidden Pod Startup Latency: Container Image Pull

    Omer Oz

    Omer Oz describes how LinkedIn investigated slow pod startups on its bare-metal fleet and traced most of the time to unpacking container image layers, not downloading them. He walks through the kubelet and containerd settings his team changed, the results he reports, and further options such as zstd compression, the EROFS snapshotter and peer-to-peer caching.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Kubernetes internals and runtimesObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: large container images slow your pod startups and you want a mental model of the pull path plus settings to try.

    1. 9:12 Oz describes the trigger: Flink apps shipping about 28 GB of images against a 20-minute pod startup SLO, which he says caused a series of incidents at LinkedIn.
    2. 14:46 Oz walks through an image pull timeline and says the download finishes early while most time goes to unpacking, which containerd does one layer at a time by default.
    3. 15:26 Oz explains LinkedIn's fix of raising the kubelet's parallel image pulls to three and lowering containerd's download concurrency, then shows the latency results he measured.
    4. 21:46 Oz presents the EROFS snapshotter for containerd, which he says skips eager unpacking and cut unpack time to under 0.4 seconds in his tests.
    5. 23:57 Oz describes a serialization point he found in containerd that capped total download throughput, and points to his pull request removing it.

    Reduce vLLM Pod Cold Starts with KServe Kernel Caching

    Jooho lee, Vishwa Hiren Gandhi

    The speakers explain how GPU kernel compilation can slow vLLM startup even when model weights are already cached. They describe KServe's approach for sharing signed kernel caches across pods and report what changed in their benchmark.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and serving

    Watch this if: Your vLLM replicas scale slowly even after you have cached the model weights.

    1. 4:33 The speakers break down a baseline vLLM startup of about 250 seconds and say the compilation step accounts for roughly 35 seconds, excluding image pulls and model downloads.
    2. 5:38 The speakers explain why a new replica can compile kernels again even after another pod has compiled them: the new pod does not inherit the previous pod's local cache.
    3. 10:31 The speakers propose packaging compiled kernels as a signed OCI artifact in a registry so another node can verify the signer before making the cache available.
    4. 13:52 The speakers distinguish a workload fingerprint that identifies where a cache came from from a compatibility fingerprint that checks whether compilation settings match.
    5. 18:24 In one benchmark, the speakers report that reusing the cache cut startup from 160 to 110 seconds and compilation from about 35 seconds to just over one second.

    Zero-Gap GPU Execution: Building Secure, Pre-Warmed Pod Runtimes for High-Velocity AI Agents

    Ankit Goyal, Cong Gu

    The LinkedIn speakers present Firepool, their control plane that hands GPU jobs from agents and reinforcement learning loops to pools of already running pods, so no pod is created on the job's critical path. They describe how pods are reset and reused per caller, how standby pods parked on busy GPUs with HAMi and Volcano take over when a job ends, and report that 19 of 20 pod requests in production are served from warm pods.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingAI agents and automationKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: You run agent or reinforcement learning loops that need GPUs in short repeated bursts and want to cut pod cold starts without keeping GPUs idle.

    1. 5:07 Ankit Goyal says that with the image cached a new GPU pod takes 20 to 30 seconds to start, and that an agent running hundreds of GPU experiments pays that cost each time when it lives on CPU nodes.
    2. 8:48 He walks through a job submission and says there is no pod creation in the critical path: the dispatcher claims a warm pod and the Firepool agent inside it fetches the job's code and runs it.
    3. 14:24 Cong Gu describes the reuse rules: the Firepool agent kills the job's process tree and verifies the reset or deletes the pod, and an idle pod is held only for the caller that used it.
    4. 19:06 Ankit Goyal explains zero-gap: they use HAMi and Volcano only to schedule several pods onto one GPU, where one pod runs a job with the whole card and the rest are standby pods ready to take over.
    5. 22:09 He says Firepool pods run sandboxed with no egress by default, and outbound calls go through a sidecar proxy that injects credentials and checks policy, so agents never hold real credentials.
