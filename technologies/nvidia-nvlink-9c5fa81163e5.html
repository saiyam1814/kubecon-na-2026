    Deep Dive Into the DRA Driver for NVIDIA GPUs: What Works Today, What’s Coming and Why It Matters

    Shiva Krishna Merla, Varun Ramachandra Sekar US

    The two maintainers describe the current state of the NVIDIA DRA driver for GPUs, covering deployment, GPU sharing, health monitoring, KubeVirt passthrough, and compute domains. They show demos of GPUs switching between container and VM use and of a compute domain daemon set, and outline what they plan for the next release and how to migrate from the device plugin.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: you allocate NVIDIA GPUs on Kubernetes and want the maintainers' view of what the DRA driver supports now and how to move off the device plugin.

    1. 6:51 The speaker describes system-mediated sharing planned for the next release, where pods in different namespaces each claim a share of a GPU using DRA consumable capacity.
    2. 8:01 The speaker says the driver watches GPU health events through NVML and publishes them as device taints, so the scheduler avoids those devices by default.
    3. 14:48 In a demo, the speaker starts four VMs and shows the node's GPUs moving from the NVIDIA driver to the VFIO driver for passthrough.
    4. 19:03 The speaker introduces a compute domain daemon set API that keeps IMEX daemons running before any workload is scheduled.
    5. 26:55 The speaker says existing device plugin requests for full GPUs and MIG devices can be translated to DRA resource claims, while time-sliced and MPS requests have no straightforward migration yet.

    From Zero to GPU Cloud: 12 months building Mistral Compute on top of Open Source

    Léonard Suslian

    Léonard Suslian of Mistral AI describes how a team of about ten built Mistral Compute, a bare-metal Kubernetes service he says now manages 5,000 hosts, on Cluster API, Metal3, Kamaji and Sveltos plus its own operators. He then goes through what he says broke along the way, from Ironic and board management controllers to SNAT exhaustion, etcd storage and a DHCP storm.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you are building or running bare-metal GPU clusters and want a candid list of what breaks between the racks arriving and tenants getting Kubernetes.

    1. 9:07 Suslian explains running each tenant's control plane as pods in the management cluster with Kamaji, which he says saves hardware and turns a control plane upgrade into a deployment rollout.
    2. 12:53 He describes using Sveltos from the management cluster to install add-ons chosen by the tenant, with drift detection that he says restores a deleted Cilium DaemonSet.
    3. 18:47 He says the team replaced Ironic with its own API-compatible rewrite in Go, scoped to their hardware, and went from first commit to production in 19 days.
    4. 21:03 He says hosts that cannot be fixed automatically get an AI agent in a pod with temporary credentials and scoped network access, and reports more than 20 such runs a day.
    5. 25:02 He recounts waking up to almost 900 nodes down because 10-minute DHCP leases caused a renewal storm the server could not keep up with, fixed by longer leases.

    Optimize Your Heterogeneous AI Cluster

    Mengxuan Li

    HAMi maintainer Mengxuan Li of Dynamia.ai explains the two problems the project targets, GPUs that can only be allocated whole and multi-GPU placements that ignore how cards are connected, and how HAMi's device plugin and HAMi-core enforce per-container memory limits to share a GPU. He then covers newer features (dynamic MIG, remote GPU access from nodes without GPUs, AMD support), a HAMi variant built on Dynamic Resource Allocation that works with other schedulers, and the roadmap.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: you operate GPU clusters on Kubernetes and want to share GPUs among workloads or see how HAMi fits with Dynamic Resource Allocation.

    1. 3:05 Li describes HAMi's two basic functions: GPU slicing, so a workload can be given part of a GPU, and scheduling that accounts for how GPUs on a node are connected when several are requested.
    2. 8:48 He explains dynamic MIG: the user states only the device memory needed, and HAMi finds a matching MIG template and a GPU with enough consecutive free slots, creates the instance, and recycles it when the workload ends.
    3. 11:22 He introduces remote GPU in version 2.11, where a pod on a CPU-only node reaches a GPU on another node over TCP and sees it when running nvidia-smi.
    4. 15:31 He says HAMi DRA, a webhook plus driver on top of Dynamic Resource Allocation, converts device-plugin style resource requests into resource claims automatically and adds allocation metrics, at the cost of the topology-aware scheduling of the original HAMi.
    5. 21:16 Roadmap: scheduling by rack and super pod network topology, kernel-level isolation through cgroups and MPS alongside HAMi-core, and multi-client remote GPU with per-client limits.

    Quota Isn't Enough: Topology Aware Scheduling on Modern GPU Clusters

    Amy Chen, JJ Jordan

    Amy Chen and JJ Jordan explain why GPU quota alone does not tell you whether a training job can fit within the physical network layout. They walk through Kueue topology-aware admission and share operational examples involving node replacement, preemption, and fragmented capacity.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: You schedule distributed GPU training and need to reason about rack boundaries, queue delays, or stranded capacity.

    1. 3:58 The speakers compare two placements with the same GPU count and explain why an uneven split across racks makes every all-reduce round wait on slower cross-rack transfers.
    2. 8:07 The speakers show how an example 128-GPU job asks Kueue for two 64-GPU slices and how admission checks both quota and rack-level fit.
    3. 13:31 The speakers compare unconstrained, preferred, and required placement, framing the choice as a trade-off between queue time and training speed.
    4. 16:37 The speakers describe replacing one unhealthy node within the same topology so a run can resume from a checkpoint without readmitting the whole workload.
    5. 19:16 The speakers explain why Kueue can decline to preempt smaller jobs when evicting them still would not free a large enough contiguous placement.

    When GPUs Become NUMA Nodes: Challenges Running Kubernetes with Memory-Coherent Hardware

    Kevin Klues

    Kevin Klues, presenting alone because his listed co-speaker could not attend, explains how NVIDIA systems with memory-coherent CPU and GPU links exposed GPU memory as NUMA nodes and why he says that broke the kubelet's memory accounting, pod memory limits and GPU isolation. He walks through two workarounds his team abandoned, the driver mode called CDMM that they adopted instead, and a kubelet fix that was still needed.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run Kubernetes on NVIDIA GB200-class or other unified-memory hardware and want to know how GPU memory interacts with kubelet memory accounting.

    1. 7:29 Klues lists three problems with the driver's NUMA mode on Kubernetes: memory over-reporting to the kubelet, a broken pod memory limit contract, and any container being able to allocate GPU memory it was not assigned.
    2. 10:55 He describes the first attempt, hiding GPU NUMA nodes from the kubelet with reserved memory settings, and says it failed on ordering with the GPU driver and on dynamic MIG devices.
    3. 13:44 He describes a second attempt using an NRI plugin to restrict each container to the NUMA nodes of its own GPUs, which he says got complicated once GPUs were injected through CDI and DRA.
    4. 16:35 He introduces Coherent Driver-Based Memory Management (CDMM), a driver mode that he says keeps GPU memory NUMA nodes offline while retaining pointer coherency over NVLink.
    5. 18:39 He says enabling the topology manager stalled the kubelet because it iterated combinations of 34 NUMA nodes, and that a patch merged in Kubernetes 1.36 skips nodes with no memory.
