    Automated Resource Allocation Driven by Service Level Objectives

    Pedro Célestin, Julia Furst Morgado

    Pedro Célestin and Julia Furst Morgado present a case study in letting teams specify service objectives and using measured performance to test resource changes automatically. They describe using error budgets and production canaries to test proposed changes instead of letting an AI agent change allocation directly.

    25 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Observability and reliabilityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: You want to turn service objectives into allocation decisions without handing an AI agent direct control of production.

    1. 2:55 The speakers translate an example latency goal into an error budget, then explain how budget burn could inform whether to add or reclaim resources.
    2. 7:20 They propose canarying a resource change, not just a new build. They explain that if more resources do not improve the service-level indicator, the cause may instead be a bad release.
    3. 9:45 They describe the division of work in their case study: developers set the service objective, while the platform tests its own allocation proposals and can keep or reverse a change based on measured results.
    4. 11:28 They say letting AI change production allocation directly did not work well for them, so they limited it to proposing changes that a production canary could verify.
    5. 13:25 They walk through their stack: Prometheus collects metrics, Argo CD deploys changes, Argo Rollouts runs canaries, and Knative is used in some cases rather than being required.

    Autoscaling Reality Check: The Hands-On HTTP Optimization Challenge

    David Pech

    David Pech runs a hands-on tutorial in which attendees benchmark HTTP services in NGINX, Go, PHP and Java with k6 and watch how autoscaling reacts, first with the CPU-based Horizontal Pod Autoscaler and then with Knative scaling on concurrent requests. He walks through each round on a Grafana dashboard and describes the tuning he tried, including cases where he says his own improvement failed.

    80 min slot · Any Level · Tutorial

    Accelerators and workload schedulingObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you run HTTP services behind HPA or Knative and want to see, round by round, where autoscaling lags behind real traffic.

    1. 10:11 The speaker lays out the lab: Argo CD, Istio, Knative and a Prometheus stack on a local k3d cluster or GitHub Codespaces, with k6 load tests run against services in several flavors.
    2. 29:15 In the NGINX round scaled on CPU, the speaker shows on the dashboard that pods stay at the maximum after the load falls and that latencies remain, and asks the room why.
    3. 36:23 The speaker explains Knative's panic window: when demand jumps well above the estimate the autoscaler enters panic mode and does not scale down during it.
    4. 1:03:46 For the Java round the speaker says slow startup limits how fast scaling can react, names GraalVM as one option, and shows a tuning attempt that he says failed completely.
    5. 1:06:13 In the scale-to-zero round the speaker points out that a workload at zero has no metrics, which he says makes alerting on it hard.

    Fireside Chat: Future of Kubernetes and Agents with Tim Hockin and Kelsey Hightower

    Tim Hockin, Kelsey Hightower, Savannah Peterson

    Tim Hockin explains why he thinks agents are a different kind of workload for Kubernetes and how Agent Substrate approaches sandboxing, idleness, and identity. Kelsey Hightower compares agents to earlier automation and advises the audience to work out whether a task needs an agent before choosing a platform for it.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Fireside Chat

    AI agents and automationKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you are deciding how to run agents on Kubernetes and want to hear the reasoning behind Agent Substrate from Tim Hockin alongside Kelsey Hightower's caution.

    1. 4:06 Tim Hockin says agents are untrusted, mostly idle, and single tenant, which he says leads to either slow startups or single-digit CPU utilization.
    2. 8:21 Tim Hockin says a few seconds of container startup does not fit an agent that runs for half a second and goes back to sleep.
    3. 12:05 Tim Hockin says every agent in Agent Substrate is sandboxed, cannot reach the network without permission, and has its own identity, with outbound traffic sent to a pluggable policy enforcement point.
    4. 14:36 Kelsey Hightower advises working out whether a task needs an agent at all, and recalls serverless functions that grew until they resembled apps on a VM.
    5. 21:37 Asked about acting on behalf of a user, Tim Hockin says version one has its own identity and issuer and that an on-behalf-of flow is being discussed with users and is not done.

    From Vibe to Live: Building for the Real World

    David Biel, Trey Caliva

    David Biel, who says he has no engineering background, and cloud security engineer Trey Caliva tell how David vibe-coded his family dental cabinet company's website and back-office tools and got them to production on managed services. Trey argues that the advice he gave in person should live in the AI's context as skills, hooks and MCP server permissions, so that platform teams can guide the many non-engineers he says are already building this way.

    30 min slot · Beginner · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experienceAI agents and automationDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: non-engineers in your company are shipping AI-built apps and you want ideas for guiding them.

    1. 2:22 David says he got the work done with a $200 Claude subscription, compared with the $5,000 to $10,000 his family was quoted for a website and brand about ten years earlier.
    2. 5:28 Trey says David's app runs on Firebase Hosting, Cloud Functions, Firestore and Firebase Auth, chosen so there are no servers to patch or clusters to run.
    3. 7:49 Trey lists what first-time builders may miss, such as open database rules, unauthenticated endpoints and pasting an API key into the chat.
    4. 10:16 Trey proposes putting guardrails in the AI's context through skills, hooks and MCP server access, with skills moving from ad hoc to a shared repo to a governed catalog.
    5. 13:47 Trey says skills advise and hooks enforce, showing a simplified hook that runs a script on each prompt to catch pasted secrets, plus deny rules that block deploying from a laptop.

    New Traffic, New Rules: Standardizing AI-Aware Networking in Kubernetes

    Nir Rozenbaum, Keith Mattix

    Nir Rozenbaum and Keith Mattix present the proposals the Kubernetes AI Gateway Working Group has been developing for Gateway API: pre-routing filters for payload processing, a Backend resource, an endpoint selector, gateway routability and a multi-stage route. They explain the AI traffic cases behind each one, such as routing on the model name in the request body and calling external model providers, and say next steps include external processing, token-based rate limiting and consolidating AI networking efforts in SIG Network.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Networking and connectivityAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: you build or operate gateways for inference or agent traffic and want to know which Gateway API additions the AI Gateway Working Group is proposing.

    1. 4:09 Nir Rozenbaum introduces pre-routing filters, an ordered list of filters that run before a route is selected, which he says is already experimental and lets a gateway promote the model name from the request body to a header for body-based routing.
    2. 7:46 Keith Mattix argues that the Kubernetes Service resource does two jobs, a producer-oriented front end with a DNS name and cluster IP and a consumer-oriented bag of endpoints, and that AI traffic to external model providers has made the limits more urgent.
    3. 12:56 He describes the Backend resource, now experimental in Gateway API, which holds an in-cluster endpoint selector or an external hostname plus TLS and protocol settings, and says it is not a full replacement for Service.
    4. 19:40 He presents the routability proposal for stating in a standard way whether a gateway is reachable only inside the cluster, and warns that a publicly reachable egress gateway becomes an open relay.
    5. 23:17 Nir Rozenbaum introduces the multi-stage route, which references existing routes and sequences stages such as tokenization, prefill and decode for one request, with payload processing handling what passes between stages.

    Untrusted Code on Secure Runtimes: Agent Sandboxes on Kubernetes

    Mofi Rahman

    Mofi Rahman leads a hands-on lab on GKE, with four Google colleagues helping attendees in the room, and explains that it uses the newly open-sourced Agent Substrate where the abstract promised Agent Sandbox. Attendees create workers, actor templates and actors, suspend and resume them with their state, allow a model call through an egress policy and run 20 actors on two workers, and he says internal benchmarks reached more than 100 million actors.

    80 min slot · Beginner · Tutorial

    AI agents and automationSecurity, identity and policyKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you want to follow a full lab on suspending and resuming agent workloads on Kubernetes and see how actors differ from one pod per sandbox.

    1. 21:39 Rahman explains why the lab switched projects: Agent Sandbox maps a sandbox to a pod with warm pools, while Agent Substrate adds actors, many per pod, and he suggests Sandbox for agents that run for days and Substrate for short, bursty sessions.
    2. 25:22 He lists Substrate's parts: an API server, Postgres in place of etcd, which he says could not scale to the 100 million actor goal, egress control, and a router that proxies requests so actors do not each need an IP address.
    3. 36:15 He suspends an actor, sends it a request and gets an answer anyway, explaining that the router holds the request while the actor resumes from its snapshot mid-state, unlike Cloud Run, Lambda or Knative starting from zero.
    4. 49:14 A model call from the actor fails because egress is denied by default, and it succeeds after he creates an egress gateway and a policy that allows the one model URL.
    5. 57:09 A third actor times out on two full workers, and he stresses that Substrate does not decide when to suspend: the platform team defines idle, here three seconds without a request, after which 20 actors share two workers.
