    Autoscaling Reality Check: The Hands-On HTTP Optimization Challenge

    David Pech

    David Pech runs a hands-on tutorial in which attendees benchmark HTTP services in NGINX, Go, PHP and Java with k6 and watch how autoscaling reacts, first with the CPU-based Horizontal Pod Autoscaler and then with Knative scaling on concurrent requests. He walks through each round on a Grafana dashboard and describes the tuning he tried, including cases where he says his own improvement failed.

    80 min slot · Any Level · Tutorial

    Accelerators and workload schedulingObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you run HTTP services behind HPA or Knative and want to see, round by round, where autoscaling lags behind real traffic.

    1. 10:11 The speaker lays out the lab: Argo CD, Istio, Knative and a Prometheus stack on a local k3d cluster or GitHub Codespaces, with k6 load tests run against services in several flavors.
    2. 29:15 In the NGINX round scaled on CPU, the speaker shows on the dashboard that pods stay at the maximum after the load falls and that latencies remain, and asks the room why.
    3. 36:23 The speaker explains Knative's panic window: when demand jumps well above the estimate the autoscaler enters panic mode and does not scale down during it.
    4. 1:03:46 For the Java round the speaker says slow startup limits how fast scaling can react, names GraalVM as one option, and shows a tuning attempt that he says failed completely.
    5. 1:06:13 In the scale-to-zero round the speaker points out that a workload at zero has no metrics, which he says makes alerting on it hard.

    Battle-tested Autoscaling Paradigms with KEDA

    Jan Wozniak, Christian Melendez

    Jan Wozniak and Christian Melendez run a live audience quiz backed by a small CPU-hosted model and use it to show KEDA scaling the pods while Karpenter scales the nodes. They walk through their recommendations on metric freshness, fallbacks between metric sources, spare node capacity through capacity buffers, and conservative scale-down.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you autoscale workloads on Kubernetes and want practical guidance on metric sources, fallbacks, node headroom and scale-down settings with KEDA and Karpenter.

    1. 3:45 Wozniak groups scaling metrics into reactive ones such as CPU and memory, proactive ones such as load balancer and queue metrics, and predictive ones, and says reactive scaling tends to run behind the load.
    2. 10:13 He says Prometheus can keep working for smaller environments, and suggests KEDA's OpenTelemetry scaler, which has metrics pushed to it, for teams that want to remove the scrape delay or that run a very large number of scaled objects.
    3. 12:58 He describes KEDA scaling modifiers and their dynamic fallback operator, then has the OpenTelemetry source killed live to show scaling continue on the Prometheus copy of the metric.
    4. 16:56 Melendez explains Karpenter's capacity buffers, where a pod template and a buffer object act as virtual pods that keep headroom ready, and compares this with the older pattern of low-priority balloon pods.
    5. 26:41 For scale-down, Melendez advises a stabilization window longer than the time an application needs to start on a fresh node, plus a delay on Karpenter consolidation, with GPU nodes removed only when empty.

    Beyond the Cluster Boundary: Sharing GPUs at Scale

    Tim Killian

    Tim Killian describes how Snapchat moved reserved GPU capacity out of team clusters into capacity pool clusters, one per machine type, and exposes it back to customer clusters as virtual nodes built on Virtual Kubelet. He says Kueue handles quota, borrowing and preemption centrally, and reports that high priority jobs saw no longer waits while lower priority jobs waited less.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: scarce GPUs sit idle in one team's cluster while another team waits, and you want a design for sharing them across clusters.

    1. 3:42 The speaker explains the decision to put each machine type in its own Kubernetes cluster and treat those clusters like cells, since everything does not fit in one cluster.
    2. 8:30 The speaker introduces Virtual Kubelet as the base for virtual nodes that stand in for remote capacity, so customers still see a normal node and a running pod.
    3. 9:56 The speaker says the team uses Kueue to decide at a granular level which workloads may land on a virtual node, with a fallback to local capacity when there is no quota.
    4. 11:36 The speaker names autoscaled inference as the hard case: HPA needs metrics from the metrics server, which has to get them from the nodes, including virtual ones.
    5. 14:52 The speaker reports the outcome: no increase in wait time for high priority jobs, a large decrease for lower priority jobs, and a cloud provider outage handled by cutting quotas.

    Engineering Kubernetes for the Next Era of Workloads

    Mike Stefaniak

    Mike Stefaniak describes bottlenecks AWS found when running large Kubernetes clusters and says the team contributed fixes upstream. He walks through watch-cache and scheduler changes, accelerator-aware provisioning with Karpenter, and parallel image unpacking in containerd.

    5 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    Accelerators and workload schedulingKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run large GPU clusters and want concrete examples of upstream fixes from image unpacking to accelerator placement.

    1. 0:47 Stefaniak says AWS replaced a long-held Kubernetes API server watch-cache lock with a lazy snapshot, cutting writer wait from over half a second to 2 ms in its million-pod benchmark.
    2. 1:03 Stefaniak says a new Horizontal Pod Autoscaler selector store removed a read/write bottleneck, letting AWS run four times as many HPA workers in its largest clusters.
    3. 1:21 Stefaniak says an incremental scheduler cache avoided rebuilding volume reference counts and that AWS saw scheduling stay above 300 pods per second in its largest clusters.
    4. 2:28 Stefaniak explains that Dynamic Resource Allocation can describe GPU and network topology, and says Karpenter can now model devices for instances it has not launched yet.
    5. 3:48 Stefaniak says parallel layer unpacking in containerd brought a 10 GB image pull from about two minutes to under 45 seconds in AWS's testing.

The speakers compare where their own configuration lives and cover rendered manifests, validation, short-lived environments, vulnerability rollouts and whether configuration should share a repository with application code.

    25 min slot · Level not listed · Birds of a Feather

    Adarsh Kumar

    The speaker recounts debugging recurring CPU spikes in Istio sidecars that kept pushing autoscalers to their maximum without any rise in traffic. He says the cause was configuration pushes triggered by churn in an unrelated namespace, and that limiting what each sidecar can see removed the spikes and lowered sidecar memory use.

    Networking and connectivityObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: You run Istio sidecars at scale and see unexplained proxy CPU spikes or heavy configuration pushes.

    2. 3:14 He says the sidecar containers showed a repeating spiky CPU pattern, and telemetry showed a large number of xDS pushes with no obvious cause.

    Kubernetes Solutions for Agent-Shaped Problems

    Tim Hockin, Dmitry Berkovich

    Tim Hockin and Dmitry Berkovich of Google say agent workloads are bursty, run untrusted code and have a human waiting, which makes one sandboxed pod per agent session wasteful and slow on Kubernetes. They present Agent Substrate, an open source system built on Kubernetes that suspends idle agents to snapshots and resumes them on request, and spend the second half answering audience questions about policy, autoscaling, memory and where it fits.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automationKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run or plan to run many agent sessions on your own Kubernetes clusters and want the reasoning behind Agent Substrate from its authors, plus a long Q&A on its current limits.

    1. 0:47 Hockin and Berkovich name three traits of agent workloads: they are bursty and mostly idle, they run untrusted code that must be sandboxed, and a human is usually waiting for the response.
    2. 6:57 Hockin states the three design principles of Agent Substrate (security by default, near-zero cost for idle agents, fast wake-up) and what he calls aspirational goals: billions of agents per cluster, 10,000 activations per second and wake-up in about 100 milliseconds.
    3. 9:16 Berkovich defines the five terms: an actor runs one agent session, an actor template configures it, a worker is the pod that hosts actors, a node-level manager handles images and snapshots, and a network layer routes requests in and applies policy to traffic out.
    4. 13:06 He explains resume speed through layers: volumes always survive, while root filesystem changes and the memory snapshot are reused only if the OCI image is unchanged, otherwise the actor cold starts from its volumes.
    5. 15:26 Hockin describes a use case they see often: a managed agent service runs the agentic loop while the work it triggers runs in Agent Substrate inside the customer's own cluster, where it can be audited and shut off.

    SIG Autoscaling Project Updates

    Jack Francis, Jason Deal, Heba Elayoty, Kuba Tużnik

    Jack Francis, Jason Deal, Heba Elayoty and Kuba Tużnik give updates on Kubernetes SIG Autoscaling projects, including Karpenter's DRA support, balanced consolidation and node repair, the capacity buffer and capacity quota APIs, and the split of Cluster Autoscaler into a core plus separate provider projects. Elayoty then explains what workload-aware scheduling needs from autoscalers, and Tużnik presents scheduler-driven autoscaling, a proposed redesign in which the scheduler requests nodes from the autoscaler.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run Karpenter or Cluster Autoscaler and want the latest feature updates plus the proposed redesign of how autoscalers work with the scheduler.

    1. 3:28 Jason Deal says Karpenter added DRA support in its scheduling simulation in v1.14, covering structured parameters, consumable capacity, partitionable devices and prioritized alternatives, with provider implementations in the AWS provider and EKS Auto Mode.
    2. 6:11 He introduces balanced consolidation, which weighs the share of pods disrupted against the share of cost saved and only acts when the score passes a threshold.
    3. 10:08 Jack Francis says Cluster Autoscaler is being split into a core repository and separate provider projects, and that the SIG will keep supporting releases in the older all-in-one model for a transition period.
    4. 19:50 Kuba Tużnik describes the split brain problem: the autoscaler has to predict what the scheduler will decide, and when its predictions are off it makes wrong scaling decisions.
    5. 24:15 He outlines the proposed design in which the autoscaler publishes potential capacity and the scheduler issues node claims, and estimates it at more than ten KEPs over one to two years.

    Your Peers Are Struggling With LLM Inference on Kubernetes Too. Here's What the Data Says.

    Danielle Cook, Andy Suderman

They group the problems into scheduling, scaling, routing, placement and ownership, and close with five steps they recommend, from starting on an AI-conformant cluster to setting a time-to-first-token SLO.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and servingObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you run LLM inference on Kubernetes and want survey numbers to compare your latency targets, GPU utilization and routing setup against.

    1. 4:33 Cook presents the survey's performance expectations: 82% of respondents need an end-to-end response within 500 milliseconds, 64% within 250, and nearly half expect to roll back within 15 minutes when inference degrades.
