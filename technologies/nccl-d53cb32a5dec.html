    Fine-Tuning a Vision Language Model Across Hundreds of GPUs on Kubernetes

    Phelipe Fabres

    Phelipe Fabres, an AWS solutions architect presenting without the customer co-speaker he says could not attend, tells how a Brazilian legal-tech startup fine-tuned a vision language model for messy legal documents on 32 nodes with 256 GPUs on Amazon EKS. He organizes the story as four patterns he says they learned, starting every worker together, moving data fast between nodes, keeping every GPU fed and recovering from failures, with two escalations as examples.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: you plan multi-node GPU training on Kubernetes and want to hear what stalled one team's jobs and the patterns they adopted in response.

    1. 2:04 Fabres opens with an escalation where instance checks on all 32 nodes passed, yet one overheating GPU stalled the whole distributed training job.
    2. 14:56 He describes gang admission, a pre-job that verifies every node is healthy before the job is submitted, because he says each hour of waiting costs GPU time across all 32 nodes.
    3. 17:46 He recounts a networking escalation in which performance dropped at batch transfers of 128 to 256 MB, traced to the NCCL, libfabric and Elastic Fabric Adapter layers inside the pod, and says tuning restored the same throughput at every size.
    4. 23:03 He explains the two data layers, object storage in Amazon S3 for prepared data and FSx for Lustre for checkpoints, copying only what each job needs to limit cost.
    5. 26:45 He outlines the recovery pattern: watch device health trends, block the node from new placements, wait for a compatible replacement and restart, and says the recovery job later grew into an agent.

    Quota Isn't Enough: Topology Aware Scheduling on Modern GPU Clusters

    Amy Chen, JJ Jordan

    Amy Chen and JJ Jordan explain why GPU quota alone does not tell you whether a training job can fit within the physical network layout. They walk through Kueue topology-aware admission and share operational examples involving node replacement, preemption, and fragmented capacity.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: You schedule distributed GPU training and need to reason about rack boundaries, queue delays, or stranded capacity.

    1. 3:58 The speakers compare two placements with the same GPU count and explain why an uneven split across racks makes every all-reduce round wait on slower cross-rack transfers.
    2. 8:07 The speakers show how an example 128-GPU job asks Kueue for two 64-GPU slices and how admission checks both quota and rack-level fit.
    3. 13:31 The speakers compare unconstrained, preferred, and required placement, framing the choice as a trade-off between queue time and training speed.
    4. 16:37 The speakers describe replacing one unhealthy node within the same topology so a run can resume from a checkpoint without readmitting the whole workload.
    5. 19:16 The speakers explain why Kueue can decline to preempt smaller jobs when evicting them still would not free a large enough contiguous placement.

    Time to Learn New Tricks to Improve Performance on DRA-Managed Devices

    Alexander Kanevskiy, Sara Qasmi

    Sara Qasmi of T-Systems shows measurements from LLM serving on two GPU server types, where she says the default Kubernetes device placement often lost bandwidth or added latency compared with hand-aligned devices. Alexander Kanevskiy of Intel explains why a NUMA number no longer describes modern CPU and PCI topology, and walks through newer Dynamic Resource Allocation features he says help express the alignment a workload needs.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: you place GPUs, NICs and CPUs for inference on Kubernetes and want measured examples of what device alignment changes.

    1. 5:23 Qasmi reports that GPU and NIC pairs aligned on the same PCI root reached full RDMA bandwidth on one platform while other pairings got 38%, yet on the second platform NUMA alignment was enough.
    2. 6:50 She says the default claim picked the wrong GPU pair in five of five runs on a busy node, and that NCCL's own peer-to-peer default also limited bandwidth until it was changed.
    3. 10:08 She measures CPU to GPU round-trip latency and says default pods were 33% slower, with eight of ten landing on the wrong socket, which an NRI topology plugin corrected.
    4. 14:56 Kanevskiy argues that a NUMA number only maps to the memory controller's mode of operation and is now a virtual value that does not describe physical topology.
    5. 20:38 He presents list attributes and derived attributes in DRA as ways to describe topology and compare values across drivers, and warns that a claim with no overlapping solution leaves the pod unscheduled.
