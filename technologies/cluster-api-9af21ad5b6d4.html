    AI Found a 0-Day in Metal3. What Happens Next?

    Tuomo Tanskanen

    Tuomo Tanskanen, who leads the Metal3 security committee, follows a vulnerability report from arrival through triage, fix, release and downstream upgrade, drawing on reports from his own AI discovery pipeline and showing how its findings broke down after triage. He also describes how fixes in OpenStack Ironic reach Metal3 and vendor distributions, and advises users to run supported releases, know their configuration and ask vendors how quickly they take in fixes.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Security, identity and policyDelivery, GitOps and release safetyAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you maintain a project that receives AI-generated vulnerability reports, or you run Metal3 and want to know how its security fixes reach you.

    1. 4:12 Tuomo Tanskanen says every vulnerability disclosure Metal3 received this year came from an AI discovery pipeline he built himself, which also scans OpenStack Ironic, and that its findings go through each project's normal security process.
    2. 8:27 He gives the pipeline's numbers: of about 220 findings, 80 were false or duplicates, 31 became hardening issues, 22 were plain bugs and 22 were confirmed, and he concludes that discovery is fast while triage, fixing and coordinated release are slow.
    3. 10:34 He says Metal3 fixes low and medium issues in the open, because GitHub private forks give almost no CI support and force manual testing, and reserves embargoed handling for high and critical ones.
    4. 14:41 He describes a lesson from combining four related cross-namespace issues into one advisory: no CVE could be issued for it, because a CVE covers a single issue.
    5. 20:20 Using two fixes as examples, he says each step down the dependency chain into vendor distributions took roughly a month, so fixes take a long time to reach production.

    Beyond Provisioning: Multi-Tenancy, Rack Awareness, and Declarative Networking in Metal3

    Kashif Khan, Sunnatillo Samadov

    Kashif Khan and Sunnatillo Samadov, both Metal3 maintainers, present three additions to the bare metal provisioning project: multi-tenancy through host claims, failure domains that spread control plane nodes across racks, and top-of-rack switch port configuration. They show a short recorded demo of each and close with which release and feature gate each one sits behind.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Kubernetes internals and runtimesNetworking and connectivityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you run Kubernetes on bare metal and want several teams to share one hardware pool, rack-aware control planes, or switch ports configured from YAML.

    1. 5:40 Kashif Khan names three gaps he says Metal3 had: teams sharing hardware meant handing out BMC passwords, nothing kept control planes off a single rack, and switch port VLANs were typed by hand.
    2. 6:46 Kashif Khan explains the multi-tenancy design: tenants create a HostClaim in their own namespace, and an admin-owned HostDeployPolicy in the pool namespace decides which namespaces may consume which hosts.
    3. 12:24 Kashif Khan explains failure domains: label each BareMetalHost with its rack, list the failure domains on the Metal3 cluster, and Cluster API then spreads control plane nodes across them.
    4. 17:43 Sunnatillo Samadov introduces the BareMetalSwitch and HostNetworkAttachment resources, which he says let Ironic set switch port mode and VLAN tags during provisioning and clean them up afterwards.
    5. 23:19 The speakers give the status: multi-tenancy is alpha behind a feature gate, failure domains are available in the Cluster API provider release they name, and switch configuration is behind a feature gate in the Bare Metal Operator.

    Data-Driven Platform Guardrails: What 16,500 Incidents Tell You to Automate First

    Henrique Santana, Henrique Dalssaso

    Henrique Santana and Henrique Dalssaso walk through the top root causes they found in more than 16,000 Kubernetes incidents, including IP exhaustion, auth failures and node provisioning problems. For each one they suggest platform guardrails to automate, and they explain how to run the same analysis on your own incident data.

    25 min slot · Intermediate · Session

    Observability and reliabilityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a list of common Kubernetes incident root causes and the guardrails these speakers suggest automating for each.

    1. 1:35 The speakers describe their dataset as more than 16,000 incidents across many Kubernetes clusters from January to mid-May, grouped with AI and then reviewed by hand. They say DNS did not make their top 10 root causes.
    2. 3:57 They say about a third of one top pattern was pods with no IP address available, from small or shared subnets, and suggest Cilium multi-pool IPAM and broader Karpenter node pools.
    3. 6:59 For auth failures they separate missing permissions, RBAC that was never bound to the pod, and policies on the target endpoint, and suggest Kyverno policies, projected service account tokens and cert-manager.

    5. 20:59 To repeat the analysis on your own data, they suggest classifying by what actually failed rather than the symptom, ranking by frequency, and asking whether the platform, the app team or both can fix it.

    From Maintenance Chaos to Steady Uptime: GitOps-Driven Scheduling across GPU Fleets

They declare the target version in Git, let a controller in each cluster report drift, and have a fleet rollout planner and a lock service decide when each cluster may start.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Delivery, GitOps and release safetyAccelerators and workload schedulingPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you run upgrades and node maintenance across many clusters and still coordinate them with spreadsheets and chat threads.

    1. 7:45 Balakrishnan describes a drift-detecting controller in every cluster that compares the version declared in Git with each node's kubelet version and raises a maintenance request to the fleet rollout planner without acting on it itself.
    2. 10:50 She says a ticket needs permission from a cluster lock service, a fleet-level gatekeeper that keeps an upgrade from colliding with incidents, change freezes or another maintenance, and the planner backs off and retries on a no.

    4. 15:32 Bandel explains the split of decisions: the fleet planner decides when an upgrade starts in a cluster, and a controller inside the cluster picks which nodes drain first based on what is running, capacity and node health.
    5. 19:46 Bandel points to upstream work inside one cluster, a KEP for eviction requests that she says targets alpha in 1.38 and one for node lifecycle conditions, and says the fleet level is still the open part.

    GitOps Meltdown: How a Commit Triggered a Cascading Delete of 39 Clusters in Production

    Deepak Goel

    Deepak Goel tells the story of an incident in which, he says, a Git commit that accidentally removed a top-level Flux Kustomization caused Flux to prune a management cluster's whole tree, including the Git server, Flux itself and 39 workload clusters. He walks through the two waves of deletion, the multi-day recovery in which application data was found in the storage system's recycle bin, and the lessons he draws about blast radius, guarded deletes and rehearsed recovery.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Observability and reliabilityDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: you run clusters with Flux and Cluster API and want to see how one bad commit can delete a fleet, and what the speaker changed afterward.

    1. 6:12 Goel explains that when a Flux Kustomization with pruning enabled is removed from the Git source, Flux deletes everything in that Kustomization's inventory and then the object itself.
    2. 11:00 He names three couplings behind the incident: the Git server ran on the management cluster Flux managed, Flux's own configuration came from Git, and one top-level Kustomization was parent to all the others.
    3. 15:07 He says that when support restarted Flux to fix the outage, the new Flux instance saw the objects marked for deletion and finished deleting them.
    4. 19:24 He says the daily Velero backups had never been tested and were of little use, and that the application data was finally recovered from the recycle bin of the underlying storage system.
    5. 22:00 His first lessons are to shrink the blast radius with one Kustomization per cluster and to make bulk deletion harder, for example by disabling prune or setting the deletion policy to orphan.
