    Cortex: Surviving High Cardinality Metrics

    Friedrich Gonzalez

    Friedrich Gonzalez, a Cortex maintainer, explains how unbounded labels multiply series counts and what Cortex offers to detect, reduce and survive high cardinality, including per-tenant limits, active series trackers, remote write v2 with native histograms, resource-based query rejection and eviction, and the Parquet block format. He also reports on the independent security audit done for the project's CNCF graduation.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Observability and reliabilityData, storage and state

    Watch this if: you run Cortex or another Prometheus-compatible backend and need ways to find and contain high-cardinality metrics.

    1. 3:05 Friedrich Gonzalez separates bounded labels such as status codes and templated routes from unbounded ones such as user, request and trace IDs, and recommends putting unbounded data in logs, traces and exemplars.
    2. 8:16 He says Cortex limits protect a cluster but do not show which label is the problem, and introduces active series trackers in Cortex 1.22, which count active series matching a chosen label.
    3. 13:30 He explains that a classic histogram costs one series per bucket, 13 in his example, and recommends remote write v2 with native histograms or converted custom-bucket histograms to send each as a single series.
    4. 19:12 He describes resource-based protection in which a component checks its own CPU and heap every 100 milliseconds, rejects new work past a threshold and evicts the longest-running query, a feature he says now covers the querier.
    5. 24:58 He reports that the security audit required for CNCF graduation took place in April and found only medium and low issues, all of which he says are fixed, and asks Cortex users to upgrade.

    Feeding the GPU: Streaming Data into Training, Lakehouse, and Serving without the Serialization Tax

    Dheeraj Kapur, Deepak Choudhary

    The speakers describe a streaming design in which producers send Arrow IPC batches once to a gateway that writes them to GPU memory and to a lakehouse without converting rows to columns again. They share their own measurements for throughput, query engines and crash recovery, and say the work is not open source yet.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Data, storage and stateAccelerators and workload schedulingAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: You build streaming pipelines that feed both GPUs and lakehouse tables and want to see where serialization cost goes.

    1. 2:59 The speaker says separate pipelines for the GPU, the lakehouse and other destinations each convert row data into a columnar format again.
    2. 6:37 The speaker says the producer should send Arrow IPC so that preparation happens once, and that the format converts one to one into cuDF and Parquet.
    3. 10:51 The speaker explains that the gateway transfers batches into GPU memory, stores them in a durable log for the lakehouse, and sends the acknowledgment once the data is on disk.
    4. 17:00 The speaker reports a throughput of 3 million points per second with 20 pods, and says they want to scale the system to 17 million points per second.
    5. 21:33 The speaker says a fix took them from 25,000 duplicates to zero duplicates and no data loss over two weeks of chaos testing.

    Fine-Tuning a Vision Language Model Across Hundreds of GPUs on Kubernetes

    Phelipe Fabres

    Phelipe Fabres, an AWS solutions architect presenting without the customer co-speaker he says could not attend, tells how a Brazilian legal-tech startup fine-tuned a vision language model for messy legal documents on 32 nodes with 256 GPUs on Amazon EKS. He organizes the story as four patterns he says they learned, starting every worker together, moving data fast between nodes, keeping every GPU fed and recovering from failures, with two escalations as examples.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: you plan multi-node GPU training on Kubernetes and want to hear what stalled one team's jobs and the patterns they adopted in response.

    1. 2:04 Fabres opens with an escalation where instance checks on all 32 nodes passed, yet one overheating GPU stalled the whole distributed training job.
    2. 14:56 He describes gang admission, a pre-job that verifies every node is healthy before the job is submitted, because he says each hour of waiting costs GPU time across all 32 nodes.
    3. 17:46 He recounts a networking escalation in which performance dropped at batch transfers of 128 to 256 MB, traced to the NCCL, libfabric and Elastic Fabric Adapter layers inside the pod, and says tuning restored the same throughput at every size.
    4. 23:03 He explains the two data layers, object storage in Amazon S3 for prepared data and FSx for Lustre for checkpoints, copying only what each job needs to limit cost.
    5. 26:45 He outlines the recovery pattern: watch device health trends, block the node from new placements, wait for a compatible replacement and restart, and says the recovery job later grew into an agent.

    Follow the GPUs: How a Regulated Fortune 100 Serves Customers When No Single Cloud Has Enough

    Yuva Peavler, Michael Kinsley

    Yuva Peavler and Michael Kinsley of GEICO describe their hybrid cloud platform, a flat network across providers with a zero trust overlay built on SPIFFE/SPIRE, Istio and Cilium, and say it lets them move workloads to wherever GPUs are available. Michael then shares a tool they wrote to probe GPU availability across AWS and Azure regions, what four weeks of samples showed them, and their idea for a booking agent that reserves capacity ahead of demand.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingNetworking and connectivityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you need GPUs without long reservations and want measured data on whether multi-region and multi-cloud help.

    1. 1:45 Michael gives the takeaways first: current single-GPU SKUs need multi-region support, flagship eight-GPU nodes have to be booked ahead, and he says the autoscaler will not do the booking.
    2. 3:43 Yuva explains the platform from the bottom up: one flat L3 network across every provider, identically built Kubernetes clusters, then SPIFFE/SPIRE identity, Istio mutual TLS and Cilium deny-by-default policy.
    3. 8:54 Yuva says they made SPIRE's data store pluggable in their fork and run Cassandra behind it for an active-active multi-region control plane, and that they have proposed this upstream.
    4. 16:54 Michael introduces their Follow the GPUs tool, which asks for GPUs, doubles the request after each yes until it gets a no, then waits about an hour and starts again.
    5. 22:02 Michael reports that in their data multi-region did not matter for small single-GPU SKUs but raised the chance of getting an RTX Pro 6000 from about 45% to 70 to 75%.

    From Packets to Paychecks: eBPF-Based Network Cost Attribution at Scale

    Brian Ugie, Aanchal Khandelwal

    Brian Ugie and Aanchal Khandelwal describe how Adobe's platform team attributes AWS network costs to tenants on multi-tenant Kubernetes clusters by collecting per-pod byte counts with Retina, rolling them up in sharded Prometheus, and classifying each flow into billing categories. They say the pipeline runs in production but still needs hand tuning of Prometheus shards per cluster, and they call it not a victory lap.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Networking and connectivityObservability and reliabilityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you run multi-tenant clusters and need to split cloud network charges by team.

    1. 3:42 Brian says Hubble flow counts were not enough for chargeback, using an example where one namespace has 90% of the flows but moves far less data than another.
    2. 5:53 Aanchal walks through the pipeline: Retina on every node, Prometheus collection, a classification step, results in S3, and a billing team that turns bytes into cost.
    3. 10:24 Aanchal says raw flow queries timed out and shards ran out of memory until a recording rule on each shard pre-aggregated bytes with node region and zone every three minutes.
    4. 12:30 Aanchal explains the classifier picks the most specific matching CIDR for each destination IP, as the VPC does, to label traffic as internet, peering, transit gateway, VPC endpoint or local.
    5. 19:35 Brian gives fleet numbers of about 85 clusters, about 630 Prometheus shards and about 30 terabytes of requested memory, and says they tune every cluster by hand.

    Kubernetes Data Protection WG Intro & Deep Dive

    Xing Yang, Dave Smith-Uchida

    Xing Yang and Dave Smith-Uchida explain why the Kubernetes Data Protection Working Group formed and walk through the backup and restore building blocks it drives, including COSI, snapshot topology, changed block tracking and volume group snapshots. Smith-Uchida then presents two white papers in progress, on data protection best practices and on recovering from ransomware attacks.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Data, storage and stateKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you back up stateful workloads on Kubernetes or are planning how to recover from a ransomware attack.

    1. 0:53 Yang says GitOps can recreate manifests, but secrets and data in persistent volumes are not in Git, which is why the group builds Kubernetes-native data protection blocks.
    2. 5:47 Yang highlights COSI v1alpha2 support for one bucket access covering multiple buckets with read-write, write-only and read-only modes.

    4. 14:08 Smith-Uchida describes ransomware as an adversarial problem where the control plane and backups may be compromised and must be verified before restoring.
    5. 15:52 Smith-Uchida recommends immutable backups, such as S3 compliance mode, and moving backup tools from static API keys to short-lived tokens obtained via OIDC.

    Longhorn: Intro, Deep Dive and Q&A

    Shuo Wu, David Ko

    David Ko introduces Longhorn, a cloud native distributed block storage system for Kubernetes, and shares its release cadence, adoption numbers and contributor growth. He then compares the V1 and SPDK-based V2 data engines, explains snapshots, backups, disaster recovery and live upgrades, and outlines the 1.13 and 1.14 roadmap with early RDMA benchmarks.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Data, storage and stateKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run Longhorn or need block storage on Kubernetes and want to compare the V1 and V2 data engines.

    1. 2:32 Ko says Longhorn runs on any compatible Kubernetes cluster and Linux distribution, including Talos, and ships minor releases every four months in line with Kubernetes.
    2. 4:58 Ko reports adoption up about 21% in six months to roughly 276,000 nodes and 60,000 clusters, from Longhorn's public usage metrics.
    3. 12:22 Ko explains that the V2 data engine uses SPDK in user space with NVMe over Fabrics, plus an interrupt mode so it can share CPU with applications.

    5. 23:44 Ko lists the 1.14 plans, including RDMA support, I/O QoS, a new data engine for strict-local volumes and application-consistent snapshots and backups.

    Yiqing Wang and Adam Buran describe how Roblox shares GPU capacity across clusters using Kueue while leaving each cluster to check its own capacity. They explain how a locality-aware dispatcher chooses clusters and how Dragonfly caches training data and checkpoints near the jobs that use them.

    Watch this if: You operate GPU clusters in more than one location and want to share capacity without repeatedly pulling the same datasets across regions.

    5. 15:55 The speakers report that half of the data served came from node-local cache and another 43 percent from peers in the same cluster, leaving 7 percent served from Amazon S3.

    1. 3:43 Joel says reuse failed when each application team copied and modified patterns, producing more than 135 variants, high cognitive load, configuration drift, and an eight-to-twelve-week path to a usable EKS cluster.

    Tracing at Scale: Running a 16 Billion Spans/Hour Pipeline at Nubank

    Felipe Araujo, Kaynan Coelho

    The speakers describe how Nubank built its distributed tracing pipeline, from instrumenting a handful of shared libraries to an ingestion platform built around the OpenTelemetry Collector, micro-batches on S3 and queues, KEDA scaling and tail sampling with Refinery before data goes to Honeycomb. They share the sampling rules they settled on, cases where traces helped them cut latency and storage, and advice for teams building something similar.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Observability and reliability

    Watch this if: You are scaling distributed tracing past what head sampling and a single vendor pipeline handle and want one bank's architecture and sampling choices.

    1. 1:34 Kaynan Coelho says the pipeline now receives more than 23 billion spans per hour, over 700 terabytes of data a day, and that five engineers work on it full time.
    2. 4:01 Coelho explains that because most Nubank services share the same Clojure template and libraries, they instrumented six core libraries instead of more than 4,000 services.
    3. 8:21 Felipe Araujo says streaming was too expensive and large batches too slow, so the collector writes roughly 10-second micro-batches to S3 and a queue notification tells the next application to pick them up, a pattern he identifies as claim check.
    4. 12:06 Araujo says percentage-based tail sampling hid rare critical paths, so they moved to dynamic rules that keep slow requests and all 500 errors and reduce fast, common successes.
    5. 14:23 Coelho describes using traces to find that some app requests waited on more than 30 content providers, and says the fixes brought latency in those transactions down to about 200 milliseconds.
