    Anatomy of an Outage: When Bursty ML Workloads Break the Kubernetes Scheduler

    Cellan Hall, Jenny Vega, Karol Szuster

    Two engineers from Isomorphic Labs and a Kueue maintainer from Google recount a 10-day outage in which autoscaled inference deployments flooded a cluster with pending Kueue workloads. They walk through its three phases and causes, the upstream fixes in Kueue and Kubernetes, and the cluster, alerting and autoscaling changes they say they made afterward.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingObservability and reliabilityKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: You run autoscaled batch or inference workloads through Kueue and want to see how pending objects can take down scheduling.

    1. 6:35 Jenny Vega says a flood of pending pods and workloads in February 2026 pushed a production cluster into a 10-day outage in three phases, ending with 20 hours of no scheduling.
    2. 8:14 Karol Szuster says the Kueue controller restarts came from one shared client-side rate-limit bucket that also covered leader election, so a storm of reconciles stopped the lease from being renewed.
    3. 9:52 Jenny Vega shows how unschedulable GPU replicas at the head of a shared queue kept admissible CPU work from being reached, and says the team split the queue while Kueue added scheduling equivalence hashing upstream.
    4. 14:38 The speakers explain that after a restart the API server had to call Kueue's conversion webhook for every workload to rebuild its watch cache, which could not finish within the five-minute compaction interval.
    5. 21:06 Cellan Hall says the team now feeds the admitted pod count back into the KEDA scaling trigger, and reports 80 percent fewer pending Kueue workloads and 40 percent fewer workload objects in etcd.

    Multi-Cluster Orchestration System: Karmada Updates and Use Cases

    Joe Nathan Abellard, Hongcai Ren

    Joe Nathan Abellard of Bloomberg, a Karmada maintainer presenting alone because Hongcai Ren could not attend, explains how Karmada propagates workloads across clusters and which recent features target AI workloads. He then describes the managed multi-cluster platform Bloomberg built on Karmada, including its observability and scale-testing setup and a fan-out scheduler he says cut propagation latency, and closes with the 2026 roadmap.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Kubernetes internals and runtimesPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you run or plan a multi-cluster platform and want to see how one company operates Karmada as a managed service and tunes its scheduler under load.

    1. 3:39 Abellard walks through the propagation pipeline: a propagation policy matches a workload, the resource detector creates a resource binding, the scheduler assigns clusters, and work objects are applied to each member cluster.
    2. 6:44 He lists recent features aimed at AI workloads: multi-component scheduling for workloads such as Ray jobs, priority-based scheduling, federated resource quotas, and overflow cluster affinities that use on-prem GPUs before cloud ones.
    3. 11:22 He describes Bloomberg's high-availability topology, where each Karmada control plane is stretched across host clusters in several data centers so the managed service can survive the loss of a whole data center.
    4. 18:43 For scale tests he says they simulate 50 member clusters of 1,000 nodes each with KWOK, with one real node per cluster, and drive load with ClusterLoader2 while a sidecar measures end-to-end propagation latency.
    5. 26:12 He explains the fan-out scheduler built for duplicated placements, which only checks feasibility and so can run many workers; he reports P99 propagation latency under 1 second at 50 objects per second, against about 3 minutes 45 seconds at 20 with the default scheduler.

    Spark on Kubernetes: The Gap Between “It Works” and “It Works Well”

    Damon Cortesi

    Damon Cortesi says Airbnb finished moving its Spark fleet from Hadoop to Kubernetes two weeks before the talk and goes layer by layer through what failed on the way: the control plane, scheduling, nodes, disks, networking, Spark itself and observability. He says most of the 34 changes were small config edits or backported patches that were hard to find, and sums up the lesson as check your config, stress test, repeat.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Data, storage and stateKubernetes internals and runtimesObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you run or plan to run Apache Spark on Kubernetes at scale and want a list of concrete failure points and the fixes one team applied.

    1. 1:23 Cortesi says Airbnb hit 100% two weeks earlier, shutting down its last legacy Hadoop cluster, with a fleet of about 588,000 daily Spark jobs.
    2. 4:55 On the control plane, he says dropping the events TTL from one hour to 10 minutes cut etcd P99 latency from half a second to under 25 milliseconds and the object count from 2 million to 400,000.
    3. 9:06 He describes two Apache YuniKorn problems, one asking for far more capacity than needed because of a Go bug and one asking for too little under FIFO sorting, and says KWOK let him test YuniKorn at scale without provisioning real capacity.
    4. 12:35 He says a Node Problem Detector log rotation bug marked healthy nodes as dead, so that about 74 nodes were removed in 24 hours, one of four node-health bugs they found.
    5. 16:28 He says unique pod labels pushed Cilium past its 65,000 identity limit at their pod creation rates, and the fix was basing identities on a short allow list of labels.

    Volcano for the Agentic AI Era: Unified Scheduling Across Training, Inference, and Agents

    Ricardo Aravena, Rainie Li

    Rainie Li explains how Volcano aims to schedule training, inference and agent workloads on one Kubernetes cluster, using a batch scheduler and an agent scheduler that share nodes through a sharding controller, plus multi-level gang scheduling, network topology hypernodes and gang-aware preemption. Ricardo Aravena then covers how workload controllers such as Kthena, KubeRay and Grove map onto Volcano primitives, the project's benchmarking work and the roadmap.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingAI agents and automationAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: you schedule AI training, inference or agent workloads on shared GPU clusters and want to know what Volcano offers for gang and topology-aware placement.

    1. 3:02 Rainie Li says Volcano uses two schedulers, a batch scheduler for coordinated workloads and an agent scheduler for latency-sensitive pods, with a sharding controller assigning candidate nodes to each and moving them as demand changes.
    2. 6:49 She introduces multi-level gang scheduling, in which a pod group contains subgroups with their own completeness requirements, such as a tensor-parallel group or a prefill or decode replica.
    3. 9:56 She explains gang-aware preemption, which treats victims as groups and simulates placement first, so no pod is evicted if the whole incoming gang cannot fit.
    4. 15:12 Ricardo Aravena says KubeRay support is merged upstream and listed for Volcano 1.16, and walks through how its head and worker groups translate into Volcano subgroups.
    5. 18:35 He describes the scheduling benchmark framework shipped with Volcano 1.15, which reports scheduling latency and binding throughput on kind, KWOK or an existing cluster, and notes it says nothing about GPU job speed.

    Zero-Ops Disaster Recovery: A Paved Path Through Multi-Cluster Orchestration

    Joe Nathan Abellard, Li Zhuyu

    Joe Nathan Abellard and Li Zhuyu describe how Bloomberg runs Karmada as a managed, highly available multi-cluster orchestration layer across data centers. They cover the operator, observability, scale testing with KWOK and ClusterLoader2, and the scheduler changes they made for large fan-out workloads.

    25 min slot · Intermediate · Session

    Observability and reliabilityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you run or plan a multi-cluster Kubernetes platform and want a detailed Karmada case study covering HA, observability and scheduler tuning.

    1. 2:11 The speakers say data center redundancy is a Bloomberg-wide mandate and that they chose Karmada, which has a standard kube API server, its own scheduler and placement driven by propagation policies.
    2. 8:06 They describe a managed Karmada operator they built that gives each tenant its own namespace and control planes, sets up ingress and registers member clusters automatically from a cluster inventory API.
    3. 10:14 They walk through an HA topology where every control plane runs on host clusters in several data centers, sharing a DNS endpoint and an etcd cluster spread over three data centers, and go through which failures it survives.
    4. 19:48 For scale tests they say they use KWOK so only one of each cluster's 1,000 nodes is real, drive load with ClusterLoader2 and measure end-to-end propagation latency with a sidecar controller.
    5. 29:43 They report that a separate fan-out scheduler for duplicated workloads cut P99 propagation latency from over three minutes at 20 objects per second to under one second at 50 objects per second in their tests.
