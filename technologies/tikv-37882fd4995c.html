    Cloud Native: Built by Humans, Engineered to Evolve

    Jeffrey Sica

    Jeffrey Sica argues that cloud native can adapt to AI because the people who maintain its projects keep changing how they work without abandoning human review. He connects agent workloads to familiar distributed systems practices such as limiting blast radius, rolling back quickly, and sandboxing untrusted code.

    10 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    Community and open source adoptionKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you want to connect AI agent workloads to the reliability practices and contribution norms cloud native teams already use.

    1. 2:55 Sica asks the audience to see the people who keep showing up behind each logo in the cloud native landscape.
    2. 4:25 Sica says maintainers responded to AI-generated contributions by writing policies for what a good contribution looks like and what they expect the submitting human to review.
    3. 5:37 Sica uses the term agent speed for the number of machine actions between human decisions and argues that the change matters for software architecture.
    4. 6:30 Sica points to small blast radii, fast iteration and rollback, sandboxing, and APIs as reasons cloud native systems can adapt to agent workloads.
    5. 7:59 Sica argues that Kubernetes fits training and inference because they are distributed systems problems, not because Kubernetes was originally built for AI.

    Explore TAG Workloads Foundation: Core Runtime, Batch Scheduling, and Moar

    Stephen Rust, Yuan Tang, Marlow Warnicke, Rajas Kakodkar, Paco Xu

    Five leads of the CNCF TAG Workloads Foundation explain what a technical advisory group does, what this one covers, and how its batch subproject and white paper initiatives run. They then walk through graduated, incubating and sandbox projects they place in the TAG's scope and describe how newcomers can join meetings, reviews and initiatives.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Community and open source adoptionKubernetes internals and runtimesAccelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: you maintain or use a workload, runtime or scheduling project and want to know what the TAG does and how to take part.

    1. 2:10 Stephen Rust describes a TAG as a technical advisory group that supports CNCF projects and says it is a way to scale advice across many projects with few people.
    2. 5:13 Rust says initiatives are time-bound efforts with a deliverable such as a white paper, while subprojects carry longer-term work, and that the TAG helps projects with their maturity reviews.
    3. 10:23 Marlow Warnicke describes the batch subproject's scheduling capabilities white paper, which she says aims to compare schedulers under different workload mixes, and asks for contributors.
    4. 14:15 Paco Xu lists graduated projects he places in the TAG's scope and says Karmada and Kubeflow graduated this year and KubeVirt graduated recently.
    5. 19:07 Rajas Kakodkar says no single role or expertise is needed to get involved, and suggests joining meetings, sharing pain points, demoing a project or reviewing white papers.

    The Storage Bottleneck AI Didn't See Coming: Inside StepFun’s Multimodal Production Stack

    Joe Zhou, Frank Li

    Frank Li explains why StepFun needed one file access layer for data preparation, training, checkpoints, and model loading across GPU clusters. Joe Zhou walks through the JuiceFS and TiKV architecture and reports what changed after StepFun tuned caching and metadata handling and contributed improvements upstream.

    3 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    AI inference and servingData, storage and state

    Watch this if: you want an example of diagnosing and improving storage bottlenecks in a multimodal AI pipeline.

    1. 0:55 Li describes how storage needs change from data preparation to training, checkpoint writes, and inference deployment.
    2. 1:23 Li says metadata overhead and unnecessary reads and writes became bottlenecks, prompting StepFun to choose a unified file layer.
    3. 1:56 Zhou breaks down JuiceFS into the client, a pluggable metadata engine, and backing storage.
    4. 2:48 Zhou explains how Kubernetes mounts provide unified file access and how a CSI driver DaemonSet automates local cache configuration.
    5. 3:33 Zhou reports more than ten thousand concurrent mount points, better read-only metadata performance, and lower read amplification after StepFun's changes.
