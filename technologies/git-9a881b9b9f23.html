    Agentic Ops: Building Guardrails for Autonomous AI with Policy-as-Code

    Pavan Madduri

    The speaker describes the guardrails he wants before agents act on production clusters: GitOps-only writes, a shadow mode, least privilege and Kyverno policies as a deterministic ring around the agent. He frames unsafe agent actions as a permissions problem, not a model problem, and sorts actions into ones the agent may take alone, ones that need human approval and ones that are always denied.

    25 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Security, identity and policyAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: You are considering letting AI agents remediate incidents on Kubernetes and want a staged, policy-backed way to limit what they can do.

    1. 0:56 The speaker describes an agent that, when a restart did not clear an alert, scaled the workload down and deleted its PVC, causing a data recovery incident.
    2. 3:21 He lays out autonomy levels from L0, where humans do everything, through suggestions and shadow mode to L3 with guardrails, and says he would not suggest L4 full autonomy without concrete guardrails.
    3. 4:56 He sets the rule that the agent only writes to Git, so every fix becomes a pull request that a human approves and a policy check validates before it reaches the cluster.
    4. 12:20 He shows a hard-deny Kyverno policy that matches every service account in the agent namespace and blocks actions on PVCs, namespaces, StatefulSets, roles and role bindings.
    5. 14:00 He sorts actions into three colors: green ones such as restarts and bounded scaling the agent does alone, amber ones that need human approval, and red ones such as data deletion that are always denied.

    GitOps Meltdown: How a Commit Triggered a Cascading Delete of 39 Clusters in Production

    Deepak Goel

    Deepak Goel tells the story of an incident in which, he says, a Git commit that accidentally removed a top-level Flux Kustomization caused Flux to prune a management cluster's whole tree, including the Git server, Flux itself and 39 workload clusters. He walks through the two waves of deletion, the multi-day recovery in which application data was found in the storage system's recycle bin, and the lessons he draws about blast radius, guarded deletes and rehearsed recovery.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Observability and reliabilityDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: you run clusters with Flux and Cluster API and want to see how one bad commit can delete a fleet, and what the speaker changed afterward.

    1. 6:12 Goel explains that when a Flux Kustomization with pruning enabled is removed from the Git source, Flux deletes everything in that Kustomization's inventory and then the object itself.
    2. 11:00 He names three couplings behind the incident: the Git server ran on the management cluster Flux managed, Flux's own configuration came from Git, and one top-level Kustomization was parent to all the others.
    3. 15:07 He says that when support restarted Flux to fix the outage, the new Flux instance saw the objects marked for deletion and finished deleting them.
    4. 19:24 He says the daily Velero backups had never been tested and were of little use, and that the application data was finally recovered from the recycle bin of the underlying storage system.
    5. 22:00 His first lessons are to shrink the blast radius with one Kustomization per cluster and to make bulk deletion harder, for example by disabling prune or setting the deletion policy to orphan.

    Kubernetes Secrets Were a Bad Idea. Now What?

    Maxime Coquerel

    Maxime Coquerel argues, as his own opinion, that Kubernetes Secrets are risky because they are only base64 encoded in etcd, readable with broad RBAC permissions and have no lifecycle. He recommends encrypting etcd with a customer managed key, keeping secrets out of Git and mounting secrets from a real vault at runtime with the Secrets Store CSI Driver or the OpenBao agent.

    5 min slot · Beginner · Lightning Talk

    Security, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you are deciding whether Kubernetes Secrets are good enough for your platform or which alternative to use.

    1. 0:43 Maxime Coquerel says a Kubernetes Secret is stored in etcd only base64 encoded, not encrypted, and that etcd is a key value store, not a vault.
    2. 1:46 He lists the weaknesses he sees, including that anyone with get, list or watch permissions can read secret contents.
    3. 2:05 He adds that Kubernetes Secrets have no lifecycle: no expiration date and no automatic renewal.
    4. 2:47 His first recommendation is to encrypt etcd with a customer managed key, which he says managed services such as Azure Kubernetes Service support.
    5. 3:49 He compares Sealed Secrets, SOPS and External Secrets Operator, and recommends mounting secrets as files at runtime with the Secrets Store CSI Driver or the OpenBao agent.
