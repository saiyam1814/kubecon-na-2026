    I Built an AI Running Coach: A Personal kagent Agent in Production

    Adam Gordon Bell

    Adam Gordon Bell of Pulumi tells how he built a personal AI running coach that messages him in Slack, first as a Lambda function and then as a single long-running kagent session on a small Kubernetes cluster, to train for a trail race against a friend. He describes the design choices he says worked for him, shows his own training graphs from the year, and encourages the audience to build small agents for their own needs.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you want a small, concrete example of a long-running personal agent on Kubernetes, told as a story with the design choices that kept it simple.

    1. 9:15 Bell describes his first version: an AWS Lambda called by a Strava webhook, a scraper for his Peloton treadmill data, a morning cron job, DynamoDB for storage and one LLM call that posted to Slack.
    2. 12:22 He shows the kagent version, an agent defined as one Kubernetes resource deployed with Pulumi, where the Strava hook, the Peloton sync and a morning cron all post messages into the running session over the agent-to-agent protocol.
    3. 13:54 His main lesson is to use deterministic code wherever possible: the MCP tool parses raw run data and hands the agent only the date, duration, incline and heart rate zones.
    4. 15:25 He keeps one session going indefinitely and handles memory with a custom compaction rule under fixed headings, plus tools that let the agent write to a plan file and an athlete file.
    5. 27:47 He reports finishing the 30K race in 4:30 against his friend's 4:41 from the previous year, after a year in which he says his weeks with three or more runs almost doubled.

    In-Toto: Experiences a Few Million Attestations Later

    Santiago Torres-Arias, James Carnegie, Sivana Hamer, Aditya Sirish A Yelgundhalli

    Santiago Torres-Arias moderates a panel on how in-toto attestations are used in practice, with James Carnegie describing Datadog's internal deployment, Aditya Sirish A Yelgundhalli speaking as a maintainer and a Bloomberg engineer, and Sivana Hamer sharing her research on attestations found in the wild. The panelists discuss making attestations easy to create, a fragmented storage picture, the coming policy framework, how consumers can trust public attestations, and what agent-written code changes.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Panel

    Security, identity and policyDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: you produce or consume software supply chain attestations and want to hear how two companies and a researcher see in-toto adoption, storage and policy.

    1. 8:08 Carnegie says Datadog made attestations easy to create by binding ephemeral keys to OIDC, and that it now collects source attestations for every commit and over 2 million attestations a month.
    2. 9:21 He says Datadog began evaluating policy at deployment time, first in monitoring mode and then enforcing, and now also checks policy before pull requests merge, in CI and at runtime.
    3. 14:46 Hamer says her research found 42 million attestations in the wild as of June across almost 150,000 components, often produced by tooling such as npm provenance or PyPI trusted publishing without users knowing.
    4. 17:00 Yelgundhalli explains that the in-toto policy framework sets requirements such as artifact rules without mandating one policy engine or language, and that verification itself emits an attestation.
    5. 21:33 Carnegie says consumers lack a standard way to learn which attestations a producer should publish and with which keys; Datadog distributes keys and policies internally with TUF, which he rarely sees in open source.

    Tracing at Scale: Running a 16 Billion Spans/Hour Pipeline at Nubank

    Felipe Araujo, Kaynan Coelho

    The speakers describe how Nubank built its distributed tracing pipeline, from instrumenting a handful of shared libraries to an ingestion platform built around the OpenTelemetry Collector, micro-batches on S3 and queues, KEDA scaling and tail sampling with Refinery before data goes to Honeycomb. They share the sampling rules they settled on, cases where traces helped them cut latency and storage, and advice for teams building something similar.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Observability and reliability

    Watch this if: You are scaling distributed tracing past what head sampling and a single vendor pipeline handle and want one bank's architecture and sampling choices.

    1. 1:34 Kaynan Coelho says the pipeline now receives more than 23 billion spans per hour, over 700 terabytes of data a day, and that five engineers work on it full time.
    2. 4:01 Coelho explains that because most Nubank services share the same Clojure template and libraries, they instrumented six core libraries instead of more than 4,000 services.
    3. 8:21 Felipe Araujo says streaming was too expensive and large batches too slow, so the collector writes roughly 10-second micro-batches to S3 and a queue notification tells the next application to pick them up, a pattern he identifies as claim check.
    4. 12:06 Araujo says percentage-based tail sampling hid rare critical paths, so they moved to dynamic rules that keep slow requests and all 500 errors and reduce fast, common successes.
    5. 14:23 Coelho describes using traces to find that some app requests waited on more than 30 content providers, and says the fixes brought latency in those transactions down to about 200 milliseconds.
