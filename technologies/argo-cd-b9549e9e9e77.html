    Automated Resource Allocation Driven by Service Level Objectives

    Pedro Célestin, Julia Furst Morgado

    Pedro Célestin and Julia Furst Morgado present a case study in letting teams specify service objectives and using measured performance to test resource changes automatically. They describe using error budgets and production canaries to test proposed changes instead of letting an AI agent change allocation directly.

    25 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Observability and reliabilityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: You want to turn service objectives into allocation decisions without handing an AI agent direct control of production.

    1. 2:55 The speakers translate an example latency goal into an error budget, then explain how budget burn could inform whether to add or reclaim resources.
    2. 7:20 They propose canarying a resource change, not just a new build. They explain that if more resources do not improve the service-level indicator, the cause may instead be a bad release.
    3. 9:45 They describe the division of work in their case study: developers set the service objective, while the platform tests its own allocation proposals and can keep or reverse a change based on measured results.
    4. 11:28 They say letting AI change production allocation directly did not work well for them, so they limited it to proposing changes that a production canary could verify.
    5. 13:25 They walk through their stack: Prometheus collects metrics, Argo CD deploys changes, Argo Rollouts runs canaries, and Knative is used in some cases rather than being required.

    Beyond End User: CAP and the Evolution of Open Source at CVS Health

    Michael Amundson, Jim Beyers

    Jim Beyers and Michael Amundson of CVS Health present CAP, the internal application platform they say lets a new hire ship to production on day one by hiding Kubernetes, Istio, DNS and certificates behind one app model. They demo creating and deploying an app, explain how CAP spreads services across clusters, and announce their first open source projects, Flag Controller and the CAP Registry API.

    5 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    Community and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you build an internal developer platform and want to see one that hides Kubernetes, Istio and DNS behind a single app model.

    1. 0:25 Jim Beyers says the CVS Health DevEx team built CAP, the CAP Application Platform, in about 10 months, and that it now serves hundreds of engineers and tens of thousands of deployments a month.
    2. 0:58 He says their feature flagging platform, built with OpenFeature, handles hundreds of millions of flag evaluations a day, and that CVS Health has joined CNCF as a platinum member.
    3. 1:45 Michael Amundson gives the question CAP is measured against, whether a new hire can ship to production on their first morning, and its tenets: no prerequisites, an abstract app model, and governance built into the platform.
    4. 3:08 Demo: a new app gets a Docusaurus quick start service, CAP creates the repo and CI pipeline, then deploys it to a cluster with a DNS name and TLS certificate in a couple of minutes.
    5. 5:30 CVS Health announces its first open source DevEx projects, the Flag Controller feature flagging product and the CAP Registry API, and says it plans to open source CAP fully next year.

    Arndt Schwenkschuster

    Arndt Schwenkschuster explains why a shared controller identity can be too broad when a Kubernetes operator acts for individual objects. He describes how the SPIFFE Broker API could give those objects separate identities and discusses uses in Flux, Istio, and secrets management.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Kubernetes internals and runtimesSecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you need a Kubernetes operator to access an external system without giving every object the same identity.

    1. 6:33 Schwenkschuster explains that assigning one service account to the controller makes onboarding easy but can give different teams' objects the same access to external Git repositories.
    2. 8:45 Schwenkschuster shows how a controller could act as a broker, requesting a SPIFFE ID for a specific GitRepository object so the external system can authorize that object rather than the whole controller.

    5. 14:51 Schwenkschuster describes work on Istio ambient mode in which ztunnel would use a pod reference to request the pod's identity rather than authenticating as ztunnel itself.

    10 min slot · Intermediate · Lightning Talk

    2. 4:10 He describes rebuilding around one OpenCode harness and a per-session sandbox, where Sandy can store full logs on disk and pull only the relevant lines into its context.

    5. 8:56 He argues for treating the internal agent as a product that keeps evolving, after putting Rufus into maintenance mode made later improvements harder to adopt.

    We Have GitOps at Home: How Meta Went from Terraform on Cron to GitOps for AI Infra

    Phong Nguyen, Shaun Hopper

    Phong Nguyen and Shaun Hopper explain how Meta's AI infrastructure team overloaded Terraform layers to deploy Kubernetes apps, so a one-line Helm change could take hours or get stuck. They describe moving app delivery to Kargo and Argo CD with a config sync tool, a pipeline.yaml order form and a managed Argo CD service, and say deploy times dropped from hours to minutes.

    5 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    Accelerators and workload schedulingDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: your Terraform pipeline also deploys your Kubernetes apps and you want a worked path to Argo CD and Kargo.

    1. 0:04 Phong Nguyen says this is Meta's first time on the KubeCon keynote stage and that Meta's Kubernetes footprint grew fast over the past year.
    2. 0:51 Shaun Hopper says each research and inference cluster is split into about 12 Terraform layers that also deploy most of the cluster's apps, which he strongly suggests never doing, since a stuck DaemonSet or cloud SDK error can block a one-line Helm change.
    3. 2:40 Phong describes the new split: Terraform builds cloud infra, Kargo promotes apps and Argo CD deploys them, plus Ketch, which syncs Meta's internal configs into Git and merges layered Helm values.
    4. 3:31 He shows pipeline.yaml, an app order form where developers answer what to deploy, where to run it and how it rolls out, with no Kargo or Argo CD expertise needed.
    5. 3:57 He credits a managed, multi-tenant Argo CD service built inside Meta, and says early results cut deployment time from more than four hours to minutes.
