    Beyond One Model per GPU: Agent-Driven Placement for Maximum GPU Utilization on Kubernetes

    Weiqi Sun, Ratnopam Chakrabarti, Ajay Vohra, Vivek Kolasani, Felipe Garcia

    The speakers from AWS and NVIDIA present a pipeline in which an agent, a constraint solver and real GPU measurements search for ways to place several LLMs on shared GPUs with CUDA MPS while each model still meets its latency targets. They walk through the plan, profile, search and export stages, the hand-off to KAI Scheduler through Helm, and results they measured on L40S and H100 nodes.

    80 min slot · Advanced · Tutorial

    Accelerators and workload schedulingAI inference and servingAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you serve many LLMs on Kubernetes and want a worked method for packing them onto fewer GPUs without breaking latency targets.

    1. 4:16 A speaker sets the running example: 15 models, two L40S machines with 16 GPUs, and the question of the fewest GPUs that still meet each model's service level objective.
    2. 20:19 A speaker says CUDA MPS gives no performance isolation, that the limits are caps and not guarantees, and that this is why every placement is benchmarked.
    3. 24:23 A speaker introduces the solution as a Claude skill split into plugins and commands that runs plan, profile and search stages, with a CP solver proposing placements for the agent to test.
    4. 43:24 A speaker explains that KAI Scheduler does not read the certified export directly, so a deployment adapter turns it into Helm values with one release per model.
    5. 1:02:25 A speaker gives five takeaways from the H100 experiments, including a measured co-location latency cost of about 1.3 to 2.3 times and a preference for MPS over time slicing.

    Dashboards-as-Code: Easy to Ship, Easy to Get Wrong

    Cecil Wöbker

    Cecil Wöbker describes how remberg moved from hand-written Grafana dashboards to scripts in CI that generate per-team dashboards, loaded into the cluster by the Grafana sidecar. He lists the mistakes his engineers ran into, including kube-state-metrics label allow lists, series that change across pod restarts and JSON that Grafana ignores.

    5 min slot · Beginner · Lightning Talk

    Delivery, GitOps and release safetyObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you want engineers to ship their own dashboards as code and avoid common mistakes.

    1. 0:58 Cecil Wöbker says remberg's two hand-written Grafana dashboards were painful to keep current, so they were broken about half the time.
    2. 1:56 He describes scripts in CI/CD, often written with AI, that generate individual dashboards for each team based on the services it owns.
    3. 2:38 He explains the deployment: CI commits the script output, a labeled ConfigMap marks it as a dashboard, and the Grafana sidecar loads it automatically.
    4. 3:30 His first caveat: kube-state-metrics does not export all labels by default, so labels used for joins or filters must be on its allow list.
    5. 4:37 He warns that valid JSON is not always valid for Grafana and that AI tends to add fields from outdated docs, so dashboards still need checking.

    Debugging the Invisible: Standardizing Kubernetes Networking Expertise

    Faseela Kundattil, Marino Wijay, James Spurin, Chad M. Crowell, Christophe Sauthier

    Five panelists who created or teach around the Certified Kubernetes Network Engineer (CKNE) exam discuss why CNCF added a hands-on networking certification and what foundational networking knowledge they think Kubernetes engineers need. They argue that AI tools can speed up troubleshooting but that an engineer still has to judge the suggested fix, and they describe what the exam covers and how to prepare.

    30 min slot · Beginner · Panel

    Networking and connectivityCommunity and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you are deciding whether to study for the CKNE exam, or you come from traditional networking and want to know how that knowledge carries over to Kubernetes.

    1. 4:10 Sauthier says CKNE is a separate hands-on exam, not an extension of CKA, because practice builds instincts for a 3 a.m. incident and he wants common ground between the older NetOps world and the cloud native one.
    2. 10:20 Spurin says network policy lets you ignore iptables, nftables and eBPF until something breaks, and argues that without that low-level knowledge a person can only approve whatever an AI tool concludes.
    3. 15:34 Kundattil gives the example of an AI tool suggesting to disable mTLS to restore pod-to-pod connectivity in a service mesh, which she calls not the right fix.
    4. 20:11 Sauthier says some exam questions name the project to use while others leave the choice open, for example Cilium or Istio, and Wijay adds that candidates need subnetting, routing and BGP fundamentals and must read a tcpdump.
    5. 28:36 Asked whether CKNE will count toward Kubestronaut, Sauthier says it becomes a requirement for both Kubestronaut and Golden Kubestronaut from February 15.

    How Kubernetes Handles AI Generated Security Reports

    Vyom Yadav, Nathan Herz

    Nathan Herz and Vyom Yadav of the Kubernetes Security Response Committee describe how a more than tenfold rise in vulnerability reports over a year, which they attribute to AI tools, has strained a committee of ten volunteers, and walk through the intake and triage process and where it is bottlenecked. They then cover the threat model behind past Kubernetes CVEs, examples of AI-generated reports that fell outside it, and automation they want to build for duplicate detection, threat model checks and reproduction.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Security, identity and policyCommunity and open source adoptionAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you triage security reports for an open source project, or you submit AI-assisted reports and want to know what maintainers need from them.

    1. 1:00 Nathan Herz says the Kubernetes HackerOne program received 15 reports in August 2025 and 219 a year later, and that only around 1% of reports result in a CVE while about 5% need the committee's triage.
    2. 8:14 He says the committee is ten volunteers with roughly five hours a week each, that a single reporter has sent hundreds of automated reports to the mailing list, and that email intake will be forwarded to HackerOne.
    3. 12:21 He reports that duplicates closed in HackerOne went from 20 in 2025 to 700 in 2026, and sketches a duplicate filter that retrieves similar past reports from a vector database and asks an LLM for a confidence score.
    4. 19:09 Vyom Yadav gives an example of an AI-generated report about name-only matching in the kubectl credential plugin allowlist, behavior he says an official Kubernetes blog post already documented.
    5. 23:13 He suggests projects define their threat model somewhere tools can find it, mentioning the idea of a per-repository threat model Markdown file, and says writing one for every Kubernetes component is no longer realistic.

    Mythos vs Maintainers: Drowning in the Vulnerability Tsunami

    Fabian Kammel, John Kjell

    Fabian Kammel, speaking as a security researcher, and John Kjell, speaking as a maintainer of in-toto's Witness, walk through finding, reporting, triaging, fixing and following up on a vulnerability at a time when they say AI models let anyone file reports that are hard to tell from real ones. They give recommendations for each side in three levels, from steps to adopt today to actions meant to help the wider ecosystem, and ask for empathy toward maintainers, whom they call the bottleneck.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Security, identity and policyCommunity and open source adoption

    Watch this if: you maintain an open source project or report vulnerabilities to one and want both sides' concrete steps for handling AI-assisted security reports.

    1. 3:45 Kjell says AI-generated reports were easy to spot and close a year ago, but that newer models let novices submit reports that look real and take a lot of effort to verify, and that curl and bug bounty programs have pulled back as a result.
    2. 8:25 Kammel says the first thing he does for AI-assisted research is build a sandbox, preferring micro VMs over sandboxes that share the host kernel, and that researchers should understand and reproduce a model's finding before reporting it.
    3. 10:40 Kjell advises maintainers to turn on GitHub's private vulnerability reporting and publish a security policy with a response time frame, report expectations, supported versions, scope and a threat model in the repository.
    4. 14:34 Kammel lists what a researcher's report should do: follow the project's security policy, check for duplicates, cut the model's text down, and propose a severity score, affected versions and a weakness type.
    5. 24:53 Kjell shows that a GitHub temporary private fork has no Actions tab, and says the Witness maintainers keep their own detached fork so that fixes can run through CI before release.

    Juho Syrjänen

He describes how module version drift and agent-generated pull requests strain the module-per-repository model, and shows an approach using Kratix where a short custom resource triggers the existing code.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you maintain Terraform or OpenTofu modules and Helm charts and want a way to turn them into a self-service platform without a rewrite.

    1. 3:11 The speaker describes the tempting plan of rebuilding everything with Crossplane, kro, Backstage and Kratix over a couple of years, and the pushback he says that proposal gets.
    2. 5:29 The speaker says the engineering behind existing infrastructure as code is solid and asks why it should not be reused.

    5. 19:19 The speaker says users and agents can then act on the platform without extra pull request approvals because a human already approved the platform code, then names Kratix and Crossplane as tools that help.

    The Missing Layer of Platform Maturity: Kubernetes Networking

    Faseela Kundattil, Christophe Sauthier, Chad M. Crowell, Marino Wijay, James Spurin

    The panel explains why Kubernetes networking remains a maturity gap despite higher-level abstractions, using incidents and observability workflows to show why engineers still need mental models of the underlying stack. The panelists describe the hands-on CKNE exam as a vendor-flexible way to validate networking skills while arguing that AI should assist rather than replace engineering judgment.

    35 min slot · Any Level · Panel

    Networking and connectivityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: your platform team owns Kubernetes networking troubleshooting or is evaluating the CKNE curriculum.

    1. 1:41 The panel argues that Kubernetes networking is a missing layer of platform maturity because usable abstractions still require underlying troubleshooting knowledge when failures occur.
    2. 10:08 Chad uses a 504 ingress incident to argue for peeling back the network path locally, from pod logs and events through service endpoints to ingress routing and host-header behavior.
    3. 15:21 James argues that engineers should learn enough iptables, nftables, conntrack, and eBPF to understand failures and judge AI-suggested fixes rather than treating AI as the authority.
    4. 17:35 Chad argues that knowing where in the networking stack a problem lives matters more than loyalty to a toolset, with observability used to narrow environment and namespace before deep packet inspection.
    5. 23:31 Christophe says SMEs from varied projects designed the hands-on CKNE exam so candidates can answer with different tools rather than being forced into one stack.
