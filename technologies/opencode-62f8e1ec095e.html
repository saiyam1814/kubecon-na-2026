    Don’t Lose Your Agent’s Mind: Platform Patterns for Stateful Agents

    Evaline Ju, Maia Iyer

    The speakers explain why stateful agents lose sessions, files, and context when treated like ordinary pods, and they examine community work on sandboxing, snapshotting, and resuming agent state. They present their moca prototype and argue for server-held sessions, harness-neutral state, and an open harness-sandbox boundary.

    25 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automationData, storage and state

    Watch this if: you are designing Kubernetes platform support for stateful or delegated AI agents.

    1. 4:00 The speakers say a plain pod is a poor fit for stateful agents because deleting the pod discards sessions, files, and learned context.
    2. 5:48 The speakers point to Agent Sandbox templates and Agent Substrate snapshot and resume work as community efforts aimed at configurable isolation and multiplexing idle agents.
    3. 9:19 The speakers present moca as their open-source managed-agents experiment, using Pi for the harness, Valkey for externalized sessions, and a relay boundary into pluggable sandboxes.
    4. 16:10 In the recorded demo, Evaline shows the user closing a session, starting another one, and still picking up the subagent's work without resending context.
    5. 18:41 The speakers argue that platforms should make sessions first-class and server-held, treat agent state as harness-neutral data, and make the harness-sandbox boundary an open protocol, while provenance, cost attribution, legibility, and delegated trust remain open.

    From Clever to Correct: Lessons from Rebuilding SeatGeek's Platform AI Agent

    Colin O'Dell

    Colin O'Dell describes why SeatGeek replaced its specialist-heavy platform agent, Rufus, with a generalist agent called Sandy that can carry a task from investigation through a code change. He explains how a per-session sandbox keeps full logs out of the agent's context and how the team uses reports from Slack or Jira to improve the agent itself.

    10 min slot · Intermediate · Lightning Talk

    AI agents and automationPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: You are deciding what to build yourself when an internal platform agent needs to diagnose problems and make changes.

    1. 1:23 O'Dell explains how Rufus routed requests to four specialists that could not easily hand work off. An Argo CD specialist could diagnose a deployment failure but could not pass the fix to the coding agent.
    2. 4:10 He describes rebuilding around one OpenCode harness and a per-session sandbox, where Sandy can store full logs on disk and pull only the relevant lines into its context.
    3. 6:43 He explains why the team reused OpenCode and Daytona for the harness and sandboxes, leaving more time for platform-specific routines and skills.
    4. 7:05 He describes how the team uses Sandy to fix Sandy: a report from Slack or Jira can start a sandboxed fix and merge request. He also describes a tool that posts a short report when the agent gets stuck.
    5. 8:56 He argues for treating the internal agent as a product that keeps evolving, after putting Rufus into maintenance mode made later improvements harder to adopt.

    The Local AI Renaissance

    Jason Stryker

    Jason Stryker explains why he runs open-weight models on his own hardware and demonstrates a laptop setup that serves a model through KServe and llama.cpp. He compares inference engines and discusses memory and concurrency limits that shape which workloads he runs locally.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and serving

    Watch this if: you want to try serving open-weight models on hardware you already own.

    1. 3:34 Stryker distinguishes local models, which run on your own hardware, from models you reach through a hosted subscription.
    2. 13:40 He identifies memory as a major constraint for local inference and explains why it matters for the model he can run in the demo.
    3. 14:39 He introduces KServe as the layer that runs inference engines on his local Kubernetes cluster.
    4. 17:11 He turns off Wi-Fi and shows the local inference request and service logs continuing without an internet connection.
    5. 23:55 He introduces vLLM as an option when concurrency matters and contrasts its key-value cache approach with llama.cpp.
