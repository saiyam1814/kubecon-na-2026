    Data-Driven Platform Guardrails: What 16,500 Incidents Tell You to Automate First

    Henrique Santana, Henrique Dalssaso

    Henrique Santana and Henrique Dalssaso walk through the top root causes they found in more than 16,000 Kubernetes incidents, including IP exhaustion, auth failures and node provisioning problems. For each one they suggest platform guardrails to automate, and they explain how to run the same analysis on your own incident data.

    25 min slot · Intermediate · Session

    Observability and reliabilityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a list of common Kubernetes incident root causes and the guardrails these speakers suggest automating for each.

    1. 1:35 The speakers describe their dataset as more than 16,000 incidents across many Kubernetes clusters from January to mid-May, grouped with AI and then reviewed by hand. They say DNS did not make their top 10 root causes.
    2. 3:57 They say about a third of one top pattern was pods with no IP address available, from small or shared subnets, and suggest Cilium multi-pool IPAM and broader Karpenter node pools.
    3. 6:59 For auth failures they separate missing permissions, RBAC that was never bound to the pod, and policies on the target endpoint, and suggest Kyverno policies, projected service account tokens and cert-manager.
    4. 16:20 For node provisioning failures, which they put at almost 15% of incidents, they suggest setting kube-reserved and system-reserved, Cluster API machine health checks and Karpenter node auto repair.
    5. 20:59 To repeat the analysis on your own data, they suggest classifying by what actually failed rather than the symptom, ranking by frequency, and asking whether the platform, the app team or both can fix it.

    Least-Privilege by Default: Standardizing Identity-Aware AI Agent Access into a Golden Path

    Masato Kozuka

    Masato Kozuka of Hitachi lays out a security model for AI agents that act for a user, in which every call to an MCP server carries both the user's and the agent's identity and is allowed only if both may do it. He then shows how he would have a platform team make that the default for every team, using Keycloak, SPIRE and Backstage templates, and plays a recorded demo of an attack being stopped.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Security, identity and policyAI agents and automationPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: your teams connect AI agents to internal tools over MCP and you want one standard way to enforce least privilege for all of them.

    1. 4:56 Kozuka explains exchanging the token at every hop with OAuth token exchange, so each token is valid for one MCP server only while the sub claim stays the user.
    2. 5:40 He calls letting the agent log in as itself with the client credentials grant a confused deputy, and says they disable agent service accounts and reject any token without a user.
    3. 7:12 He describes proving the agent's identity with SPIFFE: the pod's namespace and image digest are checked before it gets a short-lived ID, in place of a static key anyone could copy.
    4. 13:59 He splits the seven rules: three are enforced in Keycloak and SPIRE where no agent can skip them, and four ship in the sidecar and server middleware that Backstage templates generate.
    5. 25:06 In the recorded demo a receipt with a hidden instruction sends the agent after payroll data, and Kozuka shows Keycloak refusing a token for the payroll server because it was never approved.

    Sameera Jayasoma

    Sameera presents OpenChoreo as a Kubernetes-native control plane that hides tooling complexity behind guarded abstractions for humans and agents. He argues that agents should become first-class platform workloads and shows how catalogs, deployment state, logs, metrics, cost insights, and audit data can share those abstractions.

    5 min slot · Any Level · Keynote

    AI agents and automationPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you are evaluating control-plane abstractions that serve both developers and AI agents.

    1. 1:20 Sameera says platform maintainability becomes difficult when added capabilities require repeated changes to glue code across portals and pipelines.
    2. 2:18 Sameera argues that exposing every underlying tool directly to agents can work but wastes iterations and tokens, while a smaller set of guarded abstractions gives agents a controlled interface.
    3. 3:36 Sameera presents OpenChoreo as a control-plane middle layer that abstracts the tooling layer and compiles higher-level concepts toward Kubernetes while preserving their meaning for status, logs, and metrics.
    4. 5:29 Sameera says OpenChoreo expands its control plane to treat agents as first-class workloads because they can run untrusted or unpredictable code and need runtimes such as Agent Sandbox and Agent Substrate.
    5. 5:56 Sameera introduces a quick portal demo built around a CLI and Backstage-based plugins, showing catalogs, component deployment state, logs, metrics, cost insights, and audit data.

    William Rizzo of Mirantis and Kasper Borg Nissen of Dash0 argue that limits on AI agents in regulated industries should be enforced by the platform and not by the agent framework or its system prompt. They demo an insurance claims setup in which, as they describe it, agents get SPIFFE identities, reach tools only through a gateway with a hard payout ceiling, and leave an audit trail in OpenTelemetry traces.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Security, identity and policyAI agents and automationPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you have to put AI agents on a platform in a regulated business and need to show auditors what an agent did and who allowed it.

    1. 9:01 Rizzo describes the agent's identity as a passport: a SPIFFE-validated identity with no stored passwords, with Linkerd restricting which paths the agent can reach.
    2. 11:27 Nissen shows the two controls they set in agentgateway: a CEL rule with a hard 50,000 euro payout ceiling and an MCP guardrail that calls their own policy decision service.
    3. 13:00 Nissen explains using an OpenFeature flag to set how much autonomy the agent has, from every claim needing human approval to automatic payouts up to a set amount.

    5. 23:41 Nissen shows the policy decision recorded as a custom span with attributes defined through OpenTelemetry Weaver, and the human approval linked to it as a second trace.

    What Customer Success Knows That Platform Engineering Keeps Relearning

    Ryan Etten

    Ryan Etten of Broadcom maps customer success practices onto platform engineering, following a composite team through onboarding, adoption, expansion and renewal. He argues that dashboards and mandates hide quiet churn, and offers plays such as pairing with stalled teams and comparing each team with its own baseline.

    25 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: platform adoption is your problem and you want customer success plays you can apply to internal developer teams.

    1. 2:03 Etten maps customer success terms onto platforms: the champion is the tech lead who argued for you, the contract is a mandate, and renewal is the day a team stops deploying through you. He follows a composite payments team through the talk.
    2. 7:52 He says a team counts as onboarded at its first production change through the platform, and suggests that if a team has not shipped by week two, a person sits with them instead of sending automated emails.
    3. 11:30 He suggests comparing each team with its own baseline rather than the platform average, saying averages are where churn hides, and reaching out when a team drops.
    4. 17:15 He says mandates produce hostages, a customer success term he credits to a 1995 Harvard Business Review paper, and points to Backstage at Spotify as a tool that was not mandated internally.
    5. 21:24 He says the CNCF Platform Engineering Maturity Model does not use words like onboarding, retention or churn, and proposes a sixth aspect on retention.

    Why Are You Still Wasting Whole GPUs? Hardware-Level Sharing, Scheduled with DRA

    Engin Diri

    Engin Diri of Pulumi recounts helping a European customer build a self-hosted inference platform, moving from whole GPUs and time slicing to NVIDIA MIG slices that pods request through Dynamic Resource Allocation. He shows a recorded demo of MIG profiles, resource slices and claims on an AWS node serving a model behind an inference gateway, and lists open questions such as quotas, models larger than a node and start-up ordering.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: you run inference on your own GPUs and want a practical walk through MIG slices requested with Dynamic Resource Allocation.

    1. 8:32 Diri says time slicing gives no memory or fault isolation between workloads, so the choice was between wasting GPUs and risking a noisy neighbor.
    2. 11:24 Diri says Dynamic Resource Allocation, which he dates to Kubernetes 1.34, lets a request describe device attributes instead of an integer count.
    3. 22:16 In the recorded demo, Diri lists the 1G, 2G and 4G MIG profiles on the node and the resource slices the DRA driver published.
    4. 24:28 Diri lists open day-two questions he is evaluating with the customer, including Kueue for MIG quota and which workload runs first.
    5. 28:06 Diri advises starting the DRA driver only after MIG is configured, because he ran into race conditions between the two.
