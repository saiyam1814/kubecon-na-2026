    In-Toto: Experiences a Few Million Attestations Later

    Santiago Torres-Arias, James Carnegie, Sivana Hamer, Aditya Sirish A Yelgundhalli

    Santiago Torres-Arias moderates a panel on how in-toto attestations are used in practice, with James Carnegie describing Datadog's internal deployment, Aditya Sirish A Yelgundhalli speaking as a maintainer and a Bloomberg engineer, and Sivana Hamer sharing her research on attestations found in the wild. The panelists discuss making attestations easy to create, a fragmented storage picture, the coming policy framework, how consumers can trust public attestations, and what agent-written code changes.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Panel

    Security, identity and policyDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: you produce or consume software supply chain attestations and want to hear how two companies and a researcher see in-toto adoption, storage and policy.

    1. 8:08 Carnegie says Datadog made attestations easy to create by binding ephemeral keys to OIDC, and that it now collects source attestations for every commit and over 2 million attestations a month.
    2. 9:21 He says Datadog began evaluating policy at deployment time, first in monitoring mode and then enforcing, and now also checks policy before pull requests merge, in CI and at runtime.
    3. 14:46 Hamer says her research found 42 million attestations in the wild as of June across almost 150,000 components, often produced by tooling such as npm provenance or PyPI trusted publishing without users knowing.
    4. 17:00 Yelgundhalli explains that the in-toto policy framework sets requirements such as artifact rules without mandating one policy engine or language, and that verification itself emits an attestation.
    5. 21:33 Carnegie says consumers lack a standard way to learn which attestations a producer should publish and with which keys; Datadog distributes keys and policies internally with TUF, which he rarely sees in open source.

    Kubernetes Data Protection WG Intro & Deep Dive

    Xing Yang, Dave Smith-Uchida

    Xing Yang and Dave Smith-Uchida explain why the Kubernetes Data Protection Working Group formed and walk through the backup and restore building blocks it drives, including COSI, snapshot topology, changed block tracking and volume group snapshots. Smith-Uchida then presents two white papers in progress, on data protection best practices and on recovering from ransomware attacks.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Data, storage and stateKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you back up stateful workloads on Kubernetes or are planning how to recover from a ransomware attack.

    1. 0:53 Yang says GitOps can recreate manifests, but secrets and data in persistent volumes are not in Git, which is why the group builds Kubernetes-native data protection blocks.
    2. 5:47 Yang highlights COSI v1alpha2 support for one bucket access covering multiple buckets with read-write, write-only and read-only modes.
    3. 8:40 Yang explains changed block tracking, which lets backup vendors copy only the blocks that changed between two snapshots through a new CSI snapshot metadata service.
    4. 14:08 Smith-Uchida describes ransomware as an adversarial problem where the control plane and backups may be compromised and must be verified before restoring.
    5. 15:52 Smith-Uchida recommends immutable backups, such as S3 compliance mode, and moving backup tools from static API keys to short-lived tokens obtained via OIDC.

    LitmusChaos 2026: What’s New & What’s Next

    Pritesh Kiri

    Pritesh Kiri, a LitmusChaos community manager and maintainer, reviews the project's year, citing growth in GitHub stars and contributions, new adopters and a keynote at KubeCon India. He then covers an MCP server, project and security updates, and Litmus Light, a lightweight binary he says runs chaos locally in a dev environment.

    5 min slot · Level not listed · Lightning Talk

    Observability and reliabilityCommunity and open source adoption

    Watch this if: You use or are evaluating LitmusChaos and want the past year's changes and the new Litmus Light tool in five minutes.

    1. 0:26 Pritesh Kiri says the project saw around 17% more GitHub stars and 33% more contributions over the year.
    2. 0:52 He names Canonical, Motorola and iFood among the new adopters onboarded last year.
    3. 1:26 He says a new MCP server lets users list and describe experiments, explore probes and get more insights than the UI gives.
    4. 2:11 He lists project updates including native Prometheus metrics from an LFX mentorship, monthly CVE patches, a Helm installation method for chaos infrastructure and Kubernetes Jobs as chaos targets.
    5. 4:17 He describes Litmus Light as a lightweight binary installed in your application to run chaos locally in a dev environment before shipping, aimed at developers and AI agents.
