    Engineering High-Performance Telemetry Pipelines: Lessons from Fluent Bit

    Eduardo Silva

    Eduardo Silva, creator and maintainer of Fluent Bit, walks through how a telemetry pipeline is built and what changed in the 5.2 release, including a new Lua scripting interface for logs, metrics and traces and a WebAssembly build that runs in the browser. He then describes the performance work behind the release, which he says came from profiling with perf, removing repeated encoding steps, pre-allocating memory and batching.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Observability and reliability

    Watch this if: you run or build telemetry collectors and want to hear how the Fluent Bit maintainer profiles and removes CPU and memory overhead.

    1. 7:39 Silva introduces a new Lua scripting interface he calls FTS, which he says adds primitives for metrics and traces as well as logs, something scripts could not work with before.
    2. 11:13 He announces Fluent Bit Studio, an experimental web interface that he says runs Fluent Bit as WebAssembly in the browser so a configuration can be tried before it reaches production.
    3. 15:04 He describes letting an AI coding agent run perf against the code and collect results over repeated runs, and says Fluent Bit ships a benchmark skill others can copy.
    4. 18:13 He explains a fast path that decodes OTLP straight into the internal record format and encodes to MessagePack once, instead of encoding twice as before.
    5. 25:44 He compares Fluent Bit 5.2 with 5.1 and the OpenTelemetry Collector on OTLP processing and says the process of measuring mattered more than any single change.

    Exploring Telemetry Quality

    Jacob Aronoff

    Jacob Aronoff runs an interactive session on why telemetry quality matters, using audience polls and an incident from his own experience where bad telemetry overwhelmed the collectors. He then covers OpenTelemetry semantic conventions and Weaver, and introduces the policies work he and other OpenTelemetry maintainers are specifying to enforce quality rules inside the pipeline.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Birds of a Feather

    Observability and reliability

    Watch this if: you own an observability pipeline and struggle with inconsistent names, PII in logs or runaway cardinality, and want to know what OpenTelemetry is building to enforce quality rules.

    1. 3:23 Aronoff recounts an incident in which retries and stack traces overwhelmed OpenTelemetry collectors that had no persistent queuing, telemetry was dropped, and three emitters using three different names for pods made joins across the data nearly impossible.
    2. 11:25 He describes Weaver as a tool that lets an organization define its own semantic conventions with a schema, check them with custom Rego, generate types and docs, and live-check telemetry locally.
    3. 18:11 He introduces OpenTelemetry policies as matching rules plus actions that he says are meant to be portable across collectors and other pipelines, pushed dynamically without restarts, fail-open and testable locally.
    4. 24:58 He shows benchmarks in which, by his account, the Collector with OTTL and Vector with VRL stall after about one to two thousand rules, and says the policy implementation compiles rules into a Hyperscan regex database to avoid that.
    5. 26:17 On adoption, he says the OTEP has been merged, the protobufs and specification are in progress, sample libraries exist in Go, Rust and Zig, and Collector support is the nearer-term goal with SDKs hoped for next year.

    GitOps Meltdown: How a Commit Triggered a Cascading Delete of 39 Clusters in Production

    Deepak Goel

    Deepak Goel tells the story of an incident in which, he says, a Git commit that accidentally removed a top-level Flux Kustomization caused Flux to prune a management cluster's whole tree, including the Git server, Flux itself and 39 workload clusters. He walks through the two waves of deletion, the multi-day recovery in which application data was found in the storage system's recycle bin, and the lessons he draws about blast radius, guarded deletes and rehearsed recovery.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Observability and reliabilityDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: you run clusters with Flux and Cluster API and want to see how one bad commit can delete a fleet, and what the speaker changed afterward.

    1. 6:12 Goel explains that when a Flux Kustomization with pruning enabled is removed from the Git source, Flux deletes everything in that Kustomization's inventory and then the object itself.
    2. 11:00 He names three couplings behind the incident: the Git server ran on the management cluster Flux managed, Flux's own configuration came from Git, and one top-level Kustomization was parent to all the others.
    3. 15:07 He says that when support restarted Flux to fix the outage, the new Flux instance saw the objects marked for deletion and finished deleting them.
    4. 19:24 He says the daily Velero backups had never been tested and were of little use, and that the application data was finally recovered from the recycle bin of the underlying storage system.
    5. 22:00 His first lessons are to shrink the blast radius with one Kustomization per cluster and to make bulk deletion harder, for example by disabling prune or setting the deletion policy to orphan.

    Hidden Pod Startup Latency: Container Image Pull

    Omer Oz

    Omer Oz describes how LinkedIn investigated slow pod startups on its bare-metal fleet and traced most of the time to unpacking container image layers, not downloading them. He walks through the kubelet and containerd settings his team changed, the results he reports, and further options such as zstd compression, the EROFS snapshotter and peer-to-peer caching.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Kubernetes internals and runtimesObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: large container images slow your pod startups and you want a mental model of the pull path plus settings to try.

    1. 9:12 Oz describes the trigger: Flink apps shipping about 28 GB of images against a 20-minute pod startup SLO, which he says caused a series of incidents at LinkedIn.
    2. 14:46 Oz walks through an image pull timeline and says the download finishes early while most time goes to unpacking, which containerd does one layer at a time by default.
    3. 15:26 Oz explains LinkedIn's fix of raising the kubelet's parallel image pulls to three and lowering containerd's download concurrency, then shows the latency results he measured.
    4. 21:46 Oz presents the EROFS snapshotter for containerd, which he says skips eager unpacking and cut unpack time to under 0.4 seconds in his tests.
    5. 23:57 Oz describes a serialization point he found in containerd that capped total download throughput, and points to his pull request removing it.

    Look Before You Leap: Preventing In-Motion K8s Updates in Fleet Robotics

    Tom Ingleby, Rian Bogle

    Rian Bogle and Tom Ingleby of Agility Robotics describe how they run K3s on humanoid robots and manage them from the cloud with GitOps. Ingleby then shows an admission webhook he calls the locomotion guard, which he says rejects cluster changes in restricted namespaces while a robot is walking.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Delivery, GitOps and release safetySecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you run Kubernetes on edge or embedded devices and need to block changes while the device is in a state where a restart is unsafe.

    1. 4:56 Bogle lays out the on-robot stack: K3s on a read-only OS, GitOps with continuous deployment, OCI images, OpenTelemetry and Fluent Bit for telemetry, and Teleport with SPIFFE identities.
    2. 7:44 Ingleby explains the risk they want to avoid: a cluster change that restarts critical software while the robot is balancing could make it fall and damage itself or the customer site.
    3. 11:38 Ingleby describes the locomotion guard, a small admission webhook that queries the robot's control software for its state and rejects changes while it is walking.
    4. 14:43 Ingleby shows the override: an operator creates a ConfigMap that opens a short exemption window, and he says Teleport's audit trail records who did it.
    5. 28:06 Asked whether the webhook fails open or closed, Ingleby says it currently fails open and that they plan to fail closed as confidence grows.

    Securing Rural Colorado with Open Source: Running a Student-Led SOC on Kubernetes

    Maciej Wal

    Maciej Wal describes how the Cybersecurity Center at Metropolitan State University of Denver replaced a vendor's security monitoring service, first with four virtual machines and then with a Kubernetes platform that he says he runs as the one full-time staff member. He covers the outages and adoption problems along the way, and how students learn log analysis and GitOps on the platform that monitors public sector partners.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Security, identity and policyObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you are a very small team weighing Kubernetes for a security monitoring service, or want ideas for teaching students with real infrastructure.

    1. 3:19 The speaker says that after the vendor ended the service he built a replacement in about 66 days on four virtual machines, with WireGuard, single-node OpenSearch and a new ticketing tool.
    2. 6:45 The speaker explains picking the Kubernetes stack from the CNCF landscape, including Keycloak, Argo CD, OpenTofu and Fluent Bit, and says it cost around $1,000 a month.
    3. 8:33 The speaker recounts a flood of Suricata logs taking down the OpenSearch cluster, which he says had too little memory and no autoscaling, and learning of breakage days late.
    4. 12:01 The speaker says one of the hardest problems he found was the gap between offering self-service and people using it, after students did not adopt browser certificate access.
    5. 13:48 The speaker describes a student workshop on Argo CD, Envoy Gateway, cert-manager and GitHub Actions that ended with deploying a Minecraft server through GitOps.

    Spark on Kubernetes: The Gap Between “It Works” and “It Works Well”

    Damon Cortesi

    Damon Cortesi says Airbnb finished moving its Spark fleet from Hadoop to Kubernetes two weeks before the talk and goes layer by layer through what failed on the way: the control plane, scheduling, nodes, disks, networking, Spark itself and observability. He says most of the 34 changes were small config edits or backported patches that were hard to find, and sums up the lesson as check your config, stress test, repeat.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Data, storage and stateKubernetes internals and runtimesObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you run or plan to run Apache Spark on Kubernetes at scale and want a list of concrete failure points and the fixes one team applied.

    1. 1:23 Cortesi says Airbnb hit 100% two weeks earlier, shutting down its last legacy Hadoop cluster, with a fleet of about 588,000 daily Spark jobs.
    2. 4:55 On the control plane, he says dropping the events TTL from one hour to 10 minutes cut etcd P99 latency from half a second to under 25 milliseconds and the object count from 2 million to 400,000.
    3. 9:06 He describes two Apache YuniKorn problems, one asking for far more capacity than needed because of a Go bug and one asking for too little under FIFO sorting, and says KWOK let him test YuniKorn at scale without provisioning real capacity.
    4. 12:35 He says a Node Problem Detector log rotation bug marked healthy nodes as dead, so that about 74 nodes were removed in 24 hours, one of four node-health bugs they found.
    5. 16:28 He says unique pod labels pushed Cilium past its 65,000 identity limit at their pod creation rates, and the fix was basing identities on a short allow list of labels.
