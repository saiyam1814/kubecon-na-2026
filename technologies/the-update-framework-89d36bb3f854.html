    In-Toto: Experiences a Few Million Attestations Later

    Santiago Torres-Arias, James Carnegie, Sivana Hamer, Aditya Sirish A Yelgundhalli

    Santiago Torres-Arias moderates a panel on how in-toto attestations are used in practice, with James Carnegie describing Datadog's internal deployment, Aditya Sirish A Yelgundhalli speaking as a maintainer and a Bloomberg engineer, and Sivana Hamer sharing her research on attestations found in the wild. The panelists discuss making attestations easy to create, a fragmented storage picture, the coming policy framework, how consumers can trust public attestations, and what agent-written code changes.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Panel

    Security, identity and policyDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: you produce or consume software supply chain attestations and want to hear how two companies and a researcher see in-toto adoption, storage and policy.

    1. 8:08 Carnegie says Datadog made attestations easy to create by binding ephemeral keys to OIDC, and that it now collects source attestations for every commit and over 2 million attestations a month.
    2. 9:21 He says Datadog began evaluating policy at deployment time, first in monitoring mode and then enforcing, and now also checks policy before pull requests merge, in CI and at runtime.
    3. 14:46 Hamer says her research found 42 million attestations in the wild as of June across almost 150,000 components, often produced by tooling such as npm provenance or PyPI trusted publishing without users knowing.
    4. 17:00 Yelgundhalli explains that the in-toto policy framework sets requirements such as artifact rules without mandating one policy engine or language, and that verification itself emits an attestation.
    5. 21:33 Carnegie says consumers lack a standard way to learn which attestations a producer should publish and with which keys; Datadog distributes keys and policies internally with TUF, which he rarely sees in open source.

    Supply Chain, Runtime and SDLC: A Cloud Native Security Tour

    Dr. Constanze Roedig

    Constanze Roedig walks through cloud native security in three parts, the software supply chain you consume, the software development lifecycle where you build and deploy, and the runtime, naming CNCF and OpenSSF projects for each. She closes with her view that AI agents put more weight on runtime detection and on protecting and rotating secrets.

    12 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Security, identity and policy

    Watch this if: You find cloud native security overwhelming and want a 16-minute overview of which problem each group of projects addresses.

    1. 0:40 The speaker frames the talk around three areas: the supply chain of software you consume, the software development lifecycle where you produce and deploy it, and the runtime.
    2. 3:44 She says an SBOM declares what is in your software but cannot be assumed complete, and suggests following OpenSSF recommendations on tooling.
    3. 5:51 She distinguishes a signature from an attestation, which she describes as a piece of evidence put in an envelope together with a signature, and names in-toto.
    4. 10:36 For runtime detection she contrasts signatures, which encode known adversary behavior, with anomalies, which are deviations from your own baseline, and says to combine them.
    5. 14:18 She says that with AI agents there are vulnerabilities with no patches available, which in her view puts the spotlight on runtime detection and on short-lived, rotatable secrets.
