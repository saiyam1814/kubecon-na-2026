    Exploring Telemetry Quality

    Jacob Aronoff

    Jacob Aronoff runs an interactive session on why telemetry quality matters, using audience polls and an incident from his own experience where bad telemetry overwhelmed the collectors. He then covers OpenTelemetry semantic conventions and Weaver, and introduces the policies work he and other OpenTelemetry maintainers are specifying to enforce quality rules inside the pipeline.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Birds of a Feather

    Observability and reliability

    Watch this if: you own an observability pipeline and struggle with inconsistent names, PII in logs or runaway cardinality, and want to know what OpenTelemetry is building to enforce quality rules.

    1. 3:23 Aronoff recounts an incident in which retries and stack traces overwhelmed OpenTelemetry collectors that had no persistent queuing, telemetry was dropped, and three emitters using three different names for pods made joins across the data nearly impossible.
    2. 11:25 He describes Weaver as a tool that lets an organization define its own semantic conventions with a schema, check them with custom Rego, generate types and docs, and live-check telemetry locally.
    3. 18:11 He introduces OpenTelemetry policies as matching rules plus actions that he says are meant to be portable across collectors and other pipelines, pushed dynamically without restarts, fail-open and testable locally.
    4. 24:58 He shows benchmarks in which, by his account, the Collector with OTTL and Vector with VRL stall after about one to two thousand rules, and says the policy implementation compiles rules into a Hyperscan regex database to avoid that.
    5. 26:17 On adoption, he says the OTEP has been merged, the protobufs and specification are in progress, sample libraries exist in Go, Rust and Zig, and Collector support is the nearer-term goal with SDKs hoped for next year.

    From Black-Box to Auditable AI-Grid: A Deterministic Governance Layer with Hybrid Cloud

    Fatih E. Nar, Andrew Hendrickson

    The speakers outline a governance layer for AI recommendations in communication networks that makes the decision process auditable without requiring identical model wording. They describe how versioned evidence, policy gates, and energy measurements could feed into decisions about network operations.

    30 min slot · Beginner · Session

    AI agents and automationSecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you need to explain what evidence and policy allowed an AI-assisted decision in a regulated network.

    1. 4:58 Hendrickson says fixing the input, rule version, and evidence snapshot should make it possible to reconstruct the rules, evidence, and citations behind a decision.
    2. 10:33 Nar distinguishes using AI to improve network operations from using network infrastructure to host new AI workloads.
    3. 14:35 Nar walks through classifying intent, retrieving evidence, constraining reasoning with rules, synthesizing a result, and blocking an action if there is no supporting citation.
    4. 20:19 Nar cites Open Policy Agent and Kyverno for policy and describes using Git to keep track of models, rules, and standards.
    5. 24:19 Nar proposes using Kepler to measure power at host, platform, and workload levels so operators can weigh power use against service commitments.

    In-Toto: Experiences a Few Million Attestations Later

    Santiago Torres-Arias, James Carnegie, Sivana Hamer, Aditya Sirish A Yelgundhalli

    Santiago Torres-Arias moderates a panel on how in-toto attestations are used in practice, with James Carnegie describing Datadog's internal deployment, Aditya Sirish A Yelgundhalli speaking as a maintainer and a Bloomberg engineer, and Sivana Hamer sharing her research on attestations found in the wild. The panelists discuss making attestations easy to create, a fragmented storage picture, the coming policy framework, how consumers can trust public attestations, and what agent-written code changes.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Panel

    Security, identity and policyDelivery, GitOps and release safety

    Watch this if: you produce or consume software supply chain attestations and want to hear how two companies and a researcher see in-toto adoption, storage and policy.

    1. 8:08 Carnegie says Datadog made attestations easy to create by binding ephemeral keys to OIDC, and that it now collects source attestations for every commit and over 2 million attestations a month.
    2. 9:21 He says Datadog began evaluating policy at deployment time, first in monitoring mode and then enforcing, and now also checks policy before pull requests merge, in CI and at runtime.
    3. 14:46 Hamer says her research found 42 million attestations in the wild as of June across almost 150,000 components, often produced by tooling such as npm provenance or PyPI trusted publishing without users knowing.
    4. 17:00 Yelgundhalli explains that the in-toto policy framework sets requirements such as artifact rules without mandating one policy engine or language, and that verification itself emits an attestation.
    5. 21:33 Carnegie says consumers lack a standard way to learn which attestations a producer should publish and with which keys; Datadog distributes keys and policies internally with TUF, which he rarely sees in open source.

    Kubernetes Solutions for Agent-Shaped Problems

    Tim Hockin, Dmitry Berkovich

    Tim Hockin and Dmitry Berkovich of Google say agent workloads are bursty, run untrusted code and have a human waiting, which makes one sandboxed pod per agent session wasteful and slow on Kubernetes. They present Agent Substrate, an open source system built on Kubernetes that suspends idle agents to snapshots and resumes them on request, and spend the second half answering audience questions about policy, autoscaling, memory and where it fits.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automationKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you run or plan to run many agent sessions on your own Kubernetes clusters and want the reasoning behind Agent Substrate from its authors, plus a long Q&A on its current limits.

    1. 0:47 Hockin and Berkovich name three traits of agent workloads: they are bursty and mostly idle, they run untrusted code that must be sandboxed, and a human is usually waiting for the response.
    2. 6:57 Hockin states the three design principles of Agent Substrate (security by default, near-zero cost for idle agents, fast wake-up) and what he calls aspirational goals: billions of agents per cluster, 10,000 activations per second and wake-up in about 100 milliseconds.
    3. 9:16 Berkovich defines the five terms: an actor runs one agent session, an actor template configures it, a worker is the pod that hosts actors, a node-level manager handles images and snapshots, and a network layer routes requests in and applies policy to traffic out.
    4. 13:06 He explains resume speed through layers: volumes always survive, while root filesystem changes and the memory snapshot are reused only if the OCI image is unchanged, otherwise the actor cold starts from its volumes.
    5. 15:26 Hockin describes a use case they see often: a managed agent service runs the agentic loop while the work it triggers runs in Agent Substrate inside the customer's own cluster, where it can be audited and shut off.

    Rego Without Borders: Breaking Down Language Barriers for Security Policy

    Philip Conrad

    Philip Conrad, an Open Policy Agent maintainer, describes how the project wants policy decisions to run inside applications instead of in a sidecar, which he says has so far been easy only in Go. He explains how the Rego intermediate representation lets other languages run policies natively, and names Swift OPA and the Java OPA SDK as two new projects that do it.

    5 min slot · Level not listed · Lightning Talk

    Security, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you want to run Rego policies inside a Swift or Java application without calling a separate Open Policy Agent over the network.

    1. 0:41 Philip Conrad describes Open Policy Agent as a general purpose policy engine, a CNCF graduated project since 2021, that takes structured input such as JSON or YAML and returns decisions.
    2. 1:36 He says the community increasingly wants the policy engine embedded in the application itself instead of running as a sidecar.
    3. 2:22 He says Go makes it hard to package a library for other languages, so those languages have been limited to REST API calls to an Open Policy Agent running elsewhere.
    4. 2:58 He explains the Rego intermediate representation from the project's Wasm pipeline: query plans that break rules into smaller imperative instructions, built with opa build and a plan target.
    5. 3:46 He names Swift OPA and the Java OPA SDK as two new projects this year that run these plans natively, and says the main gain is debugging, with data-heavy policies sometimes running faster than in OPA.

    “Allowed but Unsupported”: When Platform Support Boundaries Meet Enterprise Reality

    Niranjan Shankar

    Niranjan Shankar describes how the Azure Kubernetes Service team opened limited escape hatches for enterprise customers and what happened when support ownership was unclear. He uses packet loss at the boundary between a self-managed network plugin and a managed service mesh to show why teams need to name who owns the risk.

    25 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experienceSecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: You need to give advanced platform users more control without leaving production support ownership ambiguous.

    1. 4:38 Shankar introduces the 'Allowed but Unsupported' tier and uses Istio to show how a team can allow a few EnvoyFilter uses, support routine MeshConfig fields, and block higher-risk settings.
    2. 11:39 He uses packet loss with a self-managed CNI and a provider-managed service mesh to explain why the boundary between supported and unsupported parts is hard to diagnose during an incident.
    3. 13:12 He recalls customers having trouble migrating from self-managed Prometheus to the managed offering because it did not support a rule resource they used.
    4. 20:35 He recommends giving unsupported features a signpost: a way to get best-effort help, an internal owner for production risk, and a path back to a supported option.
    5. 23:08 He closes with criteria for deciding what to expose, including whether teams can isolate the security risk, find an owner, or offer a narrower subset instead of the entire API.
