    AI Found a 0-Day in Metal3. What Happens Next?

    Tuomo Tanskanen

    Tuomo Tanskanen, who leads the Metal3 security committee, follows a vulnerability report from arrival through triage, fix, release and downstream upgrade, drawing on reports from his own AI discovery pipeline and showing how its findings broke down after triage. He also describes how fixes in OpenStack Ironic reach Metal3 and vendor distributions, and advises users to run supported releases, know their configuration and ask vendors how quickly they take in fixes.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Security, identity and policyDelivery, GitOps and release safetyAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you maintain a project that receives AI-generated vulnerability reports, or you run Metal3 and want to know how its security fixes reach you.

    1. 4:12 Tuomo Tanskanen says every vulnerability disclosure Metal3 received this year came from an AI discovery pipeline he built himself, which also scans OpenStack Ironic, and that its findings go through each project's normal security process.
    2. 8:27 He gives the pipeline's numbers: of about 220 findings, 80 were false or duplicates, 31 became hardening issues, 22 were plain bugs and 22 were confirmed, and he concludes that discovery is fast while triage, fixing and coordinated release are slow.
    3. 10:34 He says Metal3 fixes low and medium issues in the open, because GitHub private forks give almost no CI support and force manual testing, and reserves embargoed handling for high and critical ones.
    4. 14:41 He describes a lesson from combining four related cross-namespace issues into one advisory: no CVE could be issued for it, because a CVE covers a single issue.
    5. 20:20 Using two fixes as examples, he says each step down the dependency chain into vendor distributions took roughly a month, so fixes take a long time to reach production.

    Beyond Provisioning: Multi-Tenancy, Rack Awareness, and Declarative Networking in Metal3

    Kashif Khan, Sunnatillo Samadov

    Kashif Khan and Sunnatillo Samadov, both Metal3 maintainers, present three additions to the bare metal provisioning project: multi-tenancy through host claims, failure domains that spread control plane nodes across racks, and top-of-rack switch port configuration. They show a short recorded demo of each and close with which release and feature gate each one sits behind.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Kubernetes internals and runtimesNetworking and connectivityPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you run Kubernetes on bare metal and want several teams to share one hardware pool, rack-aware control planes, or switch ports configured from YAML.

    1. 5:40 Kashif Khan names three gaps he says Metal3 had: teams sharing hardware meant handing out BMC passwords, nothing kept control planes off a single rack, and switch port VLANs were typed by hand.
    2. 6:46 Kashif Khan explains the multi-tenancy design: tenants create a HostClaim in their own namespace, and an admin-owned HostDeployPolicy in the pool namespace decides which namespaces may consume which hosts.
    3. 12:24 Kashif Khan explains failure domains: label each BareMetalHost with its rack, list the failure domains on the Metal3 cluster, and Cluster API then spreads control plane nodes across them.
    4. 17:43 Sunnatillo Samadov introduces the BareMetalSwitch and HostNetworkAttachment resources, which he says let Ironic set switch port mode and VLAN tags during provisioning and clean them up afterwards.
    5. 23:19 The speakers give the status: multi-tenancy is alpha behind a feature gate, failure domains are available in the Cluster API provider release they name, and switch configuration is behind a feature gate in the Bare Metal Operator.

    From Zero to GPU Cloud: 12 months building Mistral Compute on top of Open Source

    Léonard Suslian

    Léonard Suslian of Mistral AI describes how a team of about ten built Mistral Compute, a bare-metal Kubernetes service he says now manages 5,000 hosts, on Cluster API, Metal3, Kamaji and Sveltos plus its own operators. He then goes through what he says broke along the way, from Ironic and board management controllers to SNAT exhaustion, etcd storage and a DHCP storm.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Accelerators and workload schedulingPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you are building or running bare-metal GPU clusters and want a candid list of what breaks between the racks arriving and tenants getting Kubernetes.

    1. 9:07 Suslian explains running each tenant's control plane as pods in the management cluster with Kamaji, which he says saves hardware and turns a control plane upgrade into a deployment rollout.
    2. 12:53 He describes using Sveltos from the management cluster to install add-ons chosen by the tenant, with drift detection that he says restores a deleted Cilium DaemonSet.
    3. 18:47 He says the team replaced Ironic with its own API-compatible rewrite in Go, scoped to their hardware, and went from first commit to production in 19 days.
    4. 21:03 He says hosts that cannot be fixed automatically get an AI agent in a pod with temporary credentials and scoped network access, and reports more than 20 such runs a day.
    5. 25:02 He recounts waking up to almost 900 nodes down because 10-minute DHCP leases caused a renewal storm the server could not keep up with, fixed by longer leases.
