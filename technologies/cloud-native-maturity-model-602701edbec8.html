  • AI Inference + Agentic

    Your Peers Are Struggling With LLM Inference on Kubernetes Too. Here's What the Data Says.

    Danielle Cook, Andy Suderman

    Danielle Cook of Akamai and Andy Suderman of Fairwinds walk through an Akamai-commissioned survey of 200 practitioners running AI in production, along with CNCF, Forrester and Cast AI figures, to show where teams say inference on Kubernetes is hard. They group the problems into scheduling, scaling, routing, placement and ownership, and close with five steps they recommend, from starting on an AI-conformant cluster to setting a time-to-first-token SLO.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and servingObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you run LLM inference on Kubernetes and want survey numbers to compare your latency targets, GPU utilization and routing setup against.

    1. 4:33 Cook presents the survey's performance expectations: 82% of respondents need an end-to-end response within 500 milliseconds, 64% within 250, and nearly half expect to roll back within 15 minutes when inference degrades.
    2. 8:19 Suderman cites a Cast AI figure of 5% average GPU utilization across Kubernetes clusters and suggests Dynamic Resource Allocation, Kueue and right-sized requests as practical responses.
    3. 10:48 Suderman argues against autoscaling inference on CPU and recommends scaling on latency signals such as time to first token, using the metrics vLLM exposes with KEDA.
    4. 12:01 Cook explains why round-robin load balancing fits LLM replicas poorly, since each has its own KV cache, and relays an example where an inference-aware gateway doubled prefix cache hits from 35% to 70%.
    5. 22:15 The speakers give their five steps: start from an AI-conformant cluster, cut model startup time, autoscale on inference signals, put an inference-aware gateway in front, and set a p95 time-to-first-token SLO.
