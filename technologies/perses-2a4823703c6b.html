    Exploring Telemetry Quality

    Jacob Aronoff

    Jacob Aronoff runs an interactive session on why telemetry quality matters, using audience polls and an incident from his own experience where bad telemetry overwhelmed the collectors. He then covers OpenTelemetry semantic conventions and Weaver, and introduces the policies work he and other OpenTelemetry maintainers are specifying to enforce quality rules inside the pipeline.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Birds of a Feather

    Observability and reliability

    Watch this if: you own an observability pipeline and struggle with inconsistent names, PII in logs or runaway cardinality, and want to know what OpenTelemetry is building to enforce quality rules.

    1. 3:23 Aronoff recounts an incident in which retries and stack traces overwhelmed OpenTelemetry collectors that had no persistent queuing, telemetry was dropped, and three emitters using three different names for pods made joins across the data nearly impossible.
    2. 11:25 He describes Weaver as a tool that lets an organization define its own semantic conventions with a schema, check them with custom Rego, generate types and docs, and live-check telemetry locally.
    3. 18:11 He introduces OpenTelemetry policies as matching rules plus actions that he says are meant to be portable across collectors and other pipelines, pushed dynamically without restarts, fail-open and testable locally.
    4. 24:58 He shows benchmarks in which, by his account, the Collector with OTTL and Vector with VRL stall after about one to two thousand rules, and says the policy implementation compiles rules into a Hyperscan regex database to avoid that.
    5. 26:17 On adoption, he says the OTEP has been merged, the protobufs and specification are in progress, sample libraries exist in Go, Rust and Zig, and Collector support is the nearer-term goal with SDKs hoped for next year.

    Sample Smarter, Not Harder (Or Not At All!)

    Reese Lee, Juliano Costa

    Reese Lee and Juliano Costa say sampling at the backend doesn't remove the overhead of generating telemetry in the application. Using the OpenTelemetry Demo, they compare disabling redundant instrumentation with probabilistic, policy-based tail, and adaptive sampling.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Observability and reliability

    Watch this if: You want to cut tracing overhead and keep visibility into slow, failing, or low-traffic requests.

    1. 8:58 The speakers show how they disabled overlapping HTTP instrumentation in the Node.js frontend so it did not generate spans they did not need.
    2. 11:49 The speakers set the collector to sample ten percent probabilistically and compare spans received by the collector with spans sent to Jaeger.
    3. 15:43 The speakers explain that tail sampling sends complete traces to the collector so it can apply policies before forwarding or dropping them.
    4. 16:49 The speakers show tail-sampling rules that keep errors and requests above a latency threshold rather than relying on a fixed fraction for all traces.
    5. 21:41 The speakers describe how adaptive sampling responds to changes in load so a rare checkout flow is more likely to remain visible during a traffic spike.

    Sandboxing on Kubernetes with WebAssembly: A Hands-on Tutorial

    Eric Gregory, Bailey Hayes

    Eric Gregory and Bailey Hayes use wasmCloud and Kubernetes to show how WebAssembly components get explicit permissions for the resources they need. In the workshop, they start with an agent that has no tools and add capabilities one at a time, then show a separate scaling benchmark.

    80 min slot · Beginner · Tutorial

    Kubernetes internals and runtimesSecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: You want to see a WebAssembly sandbox on Kubernetes and how to grant an agent tool only the access it needs.

    1. 6:18 The speakers explain the deny-by-default model and show where the wasmCloud operator schedules a workload onto a host pod.
    2. 23:14 The speakers introduce the workshop's scripted model and explain why it makes the effect of sandbox permissions easier to distinguish from model behavior.
    3. 37:40 The speakers build an optional Rust component and inspect the WebAssembly interfaces it imports for HTTP and filesystem access.
    4. 56:36 The speakers demonstrate a narrow GitHub integration for triaging pull requests and explain how the host plugin keeps the sign-in token out of the workload.
    5. 1:03:56 The speakers switch from the single-instance workshop to a recorded density test and explain the resource constraints they encountered while scaling on Kubernetes.

    Securing Rural Colorado with Open Source: Running a Student-Led SOC on Kubernetes

    Maciej Wal

    Maciej Wal describes how the Cybersecurity Center at Metropolitan State University of Denver replaced a vendor's security monitoring service, first with four virtual machines and then with a Kubernetes platform that he says he runs as the one full-time staff member. He covers the outages and adoption problems along the way, and how students learn log analysis and GitOps on the platform that monitors public sector partners.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Security, identity and policyObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you are a very small team weighing Kubernetes for a security monitoring service, or want ideas for teaching students with real infrastructure.

    1. 3:19 The speaker says that after the vendor ended the service he built a replacement in about 66 days on four virtual machines, with WireGuard, single-node OpenSearch and a new ticketing tool.
    2. 6:45 The speaker explains picking the Kubernetes stack from the CNCF landscape, including Keycloak, Argo CD, OpenTofu and Fluent Bit, and says it cost around $1,000 a month.
    3. 8:33 The speaker recounts a flood of Suricata logs taking down the OpenSearch cluster, which he says had too little memory and no autoscaling, and learning of breakage days late.
    4. 12:01 The speaker says one of the hardest problems he found was the gap between offering self-service and people using it, after students did not adopt browser certificate access.
    5. 13:48 The speaker describes a student workshop on Argo CD, Envoy Gateway, cert-manager and GitHub Actions that ended with deploying a Minecraft server through GitOps.
