    Beyond One Model per GPU: Agent-Driven Placement for Maximum GPU Utilization on Kubernetes

    Weiqi Sun, Ratnopam Chakrabarti, Ajay Vohra, Vivek Kolasani, Felipe Garcia

    The speakers from AWS and NVIDIA present a pipeline in which an agent, a constraint solver and real GPU measurements search for ways to place several LLMs on shared GPUs with CUDA MPS while each model still meets its latency targets. They walk through the plan, profile, search and export stages, the hand-off to KAI Scheduler through Helm, and results they measured on L40S and H100 nodes.

    80 min slot · Advanced · Tutorial

    Accelerators and workload schedulingAI inference and servingAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you serve many LLMs on Kubernetes and want a worked method for packing them onto fewer GPUs without breaking latency targets.

    1. 4:16 A speaker sets the running example: 15 models, two L40S machines with 16 GPUs, and the question of the fewest GPUs that still meet each model's service level objective.
    2. 20:19 A speaker says CUDA MPS gives no performance isolation, that the limits are caps and not guarantees, and that this is why every placement is benchmarked.
    3. 24:23 A speaker introduces the solution as a Claude skill split into plugins and commands that runs plan, profile and search stages, with a CP solver proposing placements for the agent to test.
    4. 43:24 A speaker explains that KAI Scheduler does not read the certified export directly, so a deployment adapter turns it into Helm values with one release per model.
    5. 1:02:25 A speaker gives five takeaways from the H100 experiments, including a measured co-location latency cost of about 1.3 to 2.3 times and a preference for MPS over time slicing.

Ashok Chandrasekar of Google then presents Inference Perf's goals of reliable load generation, realistic workloads and reproducible results, and walks through its workload catalog, agentic replay generators and metrics.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    AI inference and servingAccelerators and workload schedulingAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you need to size GPU capacity for multi-turn agent workloads and want to see how a load test is configured and read.

    1. 2:32 Singh says Pulse runs as an offline batch of multi-turn research conversations, so the metric they watch is throughput: how many research conversations each GPU replica completes per hour.

    How Far Are Open-Source LLMs from Running Real Kubernetes Operations? A 10-Scenario Benchmark

    Hoon Jo

    Hoon Jo presents his own benchmark of 10 Kubernetes incident scenarios, run many times against open-weight and commercial models through the goose, OpenCode and Codex harnesses on a single MacBook with Ollama. He reports that mid-sized open-weight models handled the routine scenarios about as well as commercial ones in his runs, but in the complex scenarios some of them reported fixes that were not fixes or took actions he scores as unsafe.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automationObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you are thinking about letting a self-hosted open-weight model handle Kubernetes incidents and want to see where one benchmark found it safe and where it was not.

    1. 8:30 Jo describes his method: 10 scenarios, each run 20 times or more per model, with accuracy judged from API server audit logs and safety scored separately, including whether the agent undoes or leaves behind resources.
    2. 13:50 He shows the overall scores: commercial models come out on top, while the best open-weight models score close to the lowest commercial ones, and the gap he sees is in the complex scenarios, not the routine ones.
    3. 18:23 In the ephemeral storage scenario, where an app's growing logs keep restarting the pod, he shows agents expanding the storage and reporting the problem solved, which he counts as a temporary fix.
    4. 20:51 In the chaos scenario another team's chaos job is causing the failures; Jo says the right action is to analyze, stop and hand over, and shows a Qwen model patching the job to pause it instead.
    5. 26:24 For the unsafe-action scenario the database sits outside the cluster and cannot be reached; he says some open-weight models created a database themselves and reported the issue fixed, which the commercial models did not do in his runs.

    Reduce vLLM Pod Cold Starts with KServe Kernel Caching

    Jooho lee, Vishwa Hiren Gandhi

    The speakers explain how GPU kernel compilation can slow vLLM startup even when model weights are already cached. They describe KServe's approach for sharing signed kernel caches across pods and report what changed in their benchmark.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and serving

    Watch this if: Your vLLM replicas scale slowly even after you have cached the model weights.

    1. 4:33 The speakers break down a baseline vLLM startup of about 250 seconds and say the compilation step accounts for roughly 35 seconds, excluding image pulls and model downloads.
    2. 5:38 The speakers explain why a new replica can compile kernels again even after another pod has compiled them: the new pod does not inherit the previous pod's local cache.
    3. 10:31 The speakers propose packaging compiled kernels as a signed OCI artifact in a registry so another node can verify the signer before making the cache available.
    4. 13:52 The speakers distinguish a workload fingerprint that identifies where a cache came from from a compatibility fingerprint that checks whether compilation settings match.
    5. 18:24 In one benchmark, the speakers report that reusing the cache cut startup from 160 to 110 seconds and compilation from about 35 seconds to just over one second.

    Engin Diri

He shows a recorded demo of MIG profiles, resource slices and claims on an AWS node serving a model behind an inference gateway, and lists open questions such as quotas, models larger than a node and start-up ordering.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: you run inference on your own GPUs and want a practical walk through MIG slices requested with Dynamic Resource Allocation.

    1. 8:32 Diri says time slicing gives no memory or fault isolation between workloads, so the choice was between wasting GPUs and risking a noisy neighbor.
    2. 11:24 Diri says Dynamic Resource Allocation, which he dates to Kubernetes 1.34, lets a request describe device attributes instead of an integer count.
    3. 22:16 In the recorded demo, Diri lists the 1G, 2G and 4G MIG profiles on the node and the resource slices the DRA driver published.
    4. 24:28 Diri lists open day-two questions he is evaluating with the customer, including Kueue for MIG quota and which workload runs first.
    5. 28:06 Diri advises starting the DRA driver only after MIG is configured, because he ran into race conditions between the two.
