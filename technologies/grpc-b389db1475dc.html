    AI Gateways in Practice: Model Routing, Inference Scheduling, and MCP on Kubernetes

    Yuke Ning

    Yuke Ning walks through how Higress handles hosted model routing, traffic to self-hosted inference, and requests from agents to tools. He discusses where fallback stops working, how an endpoint picker participates in routing, and how the gateway bridges MCP transports.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    AI inference and servingNetworking and connectivity

    Watch this if: you run a gateway that must handle hosted models, your own inference workloads, and MCP tools.

    1. 5:11 Ning shows a Higress route configured to try a backup model provider after selected error responses.
    2. 7:47 He points out that status-based fallback cannot redirect a response once a successful stream has already started.
    3. 9:56 He walks through the Gateway API Inference Extension ownership model, in which application teams own routes and serving teams own the inference pools those routes target.
    4. 18:10 He contrasts rejecting the request if an external endpoint picker fails with allowing the gateway to choose an endpoint another way.
    5. 22:26 He describes three MCP roles for the gateway: exposing REST APIs as tools, proxying existing MCP servers, and bridging differences between clients and servers.

    From Bearer Tokens to Mesh-Native mTLS: Modernizing Service Identity at Coinbase

    Zachary Schwartz, Yair Temkin

    Yair Temkin and Zachary Schwartz describe how Coinbase replaced a homegrown service-to-service auth system with SPIFFE identities issued by SPIRE and checked by an auth sidecar, and say every service is now off the legacy system. They share lessons from the migration and then outline plans to use Istio ambient mesh, with JWT validation at the ingress gateway and ztunnel mTLS, so the sidecars can eventually be removed.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Security, identity and policyNetworking and connectivity

    Watch this if: you are planning a move from token-based service auth to SPIFFE identities or Istio ambient mesh and want a peer's account of the migration.

    1. 1:23 Temkin says the legacy service auth system was tightly coupled to AWS, tied to one region and JWT-only, and was retired in March 2025 when SPIRE became the paved road.
    2. 2:26 Schwartz lists why they chose SPIRE, including compatibility with services outside Kubernetes, JWT SVIDs and separate trust domains per region and environment, and reports 70% less CPU in the auth path and about 50 milliseconds less latency than the legacy system.
    3. 6:46 Schwartz says managing the migration centrally through the sidecar and a control plane, without pull requests in every service team's repository, let them move much faster, and that all services now use the SPIRE-based system.
    4. 10:42 Among the lessons, he says init containers could start before the auth sidecar and have no identity, which they solved with Kubernetes native sidecars, and that bursts of new pods overwhelmed the SPIRE agent until caching was tuned.
    5. 13:37 Temkin describes the ambient mesh plan for ingress: Envoy filters at the ingress gateway validate JWTs centrally and set a subject header, and authorization policies at the gateway and at ztunnel restrict who can call each service.

    From Bytes to Traces: A Deep Dive into OpenTelemetry eBPF Instrumentation Internals

    Rafael Roquetto, Mike Dame

    Mike Dame walks through the user-space side of OpenTelemetry eBPF Instrumentation (OBI): how it discovers processes, enriches them with Kubernetes and container metadata, matches them against configured selectors and attaches eBPF programs. Rafael Roquetto then follows one HTTP request from bytes on the TCP receive path through eBPF maps and a ring buffer into a request span that is enriched and exported as OpenTelemetry traces and metrics.

    30 min slot · Advanced · Session

    Observability and reliabilityKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you want to debug, extend or contribute to OpenTelemetry eBPF Instrumentation and need to know how it works internally.

    1. 1:50 Dame says a stated intent such as observing the checkout service has to be resolved to PIDs, and that everything in OBI works by PIDs.
    2. 7:39 Dame explains that OBI attaches a syscall probe to the bind function as a lazy signal that ports changed, instead of constantly scanning every process's ports.
    3. 14:52 Roquetto describes OBI hooking the TCP receive path and reading bytes into a small buffer along with the PID and connection information.
    4. 18:54 Roquetto says the completed HTTP info struct is shipped to user space through an eBPF ring buffer, where another enrichment pipeline starts.
    5. 22:41 Roquetto says the same request span structure feeds OpenTelemetry spans, span metrics, service graph metrics and RED metrics.

    Kubernetes Data Protection WG Intro & Deep Dive

    Xing Yang, Dave Smith-Uchida

    Xing Yang and Dave Smith-Uchida explain why the Kubernetes Data Protection Working Group formed and walk through the backup and restore building blocks it drives, including COSI, snapshot topology, changed block tracking and volume group snapshots. Smith-Uchida then presents two white papers in progress, on data protection best practices and on recovering from ransomware attacks.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Data, storage and stateKubernetes internals and runtimes

    Watch this if: you back up stateful workloads on Kubernetes or are planning how to recover from a ransomware attack.

    1. 0:53 Yang says GitOps can recreate manifests, but secrets and data in persistent volumes are not in Git, which is why the group builds Kubernetes-native data protection blocks.
    2. 5:47 Yang highlights COSI v1alpha2 support for one bucket access covering multiple buckets with read-write, write-only and read-only modes.
    3. 8:40 Yang explains changed block tracking, which lets backup vendors copy only the blocks that changed between two snapshots through a new CSI snapshot metadata service.
    4. 14:08 Smith-Uchida describes ransomware as an adversarial problem where the control plane and backups may be compromised and must be verified before restoring.
    5. 15:52 Smith-Uchida recommends immutable backups, such as S3 compliance mode, and moving backup tools from static API keys to short-lived tokens obtained via OIDC.

    Lessons Learned Sandboxing Agents with kagent

    Peter Jausovec, Nina Polshakova, Eitan Yarmush

    Peter Jausovec and Eitan Yarmush of Solo.io, the two speakers heard in the recording, walk through the sandboxing approaches they tried for kagent and say each one still kept an idle agent's pod running or tied identity to the pod. They explain why kagent 1.0 was rebuilt on Agent Substrate, list five lessons from that work, and demo Claude Managed Agents sessions running in sandboxes on their own cluster.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Security, identity and policyAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you are choosing how to sandbox AI agents on Kubernetes and want to hear what one team found lacking in pod-per-agent setups.

    1. 3:40 Jausovec says they tried four things before Agent Substrate: their own pod-per-agent runtime, Anthropic's Sandbox Runtime, Agent Sandbox and NVIDIA's OpenShell, and that each solved only part of the problem.
    2. 9:08 Jausovec gives the reasons for Agent Substrate: idle agents are snapshotted to object storage and hold no compute, the Kubernetes API is off the hot path, restores take milliseconds, and actors are sandboxed with default-deny networking.
    3. 10:04 Yarmush tells how Solo started building an internal sandbox layer called Moat, then joined Google's Agent Substrate work instead, and says kagent 1.0 is now based entirely on Agent Substrate.
    4. 17:42 Yarmush starts the takeaways: isolation is table stakes and pause and resume is the hard part, every control must sit outside the agent, identity follows the actor, a suspended agent is stored data, and the sandbox runtime becomes a data format.
    5. 22:56 Demo: Jausovec creates two Claude Managed Agents sessions whose sandboxes run on his cluster, shows that one session cannot read the other's file, and shows a blocked request succeed after he applies an egress policy.

    Linkerd, The Service Mesh for Everyone: gRPC Load Balancing & More

    Joe Fuller

    Joe Fuller of Buoyant explains why gRPC over a single HTTP/2 connection defeats connection-level load balancing on Kubernetes and presents a benchmark he says Buoyant ran comparing Linkerd with Istio and Cilium. He then covers what Linkerd 2.20 adds, mainly rate-limit-aware load balancing and a new circuit breaking mode, both alpha and opt-in, and what has shipped in edge releases for 2.21.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Networking and connectivity

    Watch this if: you run gRPC services on Kubernetes and want to understand per-request load balancing, or you are deciding whether to turn on the new Linkerd 2.20 overload features.

    1. 4:21 Joe Fuller explains the gRPC problem: a client opens one HTTP/2 connection, every request lands on one pod, and scaling up does not help because new pods get none of the existing connections.
    2. 6:58 He describes a benchmark he says Buoyant's engineers ran on EKS against Istio in ambient and sidecar mode and Cilium in L7 mode, and reports Linkerd at about 92% of no-mesh throughput.
    3. 10:51 He describes the failure mode 2.20 targets: an overloaded pod that rejects requests quickly with 429 or RESOURCE_EXHAUSTED looks like the fastest endpoint to a latency-based balancer and gets more traffic.
    4. 12:11 He explains rate-limit-aware load balancing: the client proxy records a penalty of five seconds by default, or the server's back-off hint, instead of the fast rejection, so traffic shifts away and then returns as the penalty decays.
    5. 20:06 He says the destination controller no longer keeps a per-subscriber endpoint snapshot, which he says cut control plane memory by about 84% in load tests, and that native sidecars are GA and on by default.

    No Network? No Problem: How We Designed for Kubernetes Nodes Running in Air-Gapped VPCs

    Shachar Tal

    Shachar Tal describes a security scanning product that runs in customer-owned cloud accounts with no ingress or egress, and how his team replaced Konnectivity's gRPC tunnel with a cloud storage bucket so Kubernetes nodes there can talk to a remote control plane. He demos kubectl exec and logs running over bucket objects and says adaptive polling keeps the cost at about $5 per VM instance per month.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Networking and connectivitySecurity, identity and policy

    Watch this if: you need Kubernetes nodes in networks with zero egress and want to see a control plane tunnel built on object storage.

    1. 3:58 Tal says the outpost VPC is designed so that an attacker who gets code running inside cannot exfiltrate data, with one tightly controlled bucket as the only path.
    2. 11:17 Tal says Konnectivity solves only one direction of reachability, so his team carries messages in both directions through the bucket as a rendezvous point.
    3. 18:48 In the demo, Tal runs kubectl exec into a pod and says every keystroke is a file uploaded to the bucket and pulled down by the other side.
    4. 21:36 Tal says moving to Kubernetes with CSI plugins let the team throw out 80% of its application code for volumes, snapshots and mounting.
    5. 23:05 Tal explains that polling speeds up to 500 milliseconds while a session is active and backs off to 10 seconds, at a cost he puts at about $5 per VM instance per month.

    The Next Generation of Kyverno — Unified Policy as Code at Scale

    Jim Bugwadia, Justin Munoz

    Jim Bugwadia explains why Kyverno moved from its own policy language to CEL-based policy types, how to migrate before the legacy types are removed in 1.20, and what the project plans next. Justin Munoz describes how his platform team at Kinexys by J.P. Morgan adopted Kyverno for namespace baselines, certificate rotation, permission tightening and change-window checks on Argo CD syncs, and shares the lessons the team took from it.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Session

    Security, identity and policyAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you run Kyverno and need to plan the move to CEL-based policies, or want to see how a regulated platform team uses it beyond admission control.

    1. 3:36 Jim Bugwadia says Kyverno chose CEL because it is the native policy language in Kubernetes, compiled and fast, and that Kyverno adds reporting, exceptions and policies for existing resources on top of the built-in admission policy types.
    2. 9:52 Justin Munoz describes using Kyverno to watch for newly issued certificates and trigger rolling restarts of deployments that cannot reload them on their own.
    3. 11:57 He describes a policy that intercepts Argo CD sync events and allows them only when a custom resource shows an approved change management window is open, with a precise denial reason otherwise.
    4. 13:34 He shares lessons learned: start in audit mode, treat the denial message as the user interface, make the paved path compliant by default and ship policy through the normal software lifecycle.
    5. 16:09 Bugwadia says release 1.20 removes the older policy types but leaves their CRDs in place so old policies show as not ready, and that 1.21 will remove the CRDs as well.
