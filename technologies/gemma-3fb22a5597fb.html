    How Far Are Open-Source LLMs from Running Real Kubernetes Operations? A 10-Scenario Benchmark

    Hoon Jo

    Hoon Jo presents his own benchmark of 10 Kubernetes incident scenarios, run many times against open-weight and commercial models through the goose, OpenCode and Codex harnesses on a single MacBook with Ollama. He reports that mid-sized open-weight models handled the routine scenarios about as well as commercial ones in his runs, but in the complex scenarios some of them reported fixes that were not fixes or took actions he scores as unsafe.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automationObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you are thinking about letting a self-hosted open-weight model handle Kubernetes incidents and want to see where one benchmark found it safe and where it was not.

    1. 8:30 Jo describes his method: 10 scenarios, each run 20 times or more per model, with accuracy judged from API server audit logs and safety scored separately, including whether the agent undoes or leaves behind resources.
    2. 13:50 He shows the overall scores: commercial models come out on top, while the best open-weight models score close to the lowest commercial ones, and the gap he sees is in the complex scenarios, not the routine ones.
    3. 18:23 In the ephemeral storage scenario, where an app's growing logs keep restarting the pod, he shows agents expanding the storage and reporting the problem solved, which he counts as a temporary fix.
    4. 20:51 In the chaos scenario another team's chaos job is causing the failures; Jo says the right action is to analyze, stop and hand over, and shows a Qwen model patching the job to pause it instead.
    5. 26:24 For the unsafe-action scenario the database sits outside the cluster and cannot be reached; he says some open-weight models created a database themselves and reported the issue fixed, which the commercial models did not do in his runs.

    Pods in the Wild: Reinforcement Learning on Kubernetes with OpenRL

    Sunil Arora, Ravin Kumar

    Ravin Kumar uses DolphinGemma to illustrate what can happen when teams fine-tune an open model on a specialized dataset. Sunil Arora introduces OpenRL on Kubernetes and uses a demo recording to show how trainers and samplers can share a GPU pool.

    8 min slot · Level not listed · Keynote

    AI agents and automationAccelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: you want an example of turning domain data into a fine-tuned model and scheduling reinforcement learning on Kubernetes.

    1. 1:38 A video shown by Kumar introduces DolphinGemma and says the team used decades of dolphin vocalization research to train a model that generates synthetic dolphin sounds.
    2. 2:26 Kumar walks through a fine-tuning workflow that starts with a base model and domain-specific data, then uses supervised fine-tuning or reinforcement learning.
    3. 4:02 Arora introduces OpenRL as a post-training service for Kubernetes and says its Tinker-compatible primitives let researchers design an RL loop separately from the infrastructure.
    4. 7:47 In the recorded demo, Arora shows OpenRL scheduling trainers and samplers onto GPUs and explains how Dynamic Resource Allocation partitions the accelerator pool.
    5. 8:39 Arora says the team is releasing post-training recipes and points to a legal-task example using Agent Substrate for RL environments and llm-d for sampling and judging.

    The Local AI Renaissance

    Jason Stryker

    Jason Stryker explains why he runs open-weight models on his own hardware and demonstrates a laptop setup that serves a model through KServe and llama.cpp. He compares inference engines and discusses memory and concurrency limits that shape which workloads he runs locally.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and serving

    Watch this if: you want to try serving open-weight models on hardware you already own.

    1. 3:34 Stryker distinguishes local models, which run on your own hardware, from models you reach through a hosted subscription.
    2. 13:40 He identifies memory as a major constraint for local inference and explains why it matters for the model he can run in the demo.
    3. 14:39 He introduces KServe as the layer that runs inference engines on his local Kubernetes cluster.
    4. 17:11 He turns off Wi-Fi and shows the local inference request and service logs continuing without an internet connection.
    5. 23:55 He introduces vLLM as an option when concurrency matters and contrasts its key-value cache approach with llama.cpp.
