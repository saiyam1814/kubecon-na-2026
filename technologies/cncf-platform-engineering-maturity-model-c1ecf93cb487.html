    Is Platform Engineering and the Platform Maturity Model Still Relevant in the Agentic AI Era?

    Chris Plank, Corey McGalliard

    Chris and Corey introduce the CNCF Platform Engineering Maturity Model as a map rather than a pass-or-fail assessment and describe community updates such as plainer language, translations, a self-assessment tool, and worked examples. They argue that agentic AI makes platform maturity more relevant because proofs of concept still need production ownership, guardrails, customer feedback, and redesign for machine-speed scale.

    25 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI agents and automationPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you want a practitioner walkthrough of applying the CNCF Platform Engineering Maturity Model to AI-era platforms.

    1. 5:15 The speakers describe the maturity model's five aspects of investment, adoption, interfaces, operations, and measurement across four levels, framing it as a map rather than a pass-or-fail assessment.
    2. 7:51 Chris uses the investment aspect to argue that many AI rollouts remain provisional projects focused on training and adoption numbers without considering day-two operation or product ownership.
    3. 11:05 The speakers say the updated model includes a community-contributed self-assessment tool whose questions can reveal concrete gaps in customer feedback and platform practice.
    4. 13:20 The speakers argue that AI proofs of concept encounter the maturity question when teams ask how they will run in production, who will own them, and how day-two maintenance will work.
    5. 20:29 The speakers argue that AI multiplies existing good and bad platform behavior, while systems designed for human-scale interaction must be redesigned for agents operating continuously and at much higher concurrency.

    The CNCF Platform Engineering User Group & Platform Factors

    Colin Griffin, Avinash Sabat

    Colin Griffin describes how the CNCF Platform Engineering User Group develops resources and recommends using its maturity model to decide where to invest rather than to maximize every dimension. Avinash Sabat presents draft Platform Capability Factors and uses a self-service database to explain what a capability should deliver and who should own it.

    30 min slot · Level not listed · Birds of a Feather

    Community and open source adoptionPlatform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: you are defining what a self-service platform capability should deliver and who will own it.

    1. 5:20 Griffin says the user group brings contributors together to write resources such as the platforms white paper, the maturity model, and its self-assessment.
    2. 8:30 Griffin says teams should use the maturity model to decide where investment makes sense rather than trying to reach its optimizing column in every area.
    3. 13:27 Sabat distinguishes platform operators who own shared infrastructure and governance, specialist teams that produce capabilities, and consumers who request them through self-service.
    4. 17:13 Sabat says a self-service database should deliver a complete outcome, including storage and backups, with clear responsibilities and no need to contact the database team for every request.
    5. 19:26 Sabat says a capability should reconcile repeated requests safely without drifting, and that both producers and consumers should be able to see how it is performing.

    What Customer Success Knows That Platform Engineering Keeps Relearning

    Ryan Etten

    Ryan Etten of Broadcom maps customer success practices onto platform engineering, following a composite team through onboarding, adoption, expansion and renewal. He argues that dashboards and mandates hide quiet churn, and offers plays such as pairing with stalled teams and comparing each team with its own baseline.

    25 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Platform engineering and developer experience

    Watch this if: platform adoption is your problem and you want customer success plays you can apply to internal developer teams.

    1. 2:03 Etten maps customer success terms onto platforms: the champion is the tech lead who argued for you, the contract is a mandate, and renewal is the day a team stops deploying through you. He follows a composite payments team through the talk.
    2. 7:52 He says a team counts as onboarded at its first production change through the platform, and suggests that if a team has not shipped by week two, a person sits with them instead of sending automated emails.
    3. 11:30 He suggests comparing each team with its own baseline rather than the platform average, saying averages are where churn hides, and reaching out when a team drops.
    4. 17:15 He says mandates produce hostages, a customer success term he credits to a 1995 Harvard Business Review paper, and points to Backstage at Spotify as a tool that was not mandated internally.
    5. 21:24 He says the CNCF Platform Engineering Maturity Model does not use words like onboarding, retention or churn, and proposes a sixth aspect on retention.
