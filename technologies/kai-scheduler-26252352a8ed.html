    Beyond One Model per GPU: Agent-Driven Placement for Maximum GPU Utilization on Kubernetes

    Weiqi Sun, Ratnopam Chakrabarti, Ajay Vohra, Vivek Kolasani, Felipe Garcia

    The speakers from AWS and NVIDIA present a pipeline in which an agent, a constraint solver and real GPU measurements search for ways to place several LLMs on shared GPUs with CUDA MPS while each model still meets its latency targets. They walk through the plan, profile, search and export stages, the hand-off to KAI Scheduler through Helm, and results they measured on L40S and H100 nodes.

    80 min slot · Advanced · Tutorial

    Accelerators and workload schedulingAI inference and servingAI agents and automation

    Watch this if: you serve many LLMs on Kubernetes and want a worked method for packing them onto fewer GPUs without breaking latency targets.

    1. 4:16 A speaker sets the running example: 15 models, two L40S machines with 16 GPUs, and the question of the fewest GPUs that still meet each model's service level objective.
    2. 20:19 A speaker says CUDA MPS gives no performance isolation, that the limits are caps and not guarantees, and that this is why every placement is benchmarked.
    3. 24:23 A speaker introduces the solution as a Claude skill split into plugins and commands that runs plan, profile and search stages, with a CP solver proposing placements for the agent to test.
    4. 43:24 A speaker explains that KAI Scheduler does not read the certified export directly, so a deployment adapter turns it into Helm values with one release per model.
    5. 1:02:25 A speaker gives five takeaways from the H100 experiments, including a measured co-location latency cost of about 1.3 to 2.3 times and a preference for MPS over time slicing.

    Composing Inference on Kubernetes: Gateway, Schedule, Serve, Observe

    Karena Angell, Alex Chircop

    Karena Angell and Alex Chircop argue that production inference on Kubernetes is too often built as a closed vertical stack, and present gateway, scheduling, serving and observability as separate layers joined by explicit interfaces. They go through six kinds of composability, show recorded lab runs of several traffic shapes and component swaps, and close with questions for teams to ask of their own clusters.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    AI inference and servingNetworking and connectivityObservability and reliability

    Watch this if: you are building a shared inference platform on Kubernetes and want a way to decide which layer owns routing, admission, serving and tracing so that parts can be swapped later.

    1. 3:15 Angell says the biggest trap they see in production is building separate silos for chat, RAG and agents, which duplicates routing, scheduling and telemetry for what she describes as one nested workload.
    2. 14:16 Chircop separates admission from routing: he says a scheduler such as Kueue decides once whether pods can exist under quota and fairness rules, while the endpoint picker chooses which replica serves each prompt, using signals such as queue depth and a warm KV cache.
    3. 17:02 Chircop says the OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions give components a shared vocabulary for spans and metrics, and W3C trace context propagation joins them into a single trace across the hops.
    4. 18:45 Angell cites Hugging Face moving Text Generation Inference into maintenance mode, and says teams that kept serving behind a gateway API contract could swap engines quickly while others had to rework their client apps.
    5. 27:13 Chircop narrates recorded lab runs in which gateways are swapped while routing live traffic, llm-d's endpoint picker is replaced by the reference picker for the Gateway API Inference Extension, and the scheduler is changed between Kueue, Volcano and KAI Scheduler.

    KAI Scheduler: What's New & What's Next in AI Workload Orchestration

    Omri Cohen

    Omri Cohen, a KAI Scheduler maintainer, explains why he thinks shared AI clusters need a dedicated scheduler and describes how KAI divides resources between teams with hierarchical queues. He covers gang scheduling, topology awareness and GPU fractions, then lists roadmap items such as reservations, backfill and cluster defragmentation.

    5 min slot · Level not listed · Lightning Talk

    Accelerators and workload schedulingAI inference and serving

    Watch this if: several teams share GPUs in one Kubernetes cluster and you are evaluating schedulers for AI workloads.

    1. 1:00 Cohen says KAI is a Kubernetes scheduler that workloads opt into by setting the scheduler name in the pod spec, and that it respects the existing Kubernetes APIs.
    2. 1:23 Cohen says KAI supports hierarchical queues that can model an organization, with guaranteed resources, weighted allocation or strict priority, and lets queues borrow idle resources.
    3. 2:09 Cohen says KAI supports hierarchical gangs for workloads such as disaggregated inference, and both strict and preferred network topology constraints.
    4. 2:40 Cohen says KAI supports GPU fractions through the existing HAMi integration and newly released NVIDIA GPU fractions, plus NUMA awareness and bin packing.
    5. 3:05 Cohen lists roadmap items: time limits, reservation and backfill, GPU compute fractions, advanced topology placement, more defragmentation, and assigning nodes to queues.

    One Workload to Rule Them All: Workload Aware Scheduling for Distributed AI Training on Kubernetes

    Andrey Velichkevich

    Andrey Velichkevich explains why scheduling distributed training one pod at a time can deadlock jobs and how the Kubernetes Workload and PodGroup APIs model them as a unit. He shows how Kubeflow Trainer exposes those capabilities through training runtimes and compares three scheduling policies on a local cluster.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: You run distributed training on Kubernetes and want to understand when pod groups and topology constraints matter.

    1. 0:48 Velichkevich illustrates how two training jobs can deadlock when the scheduler gives each job some GPUs but neither gets enough to start all its ranks.
    2. 4:44 Velichkevich distinguishes the Workload template from runtime PodGroups and shows how a PodGroup can express a minimum pod count and topology constraints.
    3. 7:10 Velichkevich introduces composite PodGroups for workloads with a hierarchy, where a parent can require a minimum number of child groups before scheduling proceeds.
    4. 10:12 Velichkevich explains the Kubeflow Trainer split: platform admins define runtime blueprints, and scientists use a Python SDK to submit jobs without managing Kubernetes deployment details.
    5. 15:17 In a local kind demo, Velichkevich sets up a comparison of default pod-by-pod placement, gang scheduling, and rack-constrained placement for a PyTorch job.

    Why Are You Still Wasting Whole GPUs? Hardware-Level Sharing, Scheduled with DRA

    Engin Diri

    Engin Diri of Pulumi recounts helping a European customer build a self-hosted inference platform, moving from whole GPUs and time slicing to NVIDIA MIG slices that pods request through Dynamic Resource Allocation. He shows a recorded demo of MIG profiles, resource slices and claims on an AWS node serving a model behind an inference gateway, and lists open questions such as quotas, models larger than a node and start-up ordering.

    30 min slot · Any Level · Session

    Accelerators and workload scheduling

    Watch this if: you run inference on your own GPUs and want a practical walk through MIG slices requested with Dynamic Resource Allocation.

    1. 8:32 Diri says time slicing gives no memory or fault isolation between workloads, so the choice was between wasting GPUs and risking a noisy neighbor.
    2. 11:24 Diri says Dynamic Resource Allocation, which he dates to Kubernetes 1.34, lets a request describe device attributes instead of an integer count.
    3. 22:16 In the recorded demo, Diri lists the 1G, 2G and 4G MIG profiles on the node and the resource slices the DRA driver published.
    4. 24:28 Diri lists open day-two questions he is evaluating with the customer, including Kueue for MIG quota and which workload runs first.
    5. 28:06 Diri advises starting the DRA driver only after MIG is configured, because he ran into race conditions between the two.
